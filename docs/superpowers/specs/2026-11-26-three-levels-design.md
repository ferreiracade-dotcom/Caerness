# Three levels of simulation, the GM as storyteller, and the link between them (design, 2026-11-26)

Status: for the owner's approval. Nothing has been built.

Background:
- `docs/research/2026-11-26-three-layers-map.md`: what the code does today.
- `docs/research/2026-11-24-outside-engines-study.md`: ideas from outside engines.

## Why
In the two Dominic playtests, most faults came from one confusion. The engine used the dice that suit the city as a
whole on named people close to the protagonist. That produced:
- quarrels with no cause, and marriages out of nowhere;
- a mother offering a paid job;
- news about nobodies;
- relationships that never followed play.

The other faults were GM behaviour:
- moralizing;
- menus;
- invented hooks;
- asking the player what it should have decided.

The existing city layers (population groups, promotion into individuals, tiers) are sound. The level that matters
most for play, the protagonist's people and the people who move the city, never had its own way of working, and the
playtest fixes patched the dice instead.

**Goal:** the city lives on its own; everything near the protagonist happens for a reason; and the GM tells the story
on top of an engine that keeps every fact, sum and relationship in order.

**Anti-goals:**
- caps or ladders;
- the world bending toward the protagonist;
- events that exist only to make a story;
- one level's method forced onto another;
- an engine that writes the story;
- a GM that ignores the engine;
- building a large system before anyone has played it.

## 1. The three levels
| Level | Who | How things happen |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Groups | the population held as groups by kind and place, each group with **its own rates** (the devout parish, dockworkers, the outfit's world, the respectable middle class, ...) | rates belonging to that group; members picked by them |
| 2. Notable | individuals the city has picked out: a businessman who got rich and bought land, a rising captain, a go-between, a regular customer such as Paul Ricci | their role in the city's systems: seats, deals, land, offices, enterprises |
| 3. Close and powerful | (a) the protagonist's closest people: family, household, crew, the woman he courts and her family, true friends; (b) the movers and shakers: outfit bosses, the alderman, big developers, heads of the institutions that shape the city | concerns and ties; nothing from a yearly chance |

**Rates belong to groups, not to the city.** City totals are only what the groups add up to. A devout community
marries younger and has more children. The outfit's world has its own violence. The docks have their own injuries.

**Level-2 and level-3 people are usually exceptional.** Some are there only because they are close to the
protagonist, but most are there because they are not ordinary. They are never pulled toward an average. They are
judged against their own kind: does a captain live like a captain? A promoted person keeps his group, and its culture
still shapes what he wants and how he chooses. Being exceptional means differing from his group, not having none.

**Movers and shakers change the city.** Their plans (take a dock, push a vote, squeeze a rival, back a candidate)
work through the level-1 and level-2 people under them: crews, clients, tenants, voters, customers. A plan sets
level-2 people moving, shifts the rates of the groups it touches, and reaches the protagonist's people as pressure.

## 2. Moving between levels
Everything here happens independently of the protagonist. His presence is only one reason among many.
- **Up from 1 to 2.** A roll that makes someone *matter to the city* promotes him: he comes into money, gets a seat,
  buys land, commits a serious crime, takes an office, or opens or loses a business. Ordinary life events (a
  marriage, a child, a falling-out) stay as group statistics unless they touch someone already at level 2 or 3.
- **Up from 2 to 3.** He becomes truly close to the protagonist, through warmth built in play rather than repeated
  dealings, or he becomes a mover and shaker.
- **Met in a scene.** Anyone the protagonist meets becomes an individual who can be described and met again. Being
  met once does not raise him to level 3.
- **A promoted person's history** is drawn only from what his group and the city level already say about him. It is
  never presented to the GM as something that "has been building".
- **Down.** A person drops a level when he stops mattering, and untended ties fade. His records stay. A level-3
  person's concerns are summed up into his level-2 state when he drops.

## 3. Level 3: concerns, choices, ties
**Concerns.** A concern is one person's live state toward someone or something, with its origin:
- he owes or is owed;
- he wants something (a steady card, his own place, a woman, a site, an office);
- he resents someone, with the reason;
- he fears for someone;
- he courts someone, with the stage reached;
- for movers and shakers, he plans something (an aim, a target, and the people he uses).

Concerns come from four sources:
- scenes and acts in play;
- city changes passed down to him;
- other people's acts;
- chance happenings that *start* something (an illness, a meeting on a night out, a windfall).

Chance can start a concern, but it never produces an outcome with a made-up reason. A short-lived concern ("angry
tonight", "flush this week") carries its own end date.

**Choosing offscreen.** Between scenes, the engine decides what level-3 people do. People choose like people, not
optimally:
- **Habit first.** Most weeks a person does what he usually does. He weighs options only when a concern presses.
- **A narrow view.** He considers the few acts that his concerns and situation bring to mind.
- **Temperament and mood bend the choice.** A hot temper favours the satisfying act, and drink, pride and fear pull
  the same way. A close tie holds a hostile choice back but never forbids it.
- **Others weigh in.** Third parties' concerns add their weight: a mother's fear lowers the chance of a son's risky
  choice.
- **Noise.** Sometimes a person simply chooses badly.

Every choice stores why it was made, and doing nothing is often the choice. When the protagonist must answer, the
matter waits for him and never lapses. He lives under the same rules as everyone else, but the engine never chooses
for him.

**Ties follow play.**
- The end-of-scene record (section 4) moves ties: dinners, help given, quarrels, gifts.
- A tie keeps its heaviest dated deeds with their plain-language causes, so the GM can say "since the night at the
  Tap".
- Untended regard settles at a resting point that fits the pair (kin stays warm), not at zero.
- Kindness repeated within a short window counts for less each time.
- Affinity becomes friendship or enmity by crossing a threshold, and it has to fall back further before the bond
  ends (hysteresis).

**Breaks come with a warning.** A decision to break (leave the crew, end the courtship, move against a rival) is
stored with its reason and acted on later. The GM can see it coming.

**One contest.** When concerns meet head-on (a quarrel, a bargain, a fight, a courtship), one model decides it. It
weighs both sides' relevant states (temper, standing, money, body, skill, numbers, weapons, drink) against the stakes.
Size counts in a fight but does not decide it (the bigger man wins about two in three), and weapons override size. In
a bargain, need counts. The result changes concerns and ties.

**Scenes as social situations.** A night at the bar, a family dinner or a sale is a situation with roles and its own
natural acts. Several can run in one room at once. A night out for young men produces brief meetings with women,
banter, sometimes a brawl, and rarely the start of something.

## 4. The GM and the engine
The engine keeps everything in order: memory, arithmetic, concerns, ties, the city. The GM tells the story. They work
as partners.

**In a scene, the GM plays the people.** It reads their concerns, ties, temperament and body, and plays them from
those. It moves the story on. The engine answers what the GM proposes in one of two ways:
- **A veto**, only for a real contradiction: a dead man, money that isn't there, a fact already settled otherwise.
- **A character note**, which is not a veto ("Aurelio is cautious and hardly knows him; shouting in the street is out
  of character"). The GM weighs it and may keep its choice.

**What the GM creates becomes canon.** Any person, place, firm, suburb or happening it writes, if nothing contradicts
it, is recorded and is as real as anything the engine started with. From then on the engine simulates it. Big
additions also get a plausibility note against the city and its time.

**The GM never rewrites the past.** It never writes "has been building" with nothing on record, and never supplies a
cause for something the engine produced. A missing cause is an engine bug, to be reported. New things start now.

**Every scene is recorded when it ends**, in one compulsory record: who was present, what passed between them, and
every new person, place or fact. The engine refuses to skip time or resume until the last scene is recorded.

**Where big things come from:**
1. the movers and shakers' plans;
2. small things adding up past a threshold, with a warning first;
3. the GM bringing a big thing in directly, if it is plausible.

**The engine shows the GM what's building up.** This is a short list, chosen by how much each item matters to the
protagonist and how close it is to tipping ("Frank: wants a steady card, pressure rising"). The GM may bring these
into scenes or leave them.

**The GM's read has a budget.** Each tool response has a fixed size and order:
1. what demands the protagonist's answer;
2. what's building up;
3. news that reached him;
4. money;
5. texture.

Anything more is available through lookup, never pushed.

**Storyteller principles.** The rulebook becomes a few principles with examples from the playtests, replacing the
growing list of don'ts:
- read what the engine gave before writing;
- decide, don't ask; questions are only for the player's own choices;
- put engine material into the scene, never as announcements or out-of-character flags;
- describe people (bodies, dress, manner) and the protagonist's feelings;
- out-of-character notes only for plausibility or real consequences, in plain words;
- don't moralize; the law is lived as people of the time lived it;
- no policies in the protagonist's voice that the player didn't set;
- let conversations finish, and bring people back when the player adds something;
- no dragging things out, and attention that comes at a plausible pace.

## 5. The link between the levels
- **Down.** City changes become concerns for the specific level-3 people they touch, through the ties and roles that
  connect them. The club notices Carla, so Aurelio fears for her. The docks slow, so Frank wants a steady card more. A
  rival leans on the Hill, so Louis worries about his shop.
- **Up.** Level-3 and level-2 outcomes count back into their groups and roles: a marriage, a new business, a beating,
  a bad debt.
- **Calibration (tests only, level 1 only).** Over several runs, each group's life, with its promoted members counted
  back in, should come out near *that group's* rates. Notable people are never pulled to the average.
- **What reaches the protagonist: one gate.** An item reaches the GM only with a path and a stake.
  - **The path:** who told him or what he saw, through a level-3 person or his own presence. News moves the way
    gossip does: seen, made into a short story with an opinion attached, passed on when people meet, and forgotten
    over time.
  - **The stake:** close kin or friends, his money, his business, or danger to him or his. Salience is how often the
    subject comes up in his own memory and ties.

  Feelings (concerns) and knowledge (news) are separate records with separate fading. City changes with no path to
  him act on his world only through what they change: Gerald Orsini simply comes back to book Stage 2.

- **Kept:**

  - bodies (B1.1 and B1.2, and the appearance-reading fix);
  - `crystallization` and `tiers.py`, with level 3 on the daily clock;

- **Replaced, not added to:**

  - the four news feeds in `connector/timeflow.py` become the one gate, starting from `hearsay.py`'s path logic;
  - the old paths are switched off for level-3 people first, then deleted once the slice is proven.
- **Reworked:** the held B1 fight work becomes the contest; the P2.32 to P2.37 fixes are reviewed, and whatever
  doesn't fit is dropped.
- **Not built separately:** the queued playtest fixes (R1, R2, Aurelio's tie, Gerald's news, Mario Basso's news).
  This design covers them.
- **Group rates:** these need package data per group. Check what the Saint Jude package already holds before adding
  anything.

## 7. How "good" is judged
**A scoring sheet** is drawn from the owner's corrections in both transcripts. Each line is checkable. Some examples:
- nobody marries without a courtship first;
- every engine item is woven into the scene;
- nights out produce meetings;
- no news without a path and a stake;
- no menus;
- the GM decides instead of asking;
- no moralizing;
- every level-3 event has a stored cause;
- ties match the recorded scenes.

**Engine tests:**
- run a fresh save 3 to 6 months forward with only routine acts, and score it against the sheet;
- count stories, not executions: courtships that went somewhere, feuds with causes, plans that changed the city;
- for anything that never fires, record why: nobody wanted it, or something blocked it;
- run the group calibration across several runs;
- time a skip before and after.

**GM test bench:** a few dozen player turns taken from the transcripts, replayed against the GM with the real
connector on a test save, and scored against the sheet. Every rulebook change is measured on it. It costs tokens on
each run, so it runs on rulebook changes and before a playtest, not on every commit. Scoring is a checklist read by
me, with the mechanical lines (menus, questions, out-of-character flags) checked by script.

## 8. Build order: a vertical slice first
1. The scoring sheet and the GM test bench.
2. **The slice:**
   - level-3 concerns and ties for the protagonist's circle;
   - the end-of-scene record;
   - canon from the GM;
   - the news gate;
   - what's building;
   - the read budget;
   - the rewritten storyteller rulebook;
   - the old paths switched off for those people;
   - a **simple offscreen choice**: habit plus acting on the most pressing concern, with the non-optimal rules.
     Without it, the circle would go silent once the old paths are off.
3. **The owner plays a fresh save for an evening.** Fixes follow from that, and only then:
4. the full offscreen choice (others weighing in, warnings before breaks) and the contest;
5. movers' plans working through level 1 and level 2;
6. promotion rules and group rates.

Each step ends in play, not only in passing tests.
