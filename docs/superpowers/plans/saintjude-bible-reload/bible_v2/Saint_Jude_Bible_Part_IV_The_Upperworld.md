## 0. How to Read This Part

### 0.1 What the Upperworld Is

**Fixed.** The upperworld is everything in Saint Jude that is legal on its face and stands between the city and the underworld: the unions, the businesses, the machine, the professions, and the city's own money. Most of its people are not criminals and never meet one. They still live inside the same web of jobs, contracts, credit and permission that the families touch at a dozen points. This Part describes the web as an institution. It does not decide what any man in it will do.

### 0.2 Conventions

Tags follow Parts I to III: **Fixed**, **Open**, **Weather** (dated, with strength), **Anchor**, **Tendency**, **Design**, **Example**. Real facts are cited **[verified]** only if checked against a source in this drafting session. Everything else from general knowledge is **[memory]**, and where the memory is shaky it also says **to verify**. Every [memory] item is collected in section 8.

**Design.** Every dollar figure is a 1975 dollar and an unsourced estimate meant to be changed. Figures are net, never gross, as in Part III 7.1. **Part VI supplies the price book. Where this Part needs a number Part VI does not have, it says so in the entry, and section 8 lists them all.**

**Real people.** Real officials, union leaders and mobsters appear only as history and offstage news, with what they actually did. They never speak or act in a scene. Saint Jude stand-ins do that work.

**Named men.** Any name given here is an **Example**. The engine may change it or drop it. Local numbers other than 402 and 1201 are Examples too.

### 0.3 What This Part Will Not Do

It does not write a price book (Part VI), the outside groups or drug markets (Part V), or the narration rules (the separate Narration Standards). It invents no named secrets, conspiracies or plot hooks.

### 0.4 Amendment to Part I: Who Appoints Judges and Prosecutors

**Fixed (decision, approved).** Saint Jude sits in real New Jersey, and in New Jersey the Governor appoints Superior Court judges and County Prosecutors, with the consent of the Senate [verified]. Part I 4.3, 4.6 and 5.8 described both offices as elected, and that was wrong for this state. **The correction is now applied in Part I (4.3, 4.6, 5.8 and the anchor list in 15.2).** This Part uses the corrected rule throughout.

**Consequence for the machine (Tendency).** A judgeship or a prosecutor's office is not won at the ballot box. It is won through **the state senators whose districts lie inside the county, and the county party chairman**. **The Senate has been elected from 40 single-senator districts since 1973 [verified], so a city of 1.1 million holds several seats (Design: about six, range four to eight) and no single senator speaks for it.** The senators hold a courtesy over nominations from their own county [memory, to verify: senatorial courtesy as practice], and the chairman tells the Governor's counsel whom the party wants. The Governor's party and the machine's party can differ, and then the machine must trade. Governor Brendan Byrne (January 1974 to January 1982) is in office for the whole seed period (dates as given in Part I 3.1a; his party is [memory]). A Republican Governor after January 1982 is **Weather** for the late run [memory, to verify].

## 1. How the Upperworld Meets the Underworld

### 1.1 The Four Currencies

**Fixed.** Four things pass between the city and the families. Each is something the upperworld has and the underworld wants, or the reverse.

| Currency | Who holds it | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| **Jobs** | The machine (city, county and Port Authority payrolls), the unions (hiring halls and referral lists), large employers | Loyalty, votes, silence, a place for a cousin |
| **Contracts** | The Board of Public Works, the Port Authority, the hospitals and the school system, general contractors | Concrete, paving, hauling, security, linen, vending: the legal side of every racket |
| **Credit** | The banks, the savings banks, the credit unions, and the street (the loan shark) | A business that lives, a debtor who obeys |
| **Paper** | Licensing and Zoning boards, Buildings and Inspections, the ABC Board, the courts and prosecutors | The right to open, to build, to pour a floor, to stay out of jail |

**Tendency.** A family's strength in the upperworld is the number of these it can reach and the depth at each. A man with a union, a bank officer, a ward leader and a lawyer is stronger than a man with fifty gunmen.

### 1.2 The Touchpoints

**Tendency.** The families do not own the upperworld. They rent access to it at specific points. The family named in each row is the one usually nearest. Any of them can reach any point, and the Iron Balance (Part III 6.1) governs the quarrels.

| Touchpoint | Upperworld side | What crosses | Usually nearest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hiring hall and referral list | Union business agent, employer | Jobs, no-show pay, labor peace | Pellegrino (docks), Ferrante (trucking), Sabatini (trades) |
| Pension and welfare fund | Trustees, fund administrator, consultants, brokers | Loans, commissions, insurance business, clinic referrals | Ferrante, Sabatini |
| Bid and contract | Board of Public Works, Port Authority, general contractor | Rigged bids, kickbacks, subcontracts | Sabatini |
| Permit and inspection | Buildings and Inspections, Zoning Board, councilman | Speed, a blind eye, a closed job site | Sabatini, Vannucci |
| Licence | Licensing Board, ABC Board | Entertainment, liquor, taxi medallions, peddlers | Vannucci (the Strip), Sabatini |
| Bank loan and account | Branch manager, loan committee | Friendly deposits, structured cash, quiet loans | all four, through intermediaries |
| Insurance and bond | Broker, surety agent, bondsman | Fire claims, performance bonds, bail | Sabatini, Ferrante |
| Judgeship and prosecutor | County senator, county chairman, Governor's counsel | Sponsorship, favor, a friendly date | Sabatini |
| Campaign and patronage | Ward leader, county committee | Money in, jobs out | all four |
| Funeral home and parish | Funeral director, pastor | Meeting rooms, neutral ground, a clean burial | all four |

**Open.** Which men stand at each point in any given year, how deep each tie goes, and which ties have already gone sour.

### 1.3 What Is Not Corrupt

**Fixed.** This is Ordinary Civic Reality (Part I 0.2, Tier 5). Most union officers, contractors, bank officers, councilmen, inspectors, lawyers and judges are not on anyone's payroll. They take the ordinary favors of the period (a lunch, a bottle at Christmas, a nephew's job) and regard that as manners. The families touch the upperworld at its edges and its weak joints, and the Storyteller should keep most of it honest, or the city will read as a cartoon. A reform challenger or an honest official is not a miracle. He is a feature of the system, and the system has a way of dealing with him (see section 4).

### 2.2 What a Local Is

**Fixed.** A local is a hall, a set of officers, and a contract. The same shape holds for a dock local, a Teamsters local and a hospital local, and the differences are in who has the leverage.

- **The hall.** A building with a meeting room, an office, often a bar, and a bulletin board. A man's local is his second parish.
- **The officers.** A president, a secretary-treasurer, a recording secretary and trustees, elected by the members. The working power is the **business agent**, a full-time paid officer who deals with employers, polices the contract, runs the hiring list where there is one, and decides which grievances get pushed. The business agent is the man a worker fears and an employer courts.
- **The stewards.** Unpaid or part-paid men on the job who carry grievances up. They are the union's nerves and its informers.
- **The contract.** A written agreement with an employer or an association of employers, running two to three years, covering wages, hours, hiring, seniority and the fund contributions. The contract is the local's reason to exist and its source of leverage.
- **The dues and the funds.** Members pay dues to the local. Employers pay per-hour contributions to the **pension** and **welfare** funds under the contract. The two flows are separate, and the second is far larger. See 2.6.
- **The international.** Above most locals sits a national union that charters the local, takes a per-capita share, can place it in **trusteeship** and appoints or removes officers. A local's independence from its international varies a great deal.

**Tendency.** Local officers are often men who came up in the trade, speak for it and do not leave. A business agent in his twenty-first year knows every employer, every foreman and every worker's family.

### 2.3 Elections and Trusteeships

**Fixed [memory].** The federal labor-management reporting law of 1959 (the Landrum-Griffin Act) requires secret-ballot elections of local officers at least every three years **[verified, Department of Labor text of section 401(b), Part VI pass]**, gives members the right to nominate and to see the books, bars certain convicted persons from union office for **five years after conviction or release, whichever is later, and the bar does not take effect until the conviction is upheld on appeal [verified, DOJ Criminal Resource Manual 2442]. Weather (12 October 1984): the Comprehensive Crime Control Act lengthens the bar to thirteen years for later convictions, lets a court cut it to no less than three, and makes it take effect at conviction [verified, same source]**, and lets the Labor Department investigate election complaints and sue to set an election aside.

**Tendency.** In practice, most locals hold their elections on schedule and the incumbent slate wins. The reasons are ordinary: officers control the hall, the newsletter, the mailing list, the job referrals and the grievance process, and a worker who runs against them risks his place on the list. A challenger who is honest, well liked and patient can still win, and a few do. A local controlled by a family's man is not a local where elections are fixed so much as one where nobody runs.

**Tendency.** The usual ways an entrenched slate keeps power: it sets the nomination rules and the date, it controls who is in good standing and may vote, it uses the referral list as a favor and a threat, and in the worst case it uses a beating or a burnt car against a challenger. The Labor Department acts only on complaint, and in 1975 its investigators are few.

**Trusteeship.** An international may take over a local, remove its officers and run it through an appointed trustee, for stated reasons including corruption. Trusteeship is also a tool of the international's own politics and a source of patronage. The Storyteller may use it both ways.

**Weather (1984 onward, [memory, to verify]).** Federal prosecutors begin to use civil racketeering suits to impose court-supervised trusteeships and monitors on corrupt locals. Offstage news only, and later than the seed.

### 2.4 Hiring Halls and the Work

**Fixed.** There are three ways men get jobs in a unionized trade.

- **The pool and the call.** On the docks, registered men report to a hiring point and are called by the employers' foremen, through a system the Waterfront Commission supervises since 1954 (Part I 2.5, 9.2). The shape-up, in which men stood on the pier and were picked by hand, is dead on paper and survives in the habits of the men who make the calls.
- **The referral hall.** In the building trades, the union hall keeps a list and sends men to contractors. The business agent controls the order. A man who is out of favor sits on the bench.
- **The shop.** In garment, hotel, hospital, steel and city jobs, a man is hired by the employer and then joins, and the union polices the contract. Control is looser, and the leverage is the strike.

**Tendency.** The list is the oldest leverage in the Life. Whoever controls it can reward and punish with nothing but a phone call. Where a family has the list it also has votes, soldiers' legitimate pay, no-show jobs for men who need a paper income, and a ready pool of men who owe a favor. The Storyteller should treat a business agent's list as a real asset, with a price.

**Period fact.** Most of the building trades and the docks are closed by custom to Black men. Entry runs through fathers and uncles, and the old Irish and Italian locals hand jobs down like land. Court orders, federal contractor rules and Black contractors' associations are pushing at this door through the 1970s (Anchor [memory]: federal contract-compliance plans for the building trades began in 1969 and 1970). The pushing makes some men furious and some men ashamed, and it makes the Eastside's Black contractors and the Black unions' politics a real pressure on the machine.

### 2.5 Strikes

**Private employers (Fixed [memory]).** Under federal law, workers may strike when a contract expires or over unfair labor practices, and most contracts carry a no-strike clause for their term. Strikes in an industry that threatens a national emergency can be halted for 80 days by court injunction (the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947). A strike that breaks the contract is a **wildcat**, and the union's own officers usually oppose it.

**Public employees.** New Jersey law gives public employees the right to organize and bargain through a state Public Employment Relations Commission, and forbids them to strike [memory, to verify: the 1968 employer-employee relations act]. The prohibition is real and is broken. The weapons are the **sick-out** (the "blue flu"), the work slowdown, and the strike that happens anyway. Police and firemen win binding arbitration of their contracts in New Jersey later in the decade [memory, to verify: 1977].

**How a strike runs (Tendency).**

1. The contract expires. The local holds a strike authorization vote.
2. Pickets go up. Other unions' men (the Teamsters above all) will not cross, and a Teamster line can shut a site in an hour.
3. The strike fund pays little. Families live on savings, on credit and on the parish.
4. The employer lets production fall, hires replacements if it can, and waits for the union to weaken. The government mediator arrives.
5. A settlement is reached in a late-night room. It is almost always less than the union's first demand, and the officers call it a victory.

**Tendency.** The families do not usually want strikes. A strike cuts the take on a dock, a truck route or a building site. They want the **threat** of one, because the threat is what they sell to an employer: pay for labor peace and the strike does not come. A family that lets a strike run has decided to hurt someone. The Storyteller should treat every strike in a sector the families tax as a sign of a quarrel (between a local and its men, between a local and an employer, or between two families).

**Open.** Whether and when any given contract is struck.

### 2.6 Pensions and Welfare Funds

**Fixed.** The Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) **was signed on 2 September 1974 [verified]**. **Reporting and disclosure rules and most fiduciary standards take effect on 1 January 1975. Vesting, participation and funding standards for existing plans take effect for plan years beginning after 31 December 1975, and collectively bargained plans may wait until plan years after 31 December 1980 [verified, Social Security Bulletin, December 1974, Part VI pass].** It sets standards for private pension and welfare plans: filing, disclosure to members, minimum funding, vesting, fiduciary duty on trustees (they must act solely in the members' interest and prudently), and a ban on dealing with **parties in interest**, which includes the union and the contributing employers. It creates a federal insurer for pension plans (the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation). The Labor and Treasury Departments enforce it. **The signing date, the creation of the pension insurer, and the bar on transactions between a plan and parties in interest are [verified]. The effective dates are now [verified] (above). The rest of this paragraph, including the enforcing agencies and the fiduciary standard, is [memory, to verify].**

**Fixed.** A **multiemployer plan** is the usual form for the docks, the Teamsters, the building trades and garment. Many employers pay by the hour into one fund. Trustees are named half by the union and half by the employers. The fund holds large sums, because the money comes in for decades before any of it goes out. A fund's trustees pick its administrator, its lawyers, its actuary, its investment advisers and its insurance brokers, and each of those is a contract.

**How the families touch a fund (Tendency).**

- **Loans.** A fund lends to a developer, a hotel, a nursing home or a friend. Before ERISA the standard was loose. After January 1975 a loan to a party in interest, or an imprudent loan, is a fiduciary breach.
- **Commissions.** A welfare fund buys insurance, dental care and clinic service. The broker, the carrier, the claims administrator and the clinic each take a cut, and the choice of each is a favor.
- **Jobs.** The fund employs administrators, clerks, consultants and doctors, and each seat is patronage.
- **Referrals.** A fund that sends members to a particular doctor, lawyer or clinic makes that man's practice.

**Tendency.** ERISA changes the price of the old ways. It does not stop them. It moves them toward intermediaries, toward loans that look prudent on paper (a mortgage on a building, a participation in a bank's loan pool), and toward vendors and advisers who are friends of friends. A Labor Department auditor with a long memory and a small staff is a real danger, and the men on both sides know it.

**Weather (1975 to 1980, moderate).** First ERISA audits, first fiduciary cases and first criminal referrals. Funds rewrite their investment policies, and trustees who borrowed from the fund must unwind. The effect is slow and uneven, and it favors the men who can afford good lawyers.

**Anchor (offstage, [memory]).** The biggest Teamsters pension fund in the Midwest had a long record of loans to casinos and real-estate ventures and was a subject of federal investigation in the 1970s. In Saint Jude's world it is the model of what a fund can become and is not itself in the city.

**Design (unsourced, Part VI to supply).** A dock or Teamsters fund in a city this size holds tens of millions of dollars. Actual figures, contribution rates per hour and benefit levels are **needed from Part VI**.

**Open.** Which funds exist, who administers them, how much each holds, which loans are bad.

### 2.7 The Locals, One by One

For each: the institution, then where the families touch it. Sizes are **Design**. Names are **Examples**.

**A. Local 402, Longshoremen (north bank).**
- *Institution.* The north-bank dock local, mostly Italian, covering Piers 1 to 6, the fish market's dock side and the rebuilt container Piers 4 and 6. Membership is falling with containerization (Part I 5.1). It bargains for a guaranteed annual income (a minimum yearly pay for registered men, whether or not there is work) and for container royalties (a payment **per ton of containerized cargo** to compensate for lost work). **The guaranteed annual income began in the New York area in 1966 and paid men with at least 700 hours **(the base year is read two ways in the sources: the year before, or before the programme began; to verify against the article)** for 2,080 hours of work or pay a year [verified, Christian Science Monitor, 1 February 1984, Part VI pass]. **The container royalty terms are verified (section 2.10).**
- *The structure of power.* A president, a business agent corps, the hiring-point foremen who are the employers' men but live in the neighborhood, and the Waterfront Commission's registration list above them. Control of who is called, and of who is on the register, is control of the local.
- *Families.* **Pellegrino** is nearest. The family's grip depends on the local, and the local's decline is the family's decline (Part III 4.2). Touchpoints: the hiring point, the welfare fund, loansharking among the men, gambling on the piers, pilferage, and a share of the container royalties.
- *Tendency.* Officers are a mixed lot: some are the family's men, some are their own men who deal with the family, and some are honest and tired. Rank-and-file Italian longshoremen are loyal to the family as a neighborhood fact and resent the family as a tax.
- *Open.* Names, the state of the funds, whether the royalties are enough, who wants to be president.

**B. Local 1201, Longshoremen (south bank).**
- *Institution.* The south-bank dock local, mostly Irish, centered on Kerrytown and the South Piers. By geography it has the best claim on the **Gannet Terminal**, which is on the South Flats (Part I 6.2). That claim is contested, and the container jobs are few and valuable.
- *Families.* The **Charlestown Brigades** are nearest and hold the officers through the parish and the pub. The old 50/50 arrangement with the Pellegrinos (Part III 5.1) is a standing deal over cargo and work. The 1973 strike, in which Pellegrino crews broke an independent longshoremen's strike on the South Piers, put Pellegrino men on 1201 ground and killed a man whose brother is still in Kerrytown (Part I 2.5). That is a fresh wound in the arrangement.
- *Open.* Whether 1201's officers knew of the 1973 strike beforehand, who in the local called for outside help, whether the independents have a surviving organization, and who has the Gannet.

**C. The Teamsters.**
- *Institution.* The Teamsters are many locals under a joint council, each with its own trade (**Examples**: a freight and warehouse local for the Eastside depots and the Racks, a ready-mix and construction drivers' local, a private carting and waste local, a food and produce local at the terminal). Trucking is the economy's last link, so a Teamster picket is the most effective in the city. Sizes **Design**: each local 1,500 to 6,000 members.
- *Families.* **Ferrante** is nearest, through freight, garment trucking, and the produce terminal. **Sabatini** reaches the ready-mix drivers (a concrete pour that cannot get concrete stops). **Vannucci** reaches carting and waste hauling. **Pellegrino** reaches dock drayage through Tri-State Freight and the drivers who pick up at the piers.
- *Tendency.* A Teamster local is the most likely place for a family's man to be an actual officer. It is also the most likely target of federal attention. The national union's leadership and its funds are offstage.
- *Weather.* **1980**: the Motor Carrier Act deregulates trucking and weakens the old route monopolies, which in turn weakens the Teamsters' bargaining position and the families' protection (Part I 13). A date and detail check is on the list in section 8.

**D. The Building Trades.**
- *Institution.* The Laborers, Bricklayers, Cement Masons, Carpenters, Ironworkers, Operating Engineers, Plumbers and Electricians, each with its own local and referral hall, and above them a **Building and Construction Trades Council** of the county that sets the political line (**Example** name). About 35,000 construction jobs in the city (Part I 9.1). Union density is high in the big downtown jobs and low in residential and small work.
- *Culture.* The trades are family trades, Irish and Italian, with the Hill supplying the bricklayers, hod carriers and plasterers (Part I 5.3). Black entry is the fight of the decade (2.4).
- *Families.* **Sabatini** is nearest, through the **Concrete Club** (section 3), the Board of Public Works and the Buildings Department. The Club's reach into the unions runs through the Laborers, the Cement Masons and the ready-mix Teamsters, because a job that cannot get men or concrete is a job that does not finish.
- *Tendency.* A job-site inspector or a business agent can stop a site in an hour (Part I 4.3). A contractor who pays his way finishes. A contractor who does not finds that his men do not show up, his concrete is late and his inspections fail.
- *Weather.* Construction is cyclical and political (Part I 9.5). The fiscal crisis starves public work from 1975, and money for housing and development depends on federal block grants and a recovering bond market.

**E. The Garment Locals.**
- *Institution.* The ladies' garment, furriers' and related locals of the Racks (**Examples**: a dressmakers' local, a cutters' local, a furriers' local, a finishers' and pressers' local). Members are mostly women, Puerto Rican, Black and Italian (Part I 9.4). Officers are mostly men, a generation older, and historically Jewish and Italian. The union runs a health plan and a pension fund, and it polices the contract against employers who cut corners.
- *Structure.* The trade runs on jobbers (who design and sell), manufacturers and **contractors** (small shops that sew). The shops are undercapitalized and work on thin margins [memory]. A shop that fails to pay its workers or its union fund can close overnight and reopen under another name.
- *Families.* **Ferrante** is nearest, through trucking (the chokepoint), loans to manufacturers and the fence of Hunts Row. The union locals themselves are a source of sweetheart contracts, in which an employer pays an officer to sign a cheaper contract than the real one.
- *Tendency.* Officers range from honest social-democrat veterans to men who have sold their members for a long time. The membership has little power, and the trade's fall (Part I 9.4, 13) is a standing weakness.
- *Weather.* The work moves south and overseas through the 1970s and 1980s. Shops close, and a union that loses its shops loses its funds.

**F. The Steelworkers and the Flats.**
- *Institution.* United Steelworkers locals in Killbuck Steel and the chemical and refinery plants, with the biggest halls in the city (Part I 6.3). Packinghouse workers are in their own local. Mill employment is about 14,000 in 1975, down from 22,000 in 1965. A no-strike arrangement in basic steel from the early 1970s is **[memory, to verify]**.
- *Culture.* Skilled, proud, Polish, Slovak, Italian and Black. The district officers are often career union men and, as a rule, among the cleaner leaders in the city. The members are the most likely to be hurt by a closing.
- *Families.* **Vannucci** is nearest, but the family touches the mills at the edges: scrap and haulage contracts inside the plants, maintenance and cleaning contractors, vending, waste hauling, and the bars and halls around the gates. "Labor racketeering in the mills" (Part III 4.4) means pressure on the contractors who work there more than control of the Steelworkers' leadership. **Tendency (exceptions): no family controls the union, but an individual officer or a single local may be compromised. A dirty Steelworkers local is possible and is Open. It is not the norm.**
- *Weather.* Closings cut pensions. The federal pension insurer created by ERISA (2.6) becomes a live concern for any plan in a failing mill.

**G. Hotel and Restaurant.**
- *Institution.* A hotel, restaurant and bartenders' local covering Crown Harbor's hotels, Steakhouse Row, the Strip's clubs and the Greek diners. Members: waiters, cooks, bartenders, maids, dishwashers, many of them women and recent arrivals.
- *Families.* **Sabatini** reaches the downtown restaurants and the hotel trade. **Vannucci** reaches the clubs. The leverage is the picket line in front of a restaurant, and its use is a sale: pay the officers and the picket does not come.
- *Open.* Everything about named officers and funds.

**H. Hospital Workers.**
- *Institution.* A hospital workers' local (of the type organized in northern cities in the 1960s) covering aides, orderlies, kitchen and laundry staff at Mercy General, Saint Jude University Hospital, Mount Zion and the others. Members are mostly Black and Puerto Rican women on low wages. Federal law extended collective bargaining to private non-profit hospitals in 1974 [memory, to verify], and a wave of organizing follows.
- *Culture.* More political, more civil-rights in tradition and less tied to the old city than the other locals. Mount Zion's financial trouble (Part I 5.6) is a real threat to its members.
- *Families.* **Weak.** The touchpoints are the vendors: linen supply, laundry, waste, vending, security and building work on hospital extensions (Part I 9.5, Part III 7.4). The union is more likely to fight those contractors than to share their money. **Tendency (exceptions): no family controls the local, but an individual officer may be compromised, and a dirty hospital local is possible and Open.**
- *Tendency.* A man who wants to find the one place where the machine and the families have less hold will look here.

**I. Sanitation.**
- *Institution.* **Public**: the city's Department of Sanitation workers, municipal employees with a union of their own (**Example** name), bound by the public-employee rules (2.5) and subject to the layoffs of 1975 to 1977. **Private**: commercial carting, the Teamsters carting local and the firms that hold routes (Part I 8.4, Part III 7.4).
- *Families.* **Vannucci** is nearest in carting, waste hauling and illegal dumping. A route is a territory that can be sold, and a customer who tries to change hauler learns the price. The public side is a machine job pool, and the families' touch there is through the ward leader and the foreman.
- *Weather.* Hazardous waste becomes a national scandal in the late 1970s (Part I 13), which turns carting from a nuisance into a public-health and prosecution risk.

**J. The PBA and the Uniformed Unions.**
- *Institution.* The Saint Jude Patrolmen's Benevolent Association, the sergeants', detectives' and lieutenants' associations, the Guardians (Black officers), the Port Authority police associations, and the firefighters' union with the Kerrytown Firemen's Association (Part II 2.2, Part I 6.1, 8.4). These are the strongest public-sector unions in the city and the machine's closest political allies.
- *Politics.* They endorse, they give or withhold votes, they run candidates for council, they lobby Trenton, and in the fiscal crisis they fight layoffs (Part II 2.1). The PBA defends its men in discipline cases and resists civilian oversight.
- *Families.* **No family controls these unions, though an individual officer may be compromised, and a dirty local or association is possible and Open.** The touchpoints are the cousins, the defense lawyers who work for the legal funds, the trustees of the pension funds, and the Pad intermediaries (Part II 3.2), a few of whom are union men. Treat any claim that "the PBA is owned" as Street Belief (Tier 4).
- *Tendency.* A union man who takes from a bookmaker is a cop first and a union officer second. A union officer who refuses is a cop who has made an enemy.

### 3.2 How a Business Earns

**Fixed.** A real business in Saint Jude earns the way a real business earns anywhere. Customers pay. Out of what comes in the owner pays for goods and materials, wages (at union scale where there is a union), rent, insurance, utilities, licences and taxes, and services any debt. What is left is the owner's living and his chance to grow. The Storyteller should remember that most owners are close to the edge. A man with a good week and a bad week and a note due on Friday is the ordinary case, and he is the man the loan shark waits for.

**Period conditions (Tendency).** Rents in the old districts are low and falling. Wages and materials rise with inflation (Part I 9.10), and a small owner can raise prices only as far as his customers' paychecks allow. Credit is scarce for anyone without collateral or connections. Cash is the common coin, which helps an owner hide income and hurts him when a robber, a burglar or a collector knows it.

**Design (unsourced, wide ranges). Part VI 4.4 narrows these ranges and adds sales, rents and wages by trade; it is Design too.** The table shows rough cost structure by trade. Each figure is a share of sales. "Cash share" is the portion of sales taken in cash, which decides how easily income can be hidden.

### 3.3 The Sectors

Each entry gives how the sector earns, what 1975 is doing to it, and where the families and the machine touch it. Shares and direction follow Part I 9.1.

**Manufacturing (falling fast).**
- *Earns.* Steel, chemicals, refining, meatpacking and printing sell into national markets. Garment sells to buyers who press shops on price (3.2). The big plants are unionized, capital-heavy and run by managers who answer to owners elsewhere. The small shops are family firms.
- *1975.* Imports, the move to the Sun Belt and old plant make the mills and the Racks lose ground each year (Part I 6.3, 9.4). A plant that closes does not reopen.
- *Touch.* **Vannucci** at the edges of the mills (scrap, haulage, waste, maintenance contractors, chop shops). **Ferrante** in the Racks (trucking, loans to manufacturers, the fence). The large plants themselves are mostly clean of the families and a hard place for them.

**Port and transport (falling).**
- *Earns.* Stevedores, terminal operators, shipping agents, customs brokers, truckers, warehousemen and chandlers earn on volume and speed. A line that wants a ship turned around in a day pays to avoid trouble (Part I 9.2).
- *1975.* Containers cut labor per ton and favor the big terminals at Newark and Elizabeth (Part I 3.1a). Saint Jude's Gannet Terminal is second tier and fights for what it can get.
- *Touch.* **Pellegrino** on the north piers, fish market and drayage. **Ferrante** on the road. The **Brigades** on the south. A shipping line or a customs broker pays for peace and for the right inspector.

**Retail and wholesale (flat, moving to the suburbs).**
- *Earns.* Department stores, supermarkets, small shops and the open market on the Via. The big retailers are losing customers to suburban malls (Part I 5.2). The small shop lives on its neighborhood.
- *1975.* Downtown retail is dying. Neighborhood shops hold as long as the neighborhood does, and the Hill and the Eastside are aging and shrinking.
- *Touch.* Neighborhood shops are the ordinary payers of protection (3.4). Wholesale food, produce and fish are Pellegrino and Ferrante ground (the fish market, the produce terminal).

**Finance, insurance, real estate and law (flat).**
- *Earns.* Banks on the spread between what they pay depositors and charge borrowers. Insurers on premiums and investment. Realtors and brokers on commissions. Law firms on fees. See 3.7 for banks and section 5 for the professions.
- *Touch.* The firms most exposed to the families are the small ones. The large banks and insurers are exposed at a few officers and branches.

**Construction (cyclical).**
- *Earns.* General contractors win jobs by bid, hire subcontractors, and live on the gap between the price they bid and the price they pay. A subcontractor lives on a thin margin (3.2) and on being paid on time by the general. Developers earn on the spread between building cost and what a project sells or rents for.
- *1975.* Private building is slack and public work is squeezed by the fiscal crisis. Federal block-grant money replaces the old urban-renewal money and is smaller and more political (Part I 9.5). Interest rates fall in 1975 and rise sharply late in the decade (Part I 9.6), and a developer who borrowed short is at risk.
- *Touch.* **Sabatini** through the Concrete Club, the Board of Public Works and the Buildings Department (3.6, section 4). The whole sector pays for paper and for peace.

**Services, hotels, restaurants and entertainment (rising).**
- *Earns.* Bars, restaurants, diners, hotels, nightclubs, laundries, funeral homes, taxis, parking and cleaning. Small, cash-heavy and local.
- *1975.* The Strip is the cash capital of the city (Part I 5.5). Brighton Point's shops and restaurants are cash and fragile (Part I 5.7).
- *Touch.* **Vannucci** on the Strip. **Sabatini** downtown. Every family through bars, restaurants and funeral homes. These are the Life's meeting rooms and its fronts (Part III 7.4, 7.8).

**Health and education (rising).**
- *Earns.* Hospitals earn on patient fees and insurance reimbursement, nursing homes on fees and public programs, and Catholic and independent schools on tuition and gifts. Mount Zion is in financial trouble (Part I 5.6). The Catholic hospitals are run by religious orders (Part I 12.1).
- *1975.* Growing and increasingly expensive, with more federal and insurance money flowing through.
- *Touch.* Mostly at the **vendors**: linen, laundry, food, waste, security, vending, and the building contracts for new wings. A hospital is a great buyer, and a buyer can be pressed.

**Government (flat to falling).**
- *Earns.* Not a business in the ordinary sense. It is the buyer of the largest contracts in the city and the employer of a fifth of its workforce. See sections 4 and 6.

### 3.4 How a Business Relates to the Life

**Tendency.** A business in Saint Jude stands in one of six relations to the families. Most stand in none. The six overlap, and a business can move from one to another.

1. **None.** The great majority of businesses, including most large employers. They pay the Pad's indirect cost in higher prices and never see a family.
2. **Pays protection (the "tax").** A negotiated weekly or yearly sum for a quiet life, labor peace, credit and an introduction. **Design (unsourced):** from a few dollars a week for a candy store to a few hundred for a large club. The shopkeeper's own view varies. One pays gladly because the family settles his troubles, one pays and resents it, one pays because the man in the next shop does. The sum is a negotiation and not a demand, and it is paid on net (Part III 7.1) in principle and on what the family thinks the shop makes in practice.
3. **Borrows from the street.** A man who cannot get a bank loan borrows from a shark (Part III 7.3). The debt is a leash, and the **bust-out** (a shop run into the ground on credit) is its end. The owner who borrowed to make payroll is the commonest victim.
4. **Is a partner.** A man with money, a license or a clean name takes a family man as a silent partner, sometimes for protection and sometimes for capital. He shares the net and loses control.
5. **Is a front or a skim.** The family uses the business's cash flow (3.5).
6. **Is a customer or supplier.** A family's linen, carting, vending or concrete company sells to the business, often at a price the buyer does not set.

**Period attitudes (Part II 0.2).** Many owners regard the arrangement as the cost of doing business in the same sense as a rent or a license. A baker on the Hill who has paid for thirty years may not call it extortion. A garment owner who borrowed from a shark in 1960 and is still paying may. Individuals vary. The Storyteller should not give an owner a modern view of it.

### 3.5 Fronts and Skims, Seen From the Owner's Side

Part III 7.8 describes what the family does. This is what the owner's books look like.

**The front.** The family's dirty cash goes into a cash business's register as ordinary sales, and the owner pays tax on it as income, so the cash comes out clean at the cost of a tax bill. The weak point is **cost of goods**. A bar cannot sell more drinks than it bought bottles for. A restaurant cannot serve more meals than it bought food for. An auditor compares sales with purchases, inventory and the number of seats and hours, and a front that shows twice the sales a business of its size could make is a front. So fronts favor businesses where the goods are cheap, the work is hard to count, or both: a laundromat, a car wash, a parking lot, a jukebox route, a nightclub's cover charge, a funeral home's services, a vending route, a real-estate rent roll. The table in 3.2 shows why: high margin, high cash share, a cost structure that is hard to check.

**The skim.** The reverse. The owner takes a share of the cash before it is rung up and reports less than he earned. It is profitable and it is a tax crime. The government's usual method is to compare what the owner spent and owns with what he reported, or his bank deposits with his reported income, and to charge the difference [memory, to verify: the IRS net-worth and bank-deposits methods]. Patience is the IRS's weapon (Part II 5.4), and a skim that runs for years is a case waiting to be built. The two points where a skim shows are the **bank deposits** (cash banked in odd amounts or never banked) and the **owner's life** (a house, a car and a boat that his reported income could not buy).

**The sales tax and the license.** A cash business also owes the State its sales tax on what it sells, and a bar owes its license fee and stands under the ABC Board's eye (Part II 4.6). A skim that cheats the State is a second exposure.

**What the owner knows.** The owner of a front or a skimmed business is rarely a fool. He knows the risk and has priced it. A man who owns three bars, a pizzeria and a pizza oven he cannot afford has a view of the audit. The Storyteller should show owners who are anxious, owners who are cavalier, and owners who sleep fine, and none of them as a type.

**Open.** Which businesses are fronts, which are skims, which are both, and which are honest businesses whose owners are merely friends of the family.

### 3.6 The Concrete Club and Its Victims

**Fixed (Part III 7.4, Part I 2.5).** The Concrete Club is a cartel in downtown concrete and construction work. It rigs bids and takes about two percent from contractors who pay to be allowed to win. It is the Sabatini family's own, and in 1974 **the Sabatini boss** took it entirely into his own hands, after which a brother-in-law and three contractors are gone **(what happened to them is Open)**. In the real world a comparable New York cartel was exposed in the mid 1980s [memory, to verify, including the share and the size threshold]. Saint Jude's Club is its own, with its own terms.

**How it works (Tendency, from the known shape of bid-rigging [memory]).**

- **The rotation.** The contractors in the Club decide in advance who will win each job. The others bid high, or submit a "cover" bid that is meant to lose, or bid and then withdraw. The winner's price is higher than an honest market would produce.
- **The tax.** The winner pays a percentage of the contract to the Club, which passes it up. **Design (unsourced):** about two percent of the contract price on larger jobs. The percentage is small beside the inflation of the bid.
- **The chokepoint.** A concrete pour needs ready-mix at the right hour, and the drivers are Teamsters (2.7C). A contractor outside the Club finds his concrete late, his inspections slow and his men short.
- **The public bid.** On a public job the law requires advertised bids and award to the **lowest responsible bidder** [memory, to verify: the New Jersey public contracts law]. "Responsible" is the soft word. A board that wants a low bidder out can find that he is not responsible, because his bonding is thin, his references are poor or his equipment is short. The Board of Public Works holds this discretion (Part I 4.3, section 4).
- **The bond.** A public contractor must post a performance bond from a surety (5). A contractor who cannot be bonded cannot bid. A surety agent who is a friend of the Club is a gate.

**The victims (Tendency).** The Club's cost falls on several people at once, and most of them do not know it.

- **Contractors who refuse.** They lose bids they should have won, and some leave downtown work for suburban or private work where the Club's reach is weaker.
- **Contractors who pay.** They pass the tax and the inflated bid on, take a lower margin, or both. The honest ones among them pay out of a thin margin (3.2).
- **Subcontractors and suppliers.** They are paid late, squeezed, or told to use the right supplier.
- **Developers and owners.** They pay more to build, and so do their tenants. A downtown tower costs a few percent more than it should.
- **Taxpayers.** A public job is paid for out of a city short of money (6), and a rigged bid is a wasted dollar.
- **Excluded contractors.** Black and other minority-owned firms have been outside the Club and the unions for generations, and are pressed to the margins (2.4).
- **The men who died in 1974.** The Club's own members have the worst of it when the head decides the Club is his alone. Who they were and why they were chosen is **Open**.

**Tendency.** The honest contractor in Saint Jude has four choices: join and pay, stay outside and work where the Club is weak, quit downtown, or fight. A few fight, with a lawyer, the press or a prosecutor, and a few of those win. The Club's strength is not that every contractor is corrupt. It is that the honest ones are tired, scattered and short of friends.

**Weather.** Federal and state anti-trust and racketeering tools improve from the late 1970s, and a cartel like the Club becomes a prosecutor's target in the 1980s (Part II 6.6). In 1977 federal public-works law sets aside a share of contracts for minority-owned firms [memory, to verify], which opens a new door and a new racket: the firm that is a minority-owned front for a white contractor.

**Open.** The Club's actual members, the sums it takes, the surviving men from 1974, the jobs it holds, and what the Board of Public Works knows.

### 3.7 Banks and Credit

**Fixed (Part I 9.6).** The banks of Saint Jude are **First Harbor National Bank** and four other banks in Crown Harbor, several savings banks, and credit unions, including the **Italian Credit Union** on the Hill and the **Dunmore Savings Bank** on the Eastside, which are community institutions.

**How a bank works in 1975 (Tendency, [memory]).**

- **Deposits and loans.** A bank earns on the spread between what it pays depositors and what it charges borrowers. A branch manager has real discretion over small loans, and a loan committee at headquarters decides the large ones.
- **Regulators.** A national bank answers to a federal examiner, a state bank to the state, and a savings bank or savings-and-loan to its own regulators. Examiners come on a schedule and look mostly at the soundness of the loans.
- **Interest ceilings.** Federal rules cap what banks may pay on deposits (known as Regulation Q), and state usury laws cap what they may charge. The ceilings keep legal credit tight and send marginal borrowers elsewhere. Exact rules and dates are **to verify**.
- **Collateral and connections.** A bank lends to a man with property and a name. Old families, big firms and men known to the loan officer borrow cheaply. A new immigrant, a small shop, a Black owner or a man with no house borrows at a street rate or not at all.

**Redlining (Part I 9.6).** Banks lend cautiously to the Eastside, and the habit is just beginning to be challenged. The effect is that Eastside owners, and many on the Hill, have no bank to go to and turn to a church, a credit union, a numbers banker or a shark. The Dunmore Savings Bank and the community credit unions fill part of the gap, and they are small.

**The families and the banks (Tendency).**

- **Deposits.** A cash man needs a place to put cash. A friendly branch manager or a small bank is worth a great deal. Currency Transaction Reports exist on paper after 1970 and few are filed, and a careful man keeps his deposits small and spread (Part I 9.6, Part III 7.8).
- **Loans.** A clean front, a developer or a partner can borrow from a bank, and the loan can fund a purchase the family wants. A banker who lends against a mob-connected borrower often knows it and decides it is good business.
- **The street as competitor.** The shark lends to the men banks refuse. A credit union that lends on character to a Hill man is competing with the Life, and the Life knows it.
- **The numbers banker as lender.** On the Eastside the numbers banks have, for fifty years, financed churches, funerals and shops (Part I 9.8). The state's daily game from May 1975 begins to erode that, and the Eastside's informal bank shrinks with it (Weather).

### 4.2 How the Machine Works

**Fixed (Part I 4.4).** The machine is a chain of favors that ends in votes. At the top stands a **county party chairman** and the county committee. Below them are the **ward leaders**, one for each of the fifteen wards. Below those are the **precinct captains** (the committeemen of the election districts). Each level owes the one above it turnout and holds a list of jobs to give.

**The shape of it (Tendency).**

- **The county chairman.** The party's working head, who may or may not hold public office. He decides slates, controls the party's money and treasury, and speaks to the Governor's counsel on appointments (section 0.4). Who he is and what faction he comes from is **Open**.
- **The ward leader.** Often a councilman or the man who picks one. He runs a ward club (a storefront with a name on the glass, an office, a bar or a card room), holds office hours, and settles the ward's troubles: a job, a licence, a ticket, a landlord, a funeral, a turkey at Thanksgiving. His power is the list of people who owe him.
- **The precinct captain.** Knows every household in a few city blocks, who is sick, who is out of work, who is feuding, who will vote and who needs a ride. In New Jersey each election district has a committeeman and a committeewoman [memory, to verify], so the women of the machine hold real posts at the bottom of the ladder and are kept off the rungs above it (Part I 12.4, route 1).
- **The line.** On the primary ballot the party organization's endorsed candidates stand together in a favored position, and an independent must run off it [memory, to verify]. The line is worth more than a platform.
- **Election Day.** Captains pay "street money" to the workers who get voters out. Old and sick voters are helped to the booth or the absentee ballot. Machines are lever machines. The county board that runs the election is appointed by the Governor [memory, to verify for 1975]. Most of this is ordinary and legal, and part is not, and the line between them moves with the neighborhood.

**Tendency.** The machine is not the mob. It is a patronage organization that needs money, votes, jobs to give and a quiet city. The families are one of its customers and one of its suppliers. A ward leader may take a family's money and refuse a family's request. A family may give to every ward in a district and fear none of them. Each side uses the other and each side knows the other will leave when it must.

### 4.3 The Factions

**Tendency (Period Mind, Part II 0.2).** The machine is not one voice. It is a set of ethnic and occupational wings that cooperate when they must and distrust each other when they can. The prejudices are real and are spoken aloud, and individuals vary within them (Part II 0.3).

| Wing | Base | Tools | Wants | Edges |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Irish old guard** | Kerrytown wards, the firehouses, the PBA, the parish and the Archdiocese | A century of seniority, the party treasury, the police and fire jobs | To keep the Mayor's office, the chairmanship and the key departments | Shrinking numbers. It regards the Italians as upstarts and the Black wing as a danger. |
| **Italian wing** | The Hill wards, the building trades, contractors, the Italian Credit Union | Votes, labor, campaign money, the Sabatini family's friends and a growing bench of lawyers | The Board of Public Works, more judgeships, a share of the Mayor's appointments and the respect it has been denied since the 1950s (Part I 4.4) | Held back by the Irish. The old Bellacorte families look down on it, and it looks down on them (Part I 5.4). |
| **Black wing** | The Eastside wards, Mount Zion Baptist, Bethel A.M.E., the tenant councils, the *Courier* | Votes, pastors who can deliver thousands of them (Part I 10.3), and the federal and state money for housing, health and jobs that passes through Black institutions (Part I 5.6) | Power in proportion to 30 percent of the city. **Design:** three or four of fifteen Council seats | Resisted by the white wings. Divided among ministers, younger activists and the numbers money (Part III 5.2). |
| **Polish and Eastern European Democrats** | Kosciuszko Park, the Flats, the Steelworkers' halls | Reliable votes, union men, nuns, cops | Jobs for its sons, the schools kept as they are | Conservative on school desegregation. It supplies voters and seldom the top seats. |

### 4.4 The Wards (Design sketch)

**Fixed.** There are fifteen wards (Part I 4.3). The **Sixth Ward** is the old Greek, Lebanese and Armenian quarter near the Basin (Part I 6.4). Each ward holds about 70,000 people.

**Design.** The remaining ward lines are **Open**. As a sketch only: three wards on the Hill (North Hill, South Hill, the East Slope with Mercer Avenue), two in Kerrytown and the South Piers, two Black wards on the Eastside, one Eastside ward for the Racks, the Rail Yards and the Puerto Rican rooming houses, one for the Point and Pescadores Row, one for Crown Harbor and Bellacorte, the Sixth Ward, one for the Flats and Ironworks, one for Kosciuszko Park and the Greenwood line, one for the Heights and Fenwick, and one for Brighton Point. That makes fifteen. The Storyteller may redraw any line except the Sixth.

**Tendency.** A councilman holds his ward as a fief, and the Council defers to him on what happens inside it (a custom often called councilmanic courtesy [memory]). He decides which variance passes, which street is paved, which tavern gets its licence and who gets the sanitation job. A man who crosses a councilman in his own ward learns how long the courtesy runs.

### 4.5 The Mayor and the Departments

**Fixed (Part I 4.3).** The Mayor is the machine's head in practice. He appoints the police commissioner, the fire chief, and the heads of Buildings, Sanitation, Public Works and Law, and two of the three members of the Board of Public Works. He sets the budget and sends it to the Council.

**Tendency.** A mayor who is the machine's creature uses these powers to pay its debts. A mayor who came up against the machine uses them to break it, and finds how little he controls: the Council holds his zoning votes, the courts and the Prosecutor belong to the Governor's appointments, the Port Authority is shared with the Governor, and the unions can stop the city. The police commissioner is the Mayor's man (Part II 2.1), and a new commissioner reopens every arrangement under the Pad (Part II 3.4). The Mayor's real discretion is where he spends his appointments and what he lets his commissioners do.

**Open.** Who the Mayor is in January 1975, whether he is the man who created the Dolan Commission, whether he is running again, and what he owes.

### 4.6 The Board of Public Works

**Fixed (Part I 4.3).** Three members. Two are appointed by the Mayor and one is elected citywide. The Board awards every major contract: dredging, bridge repair, paving, school construction. This is where the Concrete Club and its competitors do business.

**What the Board decides (Tendency).**

- **The specification.** Before bids, an engineer writes what is to be built. A specification that fits one firm's equipment, one supplier's product or one contractor's experience limits the field before a bid is made. The staff, not the Board, often writes the spec, and a spec writer is a seat worth having.
- **The bid.** The job is advertised and sealed bids are opened. The law requires award to the lowest responsible bidder [memory, to verify], and "responsible" is the soft word (3.6).
- **Change orders.** The contractor wins at a low price and is paid more as the job changes. Change orders are where a low bid is made whole, and where the Board's staff and the contractor share a secret.
- **Emergency contracts.** When a bridge, a school boiler or a main fails, the Board may skip bidding for urgent work [memory, to verify]. Deferred maintenance (Part I 4.5, 8.2) produces emergencies, and emergencies produce no-bid contracts.
- **Pre-qualification and bonding.** The Board keeps a list of firms it considers fit to bid, and demands bonds (3.6, 5).

**The elected member (Tendency).** The third member is not controlled by the Mayor. He cannot outvote the other two. He can demand records, ask questions in public, dissent, leak to a newspaper and give a reform campaign a place to stand. The seat is the machine's pressure valve and the reformers' pulpit. **Open:** the member's election cycle, who holds it, and what he has seen.

**Audit.** New Jersey municipalities are audited each year by an accountant they themselves hire [memory, to verify]. The man who audits the Board's books is chosen by the city that owns them. The audit rarely finds what the Board does not wish found.

### 4.7 Zoning, Buildings and Licensing

**Zoning (Fixed, Part I 4.3).** The Zoning Board decides variances and the Council votes on rezoning. Rezoning is a councilman's gift in his ward. A variance is a lawyer's art: the petition, the hearing, the neighbors, the "hardship." The same few lawyers appear again and again, and an **expediter** (a man who knows who must be asked) is a trade.

**Buildings and Inspections (Fixed).** An inspector can close a job site in an hour. Inspectors are poorly paid, and the sum that makes one hurry or look away is small beside what a delay costs a contractor. The great majority are honest. Whether a given inspector is bought or merely afraid is often hard to tell from outside.

**The Licensing Board (Fixed).** Taxi medallions (about 3,500, Part I 8.3), peddlers' permits, and entertainment, dance, amusement and adult-business licences. A medallion is an asset that can be sold, and the Board's discretion makes it a favor. The loosening of obscenity law through the 1970s (Part I 5.5) multiplies the licences the Strip's operators want.

**Liquor (Part I 4.2).** The State ABC Board licenses every bar. In practice a bar owner needs the blessing of his councilman, the tolerance of his precinct and a clean enough record, and the ABC Board can revoke a licence for tax fraud or gambling (Part II 4.6).

**Tendency.** Each of these offices sells speed more than outcome. A permit that would be granted in a year is granted in a month, and the month costs money. A reformer's first discovery is that most of the corruption is in the delay.

### 4.8 Judges, the County Prosecutor and the Governor

**Fixed (section 0.4).** The Governor appoints Superior Court judges and County Prosecutors with the Senate's consent [verified]. A judge serves an initial seven years and, if reappointed, holds tenure until 70 [verified].

**The path to a judgeship (Tendency).** A lawyer with a practice and a party record comes to the county chairman's attention. The chairman, the state senators from the county's districts and the county bar all weigh in with the Governor's counsel. The lawyer is a machine man, a respected trial lawyer, a prosecutor's assistant, a friend of the right ward leader, or all of these. At the first reappointment, seven years later, the judge is still within reach of the same people. After tenure he owes them little, and many judges are tenured and independent.

**What a friendly judge can do (Tendency).** Grant an adjournment. Set bail (Part II 6.2). Rule on a suppression motion (Part II 6.3). Pick a lenient sentence. Delay a trial until a witness forgets. The county's **assignment judge** decides which judge hears which case [memory, to verify], and the assignment is itself a power. Most judges are honest, and an honest judge who is also a machine man is the common case. A bribed judge is rare, because a judge has much to lose and little to gain from a bribe the Prosecutor might hear of.

**Municipal Court (Part I 4.3).** Traffic, misdemeanors and preliminary hearings. Its judges are appointed by the city [memory, to verify], and the court is the machine's best-known small favor: the fixed ticket, the lost summons, the easy fine. Clerks matter more than judges.

**The county row offices (Open).** New Jersey counties elect a sheriff, a county clerk and a surrogate [memory, to verify]. In Saint Jude, where city and county are one government (Part I 1.1), these are machine prizes housed in the Hall of Records. Part I does not name them. They are Open.

**The County Prosecutor (Fixed, section 0.4).** The Governor appoints him for five years [verified]. His office prosecutes murder, robbery and assault, and decides which cases of police or union corruption to bring (Part I 4.3). He can convene grand juries (Part II 6.4), and he works alongside the State Attorney General's office, which supervises prosecutors statewide [memory, to verify]. In New Jersey of this period, assistant prosecutors were often part-time and kept private law practices [memory, to verify], which means a man can prosecute by day and defend by night. Detectives on the Prosecutor's staff are a small force of their own.

**Tendency.** A Prosecutor who prosecutes the machine may not be reappointed. A Prosecutor who prosecutes well and carefully is often rewarded with a judgeship. He serves two masters, the Governor who appointed him and the county that recommended him, and he sometimes serves a third, his own ambition. He is a mixed man like any other (Part II 0.3).

**Open.** The Prosecutor, the Governor's counsel, the assignment judge, the senators from the county's districts and the county chairman are unfixed. The Storyteller creates them when needed. A Governor of the other party after January 1982 narrows the pipeline for a machine whose friends were appointed by his predecessor [memory, to verify].

### 4.9 The Port Authority Board

**Fixed (Part I 8.1).** The Killbuck River Port Authority is a public corporation. The Governor and the Mayor appoint its board. It has a salaried executive director and no voters. It owns the Gannet Terminal, the container piers, the Narrows Bridge, the Killbuck Tunnel and the airport, and runs its own police of about 600 (Part II 4.1).

**Design.** A board of nine commissioners with staggered terms, some named by the Governor and some by the Mayor. The size and the split are **Open**.

**What it controls (Tendency).**

- **Terminal leases.** Who runs the Gannet Terminal and the container piers, and on what terms. The leases bind to labor (2.7A, B).
- **Construction and maintenance contracts.** Bridge, tunnel, terminal and airport work, on a scale larger than the city's.
- **Jobs.** Police, toll collectors, clerks and engineers, in great number. Tolls are collected in cash by the Authority's own collectors (Part I 8.2), and a toll booth is both a machine job and a skim risk.
- **Concessions.** Airport parking, restaurants, ground transport.
- **Bonds.** The Authority issues bonds against tolls and rents. Underwriters, bond counsel and financial advisers are paid on each issue (6.4).

**Tendency.** The Authority is the only place where the Governor, the Mayor and the families all have a stake, and none of them answers to a voter. Control of the board is a quiet fight that decides the port's future more than any election.

**Anchor (offstage, Part I 8.1).** The real Port Authority of New York and New Jersey is the model. In Saint Jude's world it is a sister body, and the real one does not operate in the city.

### 4.10 Campaign Money and Patronage

**Where the money comes from (Tendency).** Contractors who want city work. Unions with a contract at stake. Lawyers who appear before the Zoning Board. Banks and bond firms. Developers. Liquor distributors, vendors and licensees. Men who want a favor in the future. And, through fronts and friends, the families.

**How it travels (Tendency).** Testimonial dinners for a ward leader or judge. Advertisements in the souvenir journal printed for the dinner, which cost more than any advertisement is worth. Cash in an envelope. Tickets sold to the contractors in a ward. A donation to the feast committee or the parish. Legal limits and reporting rules exist (the State's campaign reporting law of 1973 and the federal law of 1971 and 1974 [memory, to verify]), and in a ward race few are checked.

**What it buys (Tendency).** A hearing. A seat at the table when the Board decides. A phone call to an inspector. A judge's attention. A job for a cousin. And, above all, the right not to be bothered.

**Design (unsourced). Part VI 7.4 gives defaults and ranges.** A ward race costs on the order of tens of thousands of dollars, a citywide primary a few hundred thousand.

**Patronage (Part I 4.4).** City jobs are the machine's currency. Some are classified civil service and filled by examination. Others are exempt or provisional and filled at will [memory, to verify]. Federal job programs in the 1970s add public-service jobs that the city may fill (6.7). A hiring freeze (Part I 4.5, Part II 2.1) deflates the currency, and a machine with fewer jobs to give has less to buy. This is the quiet reason it weakens.

**The families and the machine (Tendency).**

- Each family gives where it has business: the **Sabatini** in the Italian wing and the Board; the **Pellegrino** in the dock wards; the **Vannucci** in the Strip's ward; the **Ferrante** in the Racks and the Eastside's white wards. They give through fronts, friends and donations to the right feast, and they rarely give openly.
- A made man does not stand for office, because his record and his oath make him a liability. His friends and his nephews may.
- The machine keeps the families at arm's length in public and takes their help in private. Its best-known meeting places are the ones the Life already uses: the Feast of San Rocco, the wake, the funeral home (Part III 2.4, 6.4).
- A reformer's best charge is "the machine's friends," and a machine's best answer is that everyone in the city has a cousin on the Hill.

### 4.11 Reform

**Fixed (Part I 4.4).** Reform candidates win about one election in three.

**Who reformers are (Tendency, not types).** A reform candidate may be an honest Greenwood lawyer, a young Fenwick graduate, a dissident ward leader who lost a fight, a Black minister, an ambitious assistant prosecutor, a woman from a civic league, an ex-cop from the Dolan years, or a machine man who has decided he can win alone. Some are saints, some are cranks, some are opportunists, and many are several at once (Part II 0.3).

**How a reformer wins.** Through a split in the machine. Through a scandal that reaches the front page of the *Mirror* and the *Chronicle*. Through the turnout of the Eastside, the Hill's young families and the Greenwood professionals. Through a fiscal crisis that makes the machine's waste visible. Through the U.S. Attorney, the State Commission or the County Prosecutor, if one is willing (Part II 4.5, 5.3).

**How the machine answers (Tendency).**

- **Co-opt.** Offer the reformer a seat and a title.
- **Technical defeat.** Challenge his nominating petitions, deny him the line, redraw his ward.
- **Isolate.** Starve his supporters of jobs and favors.
- **Smear.** Raise his marriage, his religion, his race, his debts.
- **Wait.** A reformer in office must govern a city he does not control, and the machine can outlast him.
- **Violence** is rare and costly, and is seldom the first or the best tool. When it comes it brings the press and the federal government (Part III 9).

**After a reform win (Tendency).** The reformer discovers that the mayor's office controls the departments and not the ward leaders, that the Council is a fief of fifteen men, that the unions can stop the city, and that the money is gone (section 6). The families do not leave. Prices change. The Pad is reopened, the contracts are re-bid, and the Concrete Club learns to deal with a new Board.

**Anchor [memory, to verify].** The model is real New Jersey politics: Newark's mayor was convicted of extortion in 1970 and Jersey City's mayor and several others in 1971, which are cautionary tales of how far federal prosecutors would go against a machine. Both are offstage news.

### 5.2 What a Professional Is to the Life

**Fixed.** A professional is a man (or, rarely, a woman) who sells skill, respectability and discretion. The families need all three. A made man can be a gunman, a boss or a collector. He cannot be a lawyer in a courtroom, an accountant who signs a return, or a banker who approves a loan, because the world will not believe him.

**Tendency.** The professional stands in one of three relations to a family.

1. **Hired.** He sells his skill for a fee and keeps his independence. Most do, and most are honest in the ordinary sense.
2. **Partnered.** He has a stake in the family's business and shares its risk.
3. **Absorbed.** He started as hired, did a favor, did another, and found one day that he could not leave. This is the way most professionals who go bad go bad, and it takes years.

**The price (Tendency).** A professional's exposure is his license, his name and his liberty. A prosecutor who cannot reach a boss may reach his lawyer or his accountant. A professional who is squeezed has the best chance of any man in the Life to cooperate, and the family knows it.

### 5.3 Lawyers

**Tendency.** Four groups, which overlap.

- **The family's counsel.** A lawyer, usually of the same stock as the family and often from the Hill or Bellacorte, who handles its legitimate business, advises its men, and appears for its soldiers. He is rarely a made man, and a good one never learns more than he must. Sabatini has the best of these (Part III 4.1).
- **The criminal defense bar.** A few dozen lawyers known to every prosecutor and judge. They are paid in cash or by retainer, plead cases and try the ones that must be tried. A defense lawyer's value is whom he knows and how he reads a judge.
- **The machine's lawyers.** County committee counsel, ward lawyers, zoning lawyers, municipal bond counsel, and the lawyers who appear before the Zoning and Licensing Boards. Their value is access (4.7).
- **The downtown firms.** Old Protestant and Jewish firms serving the banks, the insurers, the developers and the Port Authority. Mostly clean, and exposed at the edges: a partner who handles a front's closing, a closing that goes through.

**Others.** Public defenders (the office exists in New Jersey from the late 1960s [memory, to verify]), legal aid, the NAACP's lawyers, and a handful of Black and women attorneys, who are few and conspicuous (Part I 12.1).

**Rules of the trade (Tendency).** A client may tell his lawyer what he did, and the lawyer need not repeat it. The privilege does not cover a crime still being planned [memory]. A lawyer who carries messages for a client in jail (Part III 6.4), who holds money for a client, or who advises a client how to hide an asset has stepped outside it. Fees are often paid in cash. A grand jury can subpoena a lawyer's fee records (Part II 6.4), and federal prosecutors treat a lawyer who works too closely with a family as a target.

**Weather (1984 onward, [memory, to verify]).** New federal law lets prosecutors seize assets used to pay a defense fee. Lawyers who serve the families become targets in racketeering cases through the 1980s.

### 5.4 Accountants

**Tendency.** Three kinds.

- **The large firms.** The big national accounting firms serve the banks, the Port Authority and the steel and chemical companies. They are clean and cautious.
- **The neighborhood CPA.** Keeps the books and prepares the returns of a hundred small cash businesses. Many are honest. Some keep two sets of books for a regular client. He has no incentive to know more than he must.
- **The tax man and the bookkeeper.** A bookkeeper in a bakery, a bar or a club often does the real arithmetic, and she is very often a woman (Part III 11).

**What the accountant knows.** The truth about the business that the return does not say. He is the one professional who can reconstruct a front or a skim (3.5).

**Exposure (Tendency, [memory, to verify]).** A federal court held in 1973 that no accountant-client privilege covers a client's records in the accountant's hands, so a tax preparer can be compelled to give them up. Penalties for preparers of false returns tighten in the mid 1970s. An accountant is therefore a likelier witness than a lawyer.

### 5.5 Bail Bondsmen

**Fixed (Part II 3.2, 6.2).** A defendant who cannot post bail pays a bondsman a premium, and the bondsman posts it. Before 1984 most defendants have a right to release (Part II 6.2), so a bondsman does steady work.

**Tendency.** The premium is on the order of ten percent of the bail [memory, to verify]. The bondsman takes collateral, knows the clerks and the judges, keeps an office near the Civic Center, and hires men to find those who run. He is the family's friend because he gets men out fast, and he is a Pad intermediary because he knows everyone who matters in the courthouse. His risk is a defendant who flees, so he prefers a man with a family and a reason to stay.

**Weather (1984).** The Bail Reform Act (Part II 6.2) lets federal courts detain a dangerous defendant before trial and shrinks the federal bondsman's trade.

### 5.6 Bankers

See 3.7. The professional banker is the branch manager, the loan officer and the trust officer. Many are honest, some are friends of a family, and the one who is neither is often the one who notices.

### 5.7 Developers

**Tendency.** Four kinds.

- **Old-line developers** of the Heights and the downtown towers, whose family names are on the buildings. Clean in their own telling.
- **Italian contractors turned developers**, who built the Hill's row houses and now build downtown. Often tied to the Concrete Club (3.6) and the Italian wing (4.3).
- **Suburban tract builders** in Marsh and Shore counties, who answer to suburban zoning, not the city's. A state court decision of 24 March 1975 against a township's exclusionary zoning changes what suburbs may refuse [verified, date; the holding is [memory]].
- **Community developers** of the Eastside and the Point, who use federal block-grant money (Part I 9.5) and the churches.

**Fixed (Part I 4.5).** "No one else will build here" is a developer's best argument with a desperate mayor. A developer needs zoning, permits, financing, concrete and labor, and can be squeezed at every one.

### 5.8 Insurance and Surety

**Tendency.** Insurance is a business of trust and paper.

- **Brokers and agents.** A broker who lands a union's welfare plan or a contractor's whole book earns commissions for years (2.6). That business is a favor.
- **Surety.** A contractor must be bonded to bid on public work (3.6). A bonding agent who is a friend is a gate.
- **Fire and property.** Decaying buildings in the Eastside and the Flats are underwritten for little and insured for more. Arson for profit is a crime of the period (Part I 7.4, Part III 5.5), and the Fire Marshal (Part II 1.1) and the insurer's own adjusters are its two watchers. - **Marine and cargo.** The port's insurers pay for the cargo that vanishes (Part III 7.5).

### 5.9 Funeral Directors

**Fixed.** In a city of Catholic neighborhoods the funeral director is one of the best-placed men in the city. He knows every family on his block. He is trusted with grief, money and silence. His trade has a high margin (3.2). Everyone, sooner or later, is his customer.

**Tendency.** The funeral home is a bank (for the families that need an advance), a political club (the wake is the machine's town hall, Part III 2.4), a meeting room (Russo and Sons, Part III 6.4) and a place where the dead are put into the hands of a man who asks no question. Funeral directors on the Hill, in Kerrytown and on Dunmore Avenue each serve a community and each keep that community's confidence. A funeral director who betrays a confidence loses his trade. One who is a family's man loses his independence.

**Fixed (Part III 6.4).** A wake is the safest meeting in the city. The Storyteller should treat this as a courtesy the whole city keeps and may break.

### 6.2 The Budget as an Operating Condition

**Fixed.** The city is also the county (Part I 1.1), so its budget carries the police, fire, sanitation, streets, parks, health, the courts' local costs, the hall of records, public works and the schools' construction (Part I 4.3).

**Design (unsourced, Part VI to supply).** Most of an old city's operating budget is payroll and its benefits, on the order of three-quarters of it [memory, rough]. Debt service and pensions take a further share. What is left for services, repairs and capital is small. The total budget, the payroll and the debt are **needed from Part VI**.

**Where the money comes from (Tendency).** The property tax on a shrinking base. State aid. Federal aid (6.7). Fees and fines. Payments from the Port Authority, if any (Open). Borrowing.

**The cash calendar.** The year begins on 1 July (Part I 3.5). Taxes come in on a schedule, and the city's bills do not, so it borrows short-term between collections. A city whose short-term borrowing is refused cannot make payroll. That is what a crisis is (6.4).

**Flag (carried, not changed).** New Jersey municipalities commonly use the calendar year as their fiscal year, and school districts are separate bodies [memory, to verify for both]. Part I sets a July year and places school construction under the Board of Public Works. These are Saint Jude's own choices, and this Part follows them.

### 6.3 The Tax Base

**Tendency.** Property values on the Eastside, the Flats and downtown are soft or falling. Owners appeal their assessments, and each successful appeal shrinks the base. The assessors and the county tax board are a touchpoint (the Governor appoints county tax boards [memory, to verify for 1975]). Abandoned and burned buildings (Part I 7.4) bring no tax. Tax abatements to developers are a bargaining chip with a mayor who needs buildings. The statute and its date are **to verify**.

### 6.4 Debt, Bonds and the Market

**How a city borrows (Tendency, [memory]).** For long-term capital, the city sells **bonds**. For the gap between taxes and bills, it sells **short-term notes** in anticipation of taxes, revenues or bond sales. Rating agencies grade the city, and a low grade raises every rate. **Underwriters** (the banks, Part I 9.6) buy and resell the issue. **Bond counsel** (a law firm) certifies that it is legal. **Financial advisers** help the city price it. Each is a fee, and each fee is a favor. A bond issue is the biggest single piece of patronage the city makes outside its payroll.

**The 1975 model.** New York City's crisis is the national case and Saint Jude's reference. The Municipal Assistance Corporation was created on 10 June 1975. The city came close to default on 17 October. The President announced on 29 October that he would refuse a federal bailout. In December Congress passed a seasonal financing act that lent the city money on conditions, including cuts to its workforce [verified for these dates, from a summary page]. Cities across the Northeast watched, and the bond market's trust in all of them fell.

**Anchor [memory, to verify].** Cleveland defaulted on short-term notes in December 1978. A default is therefore a real event of the period, and Saint Jude's own is Open.

**State oversight (Tendency, [memory, to verify]).** New Jersey supervises local finance through a state division and board that review local budgets and borrowing. A Saint Jude too deep in debt would not be left to default quietly. The State would step in, and its terms would be as bitter as the unions' fear. How far, and when, is **Open**.

**Who profits from the bond business (Tendency).** Underwriters, bond counsel, financial advisers and the banks that hold the paper. In a distressed market, the buyers of short-term notes at high rates. A man who lends to a city workers' union or a contractor who is waiting to be paid, at a street rate (Part III 7.3), earns on the city's late payments.

### 6.5 Layoffs, Seniority and the Unions

**Tendency.** When the city cuts, it cuts by seniority, last in and first out [memory]. The men most recently hired are the youngest and, because of court orders and pressure in the early 1970s (Part II 2.3), a disproportionate share of the new Black and Puerto Rican officers and firefighters. A layoff therefore removes the people the department had just been made to hire. The PBA defends seniority. The Guardians, the Black officers' association, protest. The Mayor and the Council are caught between them, and the quarrel becomes part of ward politics (4.3).

**Fixed (Part II 2.1).** The SJPD may fall from about 4,800 to about 4,300 by 1977. Police and firemen under pay and overtime cuts take the Pad's temptations more seriously, and the union fights loudly.

**Public-employee unions (2.5).** They cannot legally strike, and use the sick-out and the slowdown. A sanitation slowdown that leaves garbage on the street in July is a political weapon the machine understands.

**Weather (1975 to 1977, strong).** Cuts, freezes and layoffs. **Open:** their size and timing.

### 6.6 Contracts Under Austerity

**Tendency.** Less money means fewer contracts and more competition for each. Deferred maintenance (Part I 8.2) makes the contracts that are let into emergencies, and emergencies skip bidding (4.6). A city that cannot pay promptly pays contractors late, and a late-paid subcontractor is a borrower from the street. The Concrete Club's share of a smaller pie matters more to its members. The families look to private work, to the Port Authority and to the suburbs.
