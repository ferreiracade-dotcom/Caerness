# Saint Jude: The Setting Bible

## Part II: The Law

Baseline: the start of 1975. Dated items after that are **Weather**. Section 0 applies to the whole bible, not only to this Part.

## 0. The Period Mind

### 0.1 The Rule

Everyone in Saint Jude thinks, speaks, and judges as a person of 1975 would. The Storyteller writes them that way. It does not judge them by present standards, it does not let a modern attitude leak into a character who would not have it, and it does not steer the story toward what is comfortable now. The story is a story. Narrating a bigoted remark, a killing, or a cruelty is not endorsing it, and playing a man who does these things is not endorsing them either.

This does not make everyone the same. Inside the period, people differ. The rule is about what the *world* takes for granted. Individuals vary within it, and their variation comes from who they are, not from what the narrator would prefer.

### 0.2 What the World Takes For Granted

These are the common attitudes of the time and place. Any given person may hold a harder or softer version.

**Death and violence.** Death is close and familiar. Many men have been to a war. Cops, nurses, firemen, longshoremen, and butchers have seen bodies. Wakes last two or three days, often in the home or a funeral parlor on the block, and children attend. In the underworld a killing is business, discipline, or a message. It is not a trauma. A man may order a death and then bring a mass card and an envelope to the wake, and nobody in his world sees a contradiction. The dead are mourned, the killing is understood, and in some circles vengeance is a duty. Capital punishment is widely accepted. Ordinary people say a rosary for the dead, including the ones they think deserved it.

**The law and the police.** Cops are respected, feared, resented, and related to. In the Irish and Italian neighborhoods the police are cousins and neighbors. In the Eastside they are widely seen as an occupying force. Police brutality is routine, known, and often approved of. Courts are distrusted. Informing is the worst word in the language in most neighborhoods, and testifying against your own people is a disgrace that follows a family for generations. Faith in public institutions is at a low point after Vietnam and Watergate (Anchor: Nixon resigned in August 1974).

**Race and ethnicity.** The city is segregated in fact, by neighborhood, church, union, and club. Open prejudice is normal in speech, hiring, housing, and marriage. Slurs are in ordinary use. Italians, Irish, Jews, Poles, Puerto Ricans, and Black people all give and receive contempt. Marriage across the lines is opposed by most families. Black and white leaders deal with each other with little trust. Individuals differ: some men are decent across the line, some are hostile, many are hostile in public and decent in private. The Storyteller shows all of this as it is.

**Religion.** Catholic practice is ordinary, not special: Mass, confession, saints, holy cards, novenas, blessings, fish on Fridays (the rule has been relaxed but the habit holds). The fear of hell is real. Wiseguys go to confession and mean it. Nobody thinks the combination of faith and crime is hypocrisy in the modern sense. It is a human condition the Church has always known. Protestant, Black Church, and Jewish observance are likewise ordinary and serious.

**Sex, marriage, and gender.** Men run the public world. Women run the home and much of the neighborhood. There is a double standard: men keep mistresses (*comares*) and are expected to; a wife's infidelity is a scandal. Divorce among Catholics is a disgrace and sometimes a bar to the sacraments. Homosexuality is widely despised, hidden, and in the criminal code in some places. Gay men and women exist everywhere, including in the mob, and they live carefully. Domestic violence is treated as private business and the police are reluctant to enter a house. Abortion has been legal nationwide since 1973 (Anchor) and is fiercely opposed in Catholic neighborhoods. The women's movement is real and has not reached most of the city.

**Drink, tobacco, drugs.** Everyone smokes, including in offices, hospitals, and courtrooms. Drinking is the glue of social life, and drunk driving carries little stigma. Heroin is shameful and feared. Marijuana is common among the young. Cocaine in 1975 is an expensive amusement of the rich and of the entertainment world. **(Part VI 6.2 now sets 1975 regular cocaine users at about 2 per 1,000, about 2,200 people, to match this.)** Prescription pills are widespread among housewives and the middle aged.

**Money and debt.** Cash is king. A debt is a shame and a bond. A favor is a currency and is remembered. The loan shark is a known institution, as common as a pawnshop. Gambling is normal. Credit is hard to get without connections, and many families have never had a bank loan.

**Respect.** Respect is a thing a man can lose in public, and a lost respect can demand an answer. An insult to a man, his family, or his name is a debt. Shame is more binding than law.

**Family and children.** Physical discipline of children is normal and expected. Children work young. Sunday dinner is a duty. Elders are obeyed. A man's family is his reputation.

**Illness.** Cancer is not named aloud in many families. Mental illness is hidden. Men who came back from Vietnam are expected to get on with it.

### 0.3 Mixed Men

The old codex sorted men into four types: Rotten Patriarchs, Young Zealots, Old Butchers, and Pragmatic Sharks. **This bible does not use them.** They make men into types, and real men are not types.

**Onore is a bundle of separate obligations.** A man holds each to his own degree and for his own reasons:

1. loyalty to his *compare* and his crew,
2. care for widows and for men in prison,
3. silence before the law,
4. honesty toward other made men,
5. respect for the Boss and for the rituals,
6. his own limits on violence, drugs, women, and children,
7. faith, family, and the Church,
8. his word on a deal.

Most men are strong on some and weak on others. A capo may pay a jailed soldier's wife to the dollar and sell heroin behind his boss's back. A hardened killer may have a rule about women and children. A sentimental old man may sell out a friend to stay out of prison. The same man can be loyal one year and break the next, because the price changes.

**Age does not decide it.** Old men keep the code, and old men sell it. Young men believe it, and young men use it as a tool.

**People change.** A man's stance on each obligation is a state that events move. A strict traditionalist may soften after the code costs him a son, or after prison, or after a sickness. A cynic may return to the old ways after he watches money wreck a family and a crew. Promotion, debt, betrayal, a death, a priest, a wife, a wound, age, fear of dying in a cell: each can move a man in either direction. Nobody is fixed. The simulation should let people drift and surprise, and the Storyteller should remember that a man you trusted last year may not be the same man.

**Nobody is wholly good or wholly bad.** A man the reader hates may keep faith in one thing no one else keeps. A man the reader admires may do something unforgivable. The reader should be able to back a man for a time and turn on him later, or the reverse.

**The same applies outside the families.** Cops, agents, prosecutors, priests, union men, politicians, and businessmen are all mixed in this way.

## 1. Who Enforces the Law, and Who Fights Whom

### 1.1 The Map

Law enforcement in Saint Jude is many agencies, not one. They overlap, compete, distrust each other, and share information badly. This is a feature of the world, and it is why the underworld survives.

**Local.** The **SJPD** (the city police); the **Port Authority Police**; the **Harbor Police**; the **Transit Police**; the **Oakridge Borough Police** and other suburban departments; the **County Sheriff** of Marsh County and the others; the **Fire Marshal** (arson); and the **Killbuck County Prosecutor** and the suburban county prosecutors.

**State.** The **State Police**; the **State Attorney General** and his Organized Crime and Racketeering Section; the **State Commission of Investigation** (the real New Jersey body, **created in 1968, working from 1 January 1969** [verified]: a body with subpoena power that can hold public hearings but cannot prosecute); the **Waterfront Commission**; the **ABC Board** (liquor); the **Racing Commission**; and the **Lottery Commission**.

**Federal.** The **FBI** field office under a Special Agent in Charge; the **U.S. Attorney** and his Assistant U.S. Attorneys; the **Organized Crime and Racketeering Strike Force** (prosecutors from the Justice Department's Criminal Division, with investigators attached from several agencies); the **DEA**; the **ATF**; the **IRS Intelligence Division**; the **Customs Service**; the **U.S. Marshals**; the **Postal Inspectors**; and the **Labor Department** (union and pension audits).

### 1.2 Tendencies

**Tendency.** Agencies guard their cases and their sources. The FBI does not share informants with the SJPD, and the SJPD does not share its files with the State Police. The Strike Force and the U.S. Attorney's office compete over the same cases (Anchor: federal reviewers criticized the Strike Force program in the late 1970s for rivalry and weak results). The DEA and the FBI quarrel over drugs. Prosecutors want convictions, agents want cases, cops want a quiet street, and each answers to a different boss. A criminal who understands which agency wants what can use the gaps. A prosecutor who forgets can lose a year's work to a rival.

### 1.3 The Honest and the Corrupt Are Both Everywhere

**Fixed.** Corruption is a tendency, not an absolute. Every agency contains honest men, compromised men, ambitious men, lazy men, and men who are all of these at different times. The local police are *more* exposed to the neighborhood and the Pad. Federal agents are *less* exposed, and are still human (Anchor: FBI agents have been corrupted by informants, including in Boston from 1975 **[memory]**). A bribe or a friendly judge changes the odds. It never decides the outcome.

## 2. The Saint Jude Police Department

### 2.1 Size and Structure

**Design (range 4,200 to 5,400).** The SJPD has about **4,800 sworn officers** at the start of 1975, in 24 precincts and a set of citywide bureaus. It is led by a **Police Commissioner** appointed by the Mayor. Beneath him are a Chief of Department (the senior uniformed officer), a Chief of Detectives, and deputy commissioners. Ranks run: patrolman, detective, sergeant, lieutenant, captain, inspector, deputy chief, chief.

**Weather, strong (1975 to 1977).** The city's money crisis forces a hiring freeze and, in the worst version, layoffs. The force may fall to about 4,300. Overtime is cut. Morale drops, the union rages, and hungry cops look at the Pad.

### 2.2 Pay and Life

**Design (start $10,500 to $12,500; after three years $13,500 to $15,500).** A patrolman earns about **$11,500 to start and $14,500 after three years**. A sergeant earns about $17,500. A lieutenant about $20,000. A detective $17,000 to $19,000. A captain $24,000. The Commissioner $38,000. **Part VI 7.3 uses these figures.** Cops moonlight, as security guards, bouncers, drivers, and bar staff. Pensions after twenty years are a good reason to stay and a good reason not to rock the boat. Divorce, drink, and heart attacks are common. Many cops retire to Florida.

**The union.** The **Saint Jude Patrolmen's Benevolent Association** is powerful, defensive, and run by men who came up through the ranks. It defends officers in discipline cases and is hostile to civilian oversight and to Internal Affairs. Sergeants, detectives, and lieutenants have their own associations.

### 2.3 Who the Cops Are

**Fixed.** The force is overwhelmingly white and male in 1975, and overwhelmingly Irish and Italian in the Catholic parishes that supply it: the SJPD is the Irish Church's secular arm and the Italians' next stop after the building trades. Black officers are a small and growing share, pushed by court orders and the **Guardians**, a Black officers' association. Women patrol officers have served since 1973 (Anchor: national trend) and are under 2 percent of the force, kept out of the units that matter. Whatever the force's official color, its culture is that of the Catholic working class.

### 2.4 The Precincts

**Fixed.** The **14th Precinct** sits at **Mercer and Ninth** in a soot-stained 1920s stationhouse, and covers the Strip. It is the best-known precinct in the city. It is **not** the whole department. Other notable precincts:

| Precinct | Covers | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 2nd | The Heights | quiet, political, old money |
| 3rd | North San Lorenzo | Italian, closed-mouthed, a precinct of cousins |
| 4th | South Hill | working class, Pellegrino loyalists |
| 6th | Bellacorte quarter | polite, small |
| 8th | Crown Harbor | the Civic Center; brass and politics |
| 9th | The Point, North Piers | docks, the fish market |
| 11th | Kerrytown | Irish; the cathedral; the Brigades |
| 14th | Mercer Avenue | vice, nightclubs, the Pad |
| 15th | The Racks | garment, freight, loft burglaries |
| 17th | Dunmore Avenue | Black; strained relations with the force |
| 19th | The Eastside housing | high crime, understaffed |
| 21st | Brighton Point | quiet, old, a new language to learn |
| 23rd | The Flats | industrial, empty at night, bodies |

The other precincts exist and are **Open**. **The 3rd and 4th precincts split the district Part I calls San Lorenzo, the Hill: "North San Lorenzo" and "South Hill" are its two halves, and the boundary between them is Open. The "Eastside housing" of the 19th is the public housing Part I places in the Eastside.**

### 2.5 The Citywide Units

**Fixed.**

- **The Detective Bureau.** Precinct detective squads handle ordinary crime. The **Homicide Squad** works out of headquarters, takes the murders, and is proud, overworked, and politically sensitive.
- **The Organized Crime Control Division** (everyone calls it "the Mob Squad") gathers intelligence on the families, keeps photo files and charts of who sits with whom, and works with the FBI and the Strike Force when it chooses to. Small, wary, and not always trusted by the rest of the department.
- **The Public Morals Division** (Vice) enforces the gambling, prostitution, and liquor laws. After the Dolan Commission its old plainclothes squads were broken up and reformed. It is where much of the department's remaining gambling corruption lives.
- **The Narcotics Division.** Street drug enforcement. Under pressure from federal agencies and from the Mayor.
- **Internal Affairs Division** (IAD), with small field units in the precincts. Hated by the rank and file. Honest officers fear it, and corrupt officers fear it more.
- **Emergency Service, the Harbor Unit, the Crime Laboratory, Youth Aid,** and traffic.

### 2.6 What the Dolan Commission Left Behind

**Fixed.** Between 1970 and 1972 the mayor's commission found that gambling payoffs in the plainclothes squads were routine, organized, and paid weekly by bookmakers and numbers banks to cops who shared the money by rank. It sorted the department into "**grass-eaters**" (who take small gratuities, free meals, and a little from bar owners, and do not hunt for more) and "**meat-eaters**" (who seek out payoffs, extort bookies and dealers, and sometimes rob them). The commission's hearings put several officers in prison, and the Commissioner resigned.

The reforms were real. Plainclothes gambling squads were broken up. Cops were rotated through precincts so that no man stayed long enough to build a network. IAD got field units. Commanders were held responsible for corruption in their commands. Promotion for informing on a colleague was talked about, and a few were rewarded.

**Tendency.** The reforms changed how the Pad works and did not end it. See section 3.

**Open.** Which officers were convicted, who the commission's lawyer was, which reforms have already faded.

### 2.7 The Ordinary Department

**Fixed.** Most of what the SJPD does has nothing to do with the mob. Patrol, traffic, domestic calls, bar fights, burglaries, car thefts, drunks, accidents, junkies, missing children, noise complaints. A day-shift patrolman on the Hill and a night-shift patrolman on the Eastside live in different worlds. The majority of cops are neither saints nor villains. They are men trying to get through the week, pay the mortgage, and avoid trouble.

## 3. The Pad

### 3.1 What It Is

**Fixed.** "The Pad" is the system of regular payments by illegal operators to police, in return for protection from enforcement. The word comes from the list on which the payments are recorded. A **pad** may be a weekly or monthly fixed sum, paid by a bookmaker, a numbers bank, a bar that stays open late, a brothel, or an after-hours club. It is the cost of doing business, like rent.

### 3.2 How It Works After the Dolan Commission

**Tendency.** The Pad survives the reforms by changing shape.

- **It moves up and out.** Fewer cops touch the money directly. Payments are smaller in number and larger in size, and are routed through an **intermediary**: a bail bondsman, a lawyer, a bar owner, a retired cop, a union man. Cops fear that any collector may be an IAD plant.
- **Rotation weakens loyalty.** Because officers are moved every few years, no man holds a precinct long. A relationship built over a decade can be lost in a month. The rule among careful bookmakers is: pay the *position*, not the man.
- **Fewer men are in it, and they are more careful.** A lieutenant who survived the Dolan years may have done so by never touching the money himself. Fear has taught the department discretion.
- **Grass is still everywhere.** Free meals, free drinks, a cut-rate suit, a Christmas envelope, a "gift" from a grateful shopkeeper. This is not seen as corruption by most of the force.
- **Meat is rarer and nastier.** The men who actively hunt payoffs are fewer after Dolan, and the ones who remain are dangerous.

### 3.3 What the Pad Buys

**Tendency.** The Pad purchases limits, not immunity.

- **Warning of a raid** (a phone call that rings twice and stops, or a word passed through the intermediary).
- **Soft treatment**: a patrol car that does not stop, a Vice team that raids on a quiet night.
- **Lost paper**: a missing report, a mistake on a warrant, an unsigned evidence log that helps the defense get a suppression order.
- **Information**: who is being watched, who is asking questions.
- **A blind eye** to a late-night bar, a floating game, a shipment moved at 4 a.m.

### 3.4 What the Pad Cannot Buy

**Fixed.**

- It cannot make a public killing go away. A body in front of witnesses, a bystander hit, a front-page story will force an arrest regardless of who is on the Pad.
- It does not reach the FBI, the Strike Force, or the State Attorney General.
- It does not control the Homicide Squad's honest men, the Medical Examiner, or IAD.
- It does not cover an officer who is killed. A cop killing brings every officer in the city and every agency above them.
- It does not bind a new commander. When a captain is replaced, the arrangement is reopened.

### 3.5 The Human Side

**Tendency.** A cop on the Pad is not simply a crook. He may be a man with a sick child, a second mortgage, and a boss who takes more than he does. He may be a man who takes from a bookie and would never take from a dealer. He may take for twenty years and then turn on the men who paid him, out of fear or conscience. Most officers who take are mixed in exactly the way this bible says all men are mixed (section 0.3).

**Example.** A Vice sergeant who has not touched an envelope in five years and has a friend who does. A patrol captain who knows exactly what happens in his precinct and has decided not to know. A young detective who refuses a Christmas envelope and learns that it costs him a partner.

## 4. Other Local and State Agencies

### 4.1 Port Authority Police

**Fixed.** About 600 officers under the Port Authority's own chain of command (section 8.1 of Part I). They police the bridge, the tunnel, the airport, and the terminals. They are underpaid and politically appointed, and the Authority's patronage makes them a source of jobs for the machine. They have their own corruption, quieter than the SJPD's, tied to cargo theft and the unions at the terminals.

### 4.2 Harbor Police and Customs

**Fixed.** The Harbor Police patrol the Basin and the Narrows. The **Customs Service** inspects imports at the Gannet Terminal and the piers and is the first federal agency to see what comes in. A sealed container can be opened by Customs, or can pass on a stamp. A Customs inspector who looks away is worth a great deal.

### 4.3 Suburban Departments

**Fixed.** Every suburb has a police department and a prosecutor. They are small, local, and close to their voters. **Oakridge's** twenty-odd officers know the bosses who live there by name and by car. When something serious happens in Oakridge the Borough Police turn to the Marsh County Prosecutor, the State Police, or the FBI, and everyone involved understands that the quiet is over.

### 4.4 The State Police

**Fixed.** A professional force with a reputation for being cleaner than the city police. It patrols the highways, the Pines, and the smaller counties, and supports local departments in serious cases. Its detectives work gambling, auto theft, and drug cases. Its organized crime unit is small and sometimes well informed.

### 4.5 The State Commission of Investigation

**Fixed.** A standing commission of appointed members with the power to subpoena witnesses and records, hold public hearings, and publish reports. It cannot prosecute, but it can embarrass. Its hearings are the closest thing the state has to the Senate crime hearings, and a witness who pleads the Fifth in public is a story.

### 4.6 Regulatory Bodies

**Fixed.** The **Waterfront Commission** registers longshoremen, checkers, and pier workers, and can strike a man from the rolls. The **ABC Board** licenses every bar and can revoke a license for tax fraud or gambling. The **Racing Commission** governs Bayview Downs and licenses trainers, jockeys, and owners. The **Lottery Commission** runs the state game. Each is a prize to capture and a place where an honest investigator can do real damage.

## 5. The Federal Presence

### 5.1 The FBI Field Office

**Fixed.** The Saint Jude field office is run by a **Special Agent in Charge** (SAC). Under him are Assistant Special Agents in Charge and squads by subject: organized crime, foreign counterintelligence, bank robbery and fugitives, white-collar, civil rights, and so on. Agents are mostly white, male, Catholic or Protestant, former military or lawyers or accountants, and proudly clean. The Bureau after Hoover (Anchor: Hoover died in May 1972) is in a long hangover of scandal and reform, and its leadership is trying to prove it can work cases against organized crime and political corruption, not only radicals.

**Tendency.** Agents are proud, careful, and territorial. They do not talk to local cops about their informants. They trust their own paper. Some are brilliant and patient. Some are careerists. Some run informants too close and cross lines that they cannot see crossing. The Bureau as an institution says it is incorruptible, and its individual agents are mixed like everyone else (Anchor [memory]: in Boston from 1975 an agent began a long and corrupting relationship with an informant).

### 5.2 The Strike Force

**Fixed [verified in outline].** The **Organized Crime and Racketeering Strike Force** is a Justice Department program of prosecutors, placed in the field, who build cases against organized crime with investigators from the FBI, IRS, DEA, ATF, Customs, and Labor attached to it. It is prosecutor-led. It is not an FBI unit, and nobody should call it one.

**Tendency.** It competes with the U.S. Attorney's office over credit and cases, and its attorneys are often younger, more aggressive, and more willing to try new theories than the old hands. Federal reviewers in the late 1970s criticized the program for rivalry and uneven results (Weather below).

### 5.3 The U.S. Attorney

**Fixed.** A presidential appointee, usually political, who runs the Assistant U.S. Attorneys. The office tries federal crimes in the district, including bank fraud, bribery, narcotics, tax, and racketeering. A U.S. Attorney with ambitions is a danger to the machine, and a U.S. Attorney with a patron is a friend of it.

### 5.4 The Other Federal Agencies

**Fixed.**

- **DEA** (formed 1 July 1973 [verified]) works heroin and cocaine cases and is the rival of the FBI over drugs.
- **ATF** works guns, explosives, arson, and untaxed liquor and cigarettes. It is the agency most likely to catch a man with a bad pistol.
- **IRS Intelligence Division** builds tax cases. It is patient, thorough, and dangerous to anyone who spends more than he reports. Tax evasion is how many men of the old generation went down.
- **Customs** inspects what crosses the border and the docks.
- **U.S. Marshals** guard courts, move prisoners, and from 1971 run the witness security program [verified].
- **Postal Inspectors** work mail fraud and stolen mail, and have a long memory.
- **Labor Department** auditors examine union books and pension funds under ERISA (signed 2 September 1974, Public Law 93-406 **[verified, Part VI pass]**).

### 5.5 The Federal Courts

**Fixed.** The federal district court sits in a downtown courthouse. Federal judges are appointed for life and mostly decline to be bought, though a friend of a friend can be useful for a lenient date. Federal juries are drawn from a wider area and are less likely to be the defendant's neighbors than a county jury. Federal sentences for racketeering and narcotics are heavy by the standards of the time and heavier after 1984.

## 6. Law and Procedure

Everything here is dated. A rule changes when its date arrives, and **the Storyteller must not apply a later rule earlier.** Tags: [verified] means checked against sources, [memory] means from general knowledge and still to be checked.

### 6.1 Arrest, Questioning, and Counsel

**Fixed [memory].** Miranda warnings (1966) are in force. A suspect may refuse to talk and may have a lawyer. Wiseguys know this by heart: the standard policy is to say nothing, "ask for your lawyer," and keep walking. A man who talks to the police without counsel is considered a fool or an informant. Cops and detectives know all the older methods of pressure and use them where they can: the long wait, the friendly cop and the hard cop, the threat to the wife, the offer to deal. Roughing up a prisoner is more tolerated than it will later be, and is rarely punished.

### 6.2 Bail

**Fixed [verified].** Before the Bail Reform Act of 1984, federal defendants in non-capital cases had a statutory right to release on bail unless a judge believed they would flee; dangerousness was not a ground for detention. Most mobsters therefore walk out after arraignment on a bond, and often on property. State rules are similar. The bondsman is an institution and often a friend of the families.

**Weather (1984).** The 1984 act allows detention before trial for dangerousness, and the Supreme Court upholds it in 1987 [verified]. Before 1984, do not hold a boss without bail on the strength of his reputation.

### 6.3 Search Warrants and Wiretaps

**Fixed.** A search needs a warrant from a judge on probable cause, with exceptions. Cops without one find ways: a pretext stop, a "consent," a lie in the affidavit. Evidence found by an illegal search can be suppressed in court. A bribed or sloppy cop can make the difference at a suppression hearing.

**Fixed [verified].** The federal wiretap statute (Title III, 1968) permits electronic eavesdropping only on a judge's order, for a limited set of serious crimes, for limited periods (30 days, renewable), with a duty to "minimize" listening to irrelevant calls. Federal applications need a Justice Department sign-off before they go to the judge. Agents prefer to say that "bugs" are placed by "surreptitious entry," which is a burglary with a warrant. Illegal bugs and taps (the "black bag" jobs of earlier years) are discredited after the Church hearings and are now rarer and more dangerous to a case.

**Fixed [verified].** An informant may carry a transmitter or recorder, and agents may testify to what they hear, without a warrant (*United States v. White*, decided 5 April 1971, six to three). An informant with a body wire is therefore legal in federal court, and is the main way a family is broken from within. State law may differ. New Jersey's wiretap act (1968) **allows recording with the consent of one party to the conversation (N.J.S.A. 2A:156A-4) [verified in outline, Part VI pass]**.

**Fixed [verified].** A pen register (a record of numbers dialed) needs no warrant after *Smith v. Maryland* (June 1979). Before then its status is murkier. Phone records can otherwise be had by subpoena.

**Tendency.** Mob men know the phones are listened to. They speak in code, use pay phones, meet in parks, walk and talk in motion, and write on paper that goes into the toilet or the oven. The talk of bugs is a daily fear. Careful bosses leave the house to speak.

### 6.4 Grand Juries, Immunity, and Contempt

**Fixed [memory].** The grand jury hears witnesses in secret and issues indictments. A witness may plead the Fifth Amendment unless granted immunity, and a witness who refuses to testify after immunity can be jailed for civil contempt until he talks, until the grand jury's term ends, or for 18 months at most, whichever comes first **[verified, 28 U.S.C. 1826, Part VI pass]**. Mobsters often prefer a year in jail to testifying, and are regarded with respect when they do it. A man who takes immunity and then talks is feared.

### 6.5 Plea Bargaining and "Reaching the Box"

**Fixed.** Most cases end in a plea. Defense lawyers bargain a lesser charge and a short sentence, and the best lawyers are known to the prosecutors on both sides. A made man typically refuses to plead if it requires naming others. A man who "takes the box" (testifies for the government) in 1975 is a rarity.

### 6.6 RICO

**Fixed [verified].** The RICO statute was enacted in October 1970, but it was used little in its early years: one secondary account says it "remained largely unused until 1975" and was used about 200 times by the late 1970s. **RICO convictions came early but were few. In *United States v. Parness* a jury convicted under the statute in October 1973, affirmed in 1974 [verified, 503 F.2d 430 (2d Cir.)]. A case against a New York longshoremen's union leader (*United States v. Scotto*) was brought in the Southern District of New York in September 1979 [verified in outline, a legal-history account, Part VI pass]. Use against Mafia leadership came later.** The first major Mafia leadership case is the Commission case, **indicted on 25 February 1985 and tried in 1986, with verdicts on 19 November 1986 [verified, Part VI pass]**. Before the late 1970s prosecutors prefer older tools: gambling, extortion, tax evasion, narcotics conspiracy, Hobbs Act, interstate theft, labor racketeering.

**Weather (late 1970s to 1985).** RICO moves from the law books to the front line. A prosecutor who can show an "enterprise" and a "pattern" can charge an entire family for twenty years of crime in a single indictment, with forfeiture and long sentences.

### 6.7 Witness Protection

**Fixed [verified].** WITSEC is authorized in 1970 and begins in 1971, run by the U.S. Marshals. A man who flips can be moved with a new name. The program is real in 1975 and is a strong inducement to a man facing long prison, though it is untested in a mob context at scale until the late 1970s.

### 6.8 Forensics

**Fixed.** The tools of 1975: fingerprints, ballistics, blood typing, hair and fiber comparison, handwriting, tool marks, photographs, autopsies, and the Medical Examiner's findings. There is **no DNA** testing [memory: forensic DNA profiling arrives in 1986 and reaches courts from 1987]. Evidence is gathered with wide variation in care. Contamination, lost evidence, and sloppy crime scenes are common. A body in water or a fire can destroy much. A clean killing with a stolen gun, a plain car, gloves, and no witnesses can go unsolved forever, and many do. The Medical Examiner's office is a respectable institution with a political head.

## 7. Informants, Cooperators, and Undercover Agents

### 7.1 Informants

**Tendency.** Every agency has informants: junkies, bar owners, bookmakers, wives, cousins, small crooks with a case. They give cops and agents tips, and rarely much more. A soldier or associate who is arrested and frightened can talk. The risk of being killed for informing is real, the world's culture treats it as the worst crime, and so informants are rarely the made men who see everything.

**Fixed [verified, Part VI pass].** The FBI's Top Echelon Criminal Informant Program began in 1961, when Hoover told every Special Agent in Charge to develop live sources "within the upper echelon of the organized hoodlum element". It ran until 1978, when the Bureau replaced it with a general Criminal Informant Program. The first Attorney General guidelines on informants came in 1976, and suitability reviews follow from them. In 1975 a Saint Jude agent may therefore have a source near the top, and the Bureau will not share him (1.2).

**Fixed [memory].** The government has had a few big cooperators, most famously Joseph Valachi (1963). Their testimony revealed the existence of the Cosa Nostra to the public but did not by itself break the families.

**Weather (1977 to 1985).** Cooperation by high-ranking men becomes more frequent. A West Coast acting boss turns informant in 1977 and enters WITSEC in 1980 [verified]. By the mid-1980s the Omertà that held the families is visibly weakening, and prosecutors have learned how to build a case from the inside. This should feel like a slow change in climate, not a switch.

### 7.2 Undercover Agents

**Fixed [verified].** The FBI had avoided undercover work among the Mafia under Hoover. After he dies, the Bureau begins to use it. A famous operation puts an agent inside a New York family from September 1976 to July 1981 [verified]. In Saint Jude this is **Weather**: such an operation, if it occurs, begins no earlier than 1976, and the Storyteller should treat it as rare and hard. A man must be proposed and vouched for, he must show he can "earn," and the family's suspicion of outsiders is at its keenest.

### 7.3 The Honest Question of the Rat

**Tendency.** When a man is suspected of informing, the family does not usually act on suspicion alone. It watches, tests, and sometimes feeds him false information to see where it goes. A wrongful killing of a loyal man for suspicion is a tragedy that happens, and it can break a crew.

## 8. Prisons and the Courts' Aftermath

**Fixed.** The prison system has several levels. The county jail holds the unsentenced and short sentences; it is dirty, overcrowded, and dangerous. The federal detention center downtown holds federal defendants awaiting trial (name **Open**). State prison (the **Ironhaven** complex upstate) holds state felons for long terms. Federal prisoners are sent to penitentiaries and lower-security camps in other states (Lewisburg, Atlanta, Leavenworth, Danbury, and others). **Opening years [verified, Part VI pass]: Atlanta 1902, Leavenworth 1903, Lewisburg 1932, Danbury 1940, Marion 1963 (built to replace Alcatraz). Lewisburg was criticized by a federal judge in 1976 for inmate violence, so it can be written as a rough place in 1975. Marion is a penitentiary in 1975; its maximum-security area is described by 1979 and its supermax-style lockdown dates from 1983, so do not write it as the end of the line before 1978. Danbury held both men and women in the 1970s and is a lower-security place; its exact security class in 1975 was not found. Which mobster does time where is Open.**

**Tendency.** Prison is part of life for many in the underworld. A made man doing time expects his family looked after (obligation 2 in section 0.3), expects deference from other prisoners, and often finds that old and new friends are there. Prison hierarchy follows race and neighborhood. Mob men live relatively well inside if they have money. Time inside can cost a man his place outside, because somebody else takes his job and his money. It can also make his name.

**Fixed.** Parole is available in many cases. A **Parole Commission** reorganized in May 1976 [verified] sets release dates by guideline. A man out on parole can be sent back for associating with criminals. This is a real constraint on mobsters, who are forbidden by their parole terms to meet each other.

**Open.** Capital punishment in the state. The U.S. Supreme Court suspended executions in 1972 and allowed new statutes in 1976 [memory]. **[verified, Part VI pass] New Jersey's death penalty was under a de facto ban from 1972, was reinstated in 1982, and was abolished on 17 December 2007. That no one has been executed since 1963 is now [verified]: Ralph Hudson, executed on 22 January 1963, was the last.** The five-year run may give a reason to treat it differently. Mob killings are overwhelmingly prosecuted as ordinary murder.
