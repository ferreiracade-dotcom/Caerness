**Draft status.** Complete draft, sections 0 to 12. **Bold marks edits made after Part VI was drafted (9.1, 9.2 and 12.7).** Parts I, II and III were amended in the project to fit it (section 1).

## 0. How to Read This Part

### 0.1 What the Outside Groups Are

**Fixed.** The outside groups are the criminal worlds of Saint Jude that are not the four Italian families. Some are old and rooted (the Irish of Kerrytown, the policy men of Dunmore Avenue). Some are small and new (the Russian handful in Brighton Point). Some are trades more than organizations (the Jewish dealers and financiers of Hunts Row). None is a made man. None is bound by the Italian oath. Each holds its own code, its own loyalties and its own grievances, and each meets the families at points of need.

This Part describes them as institutions in early 1975. It does not decide what any man in them will do.

### 0.2 Conventions

Tags follow Parts I to IV: **Fixed**, **Open**, **Weather** (dated, with strength), **Anchor**, **Tendency**, **Design**, **Example**. A real fact is **[verified]** only if checked against a source in this drafting session. **This Part was written from memory, so a real-world item is [memory] and carries "to verify" unless Part VI or the Index marks it verified. All of them are collected in section 12.6.**

**Design.** Dollar figures are 1975 dollars, net not gross (Part III 7.1), and unsourced. **Part VI gives the price book. This Part gives only rough figures; 12.7 lists what Part VI was asked to supply.**

**Named men.** Every name is an **Example**. The engine may change or drop it.

**Real people and organizations.** Real people appear only as history and offstage news. Real organizations (the Provisional IRA, a national motorcycle club, a Soviet-era emigration, a federal agency) are offstage context, tagged [memory]. A real club's real internal events are never Saint Jude history. The Saint Jude groups are stand-ins and are not copies.

### 0.3 What This Part Will Not Do

It does not write a price book (Part VI) or the narration rules. It gives no operational detail on making, moving or hiding drugs, weapons or explosives, and none on the arms trade to Ireland or any attack. Part I 0.5 principle 7 governs. It invents no named secret, conspiracy or plot hook. It does not write the 1980 state. The run decides it.

### 0.4 A Gap in the Source Material

**Flag.** The handoff asks that this Part keep to "the codex's old narcotics material." That material is not in the project. The only narcotics rules in the four attached Parts are Part III 7.6 (the families' contradictory attitude) and Part III 9 (the taboo row for dealing where a family forbids it). This Part keeps to those. If the codex has more, it should be compared against section 9 before the Part is locked.

## 2. The Common Frame

### 2.1 What Makes a Group "Outside"

**Fixed.** An outside group has four things the Italian families have in some other form: a **base** (a neighborhood, a pub, a club, a church), a **trade** (the thing it earns on), a **code** (what its men owe each other) and **a relation to the families**. It lacks a made man's oath, the Ladder (Part III 3.1) and a seat at a sit-down. When a boss wants to talk to one, he sends a go-between, or he asks a man he respects.

### 2.2 How Onore Applies to an Outside Group

Part II 0.3 and Part III 10 treat Onore as eight separate obligations, none of which a man must keep all of. The outside groups hold some and not others. The table is a Tendency, and individuals differ.

| Obligation (Part III 10) | Irish (Brigades) | Black underworld | Russians | Bikers | Jewish specialists | Chinatown |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silence before the law | strong, tied to the parish and the cause | strong, tied to the block | weak in the handful; fear of police is strong | strong inside the club, weak outside | strong, tied to the lawyer | very strong |
| Respect for the elder | strong | strong (the older policy man) | weak (no elders yet) | club rank, not age | strong | very strong |
| Keeping one's word | strong among themselves | strong to the church and the block | uncertain | within the club only | the whole basis of the trade | very strong |
| Family before the group | strong | strong | strong (kin are the only trust) | the club is the family | strong | strong |
| Honor in the neighborhood | strong, a rule against hurting the parish | strong | none in 1975 | none | strong | strong |
| Vengeance for blood | strong and long | strong | uncertain | strong inside the club | rare | by association rule |
| Deference to the stronger power | grudging | grudging | high (a stranger in a strange town) | resented | high | by arrangement |
| One's own limits | varies | varies | varies | varies | varies | varies |

**Tendency.** The Storyteller uses the table to make each group read as itself. An Irishman will not cooperate with a cop for a reason an Italian would. A Black policy man will not break the church's peace for a reason a biker would. A Russian will not trust a stranger for a reason no one else would.

### 2.3 The Gold Coast Sanctuary and the Outside Groups

**Fixed (Part III 6.4).** The Oakridge sanctuary is a convention among the four bosses, backed by self-interest and by a borough's own police.

**Tendency.** It binds an outside group only through the family that can hurt it. A biker or a Russian who brings gunfire to Oakridge answers to the four bosses together, and the bosses' revenge is the sanction, because the group has no seat at the convention. The Brigades respect it out of old courtesy and out of fear of the heat. Chinatown has no business there. The Black underworld's leaders live in a few Eastside and Fenwick streets and have their own sanctuary habit (the church, the funeral). **Open:** whether the outside groups have a sanctuary of their own.

### 2.4 The Go-Betweens

**Tendency.** A family reaches an outside group through a man both sides can stand. The usual types are a lawyer, a funeral director (Part IV 5.9), a bar owner, a retired cop or a ward leader (Part IV 4.2), and, for the Black underworld, a pastor. A go-between is paid in favor, not money. He is also the first man a prosecutor leans on.

## 3. The Charlestown Brigades

### 3.1 The Institution

**Fixed (Part III 5.1).** The Brigades are the Irish underworld of Kerrytown and the South Piers. The **Harp and Crown**, a pub, is the headquarters. They are older in the city than the Italians by a generation and proud of it. Their power rests on the south-bank docks (Local 1201, Part IV 2.7B), tugboat yards, the South Pier crane crews, cargo theft and a share of the machine's old Irish wing (Part IV 4.3).

**Tendency.** "The Brigades" is an outsider's word, and it is more a set of loyalties than a roster. The parish, the pub, the firehouse, the union hall and the family are the real ties. A man is "with the Harp and Crown" because his father was. Nobody is inducted. The Storyteller should keep the lines blurred, with older men who give counsel, middle-aged men who hold a crane crew or a union post, and younger men who do the work.

### 3.2 The Trade

**Tendency.** In order of weight:

- **The south-bank docks.** Hiring in Local 1201's list, pilferage on the South Piers, no-show jobs, and the favors that go with a job at the hall. The decline of the docks (Part I 9.2) cuts all of it.
- **Tugs and lighterage.** The old yard families run the tugs. The cross-river part is the 50/50 with the Pellegrinos (section 1, #1).
- **Cargo theft.** Warehouse and truck-yard theft on the South Flats side of the river. The Ferrante family's trucking and fence meet it at the road (Part III 4.3).
- **Union posts.** Seats in several locals and in the firemen's and cops' associations (Part IV 2.7J, with the limit that no family controls the police or fire unions and a single officer may be compromised).
- **Gambling and the loans of the pub.** A small, old and local bookmaking and shylock business for the parish.
- **The cause.** Money for Ireland (3.5).

**Open.** The size of each branch of the trade, and who holds each.

### 3.3 The 50/50 and Its Strain

**Fixed (section 1, #1; Part III 5.1; Part IV 2.7B).** The old 50/50 arrangement with the Pellegrinos covers cross-river items only. It was made by older men on both sides and is honored as a promise between the old men, though neither side's young men like it.

**Fixed (Part I 2.5, Part IV 2.7B).** In 1973 Pellegrino crews broke an independent longshoremen's strike on the South Piers. A man was killed whose brother is still in Kerrytown. The arrangement is strained by it.

**Tendency.** The strain is a pressure, not a war. The Irish old guard want to keep the deal because the docks are shrinking and they cannot afford a fight. The younger men want a reckoning for the dead man. The Pellegrino old guard want the deal for the same reason as the Irish. A Pellegrino hothead sees an Irish debt to collect. Each side watches the other's funerals and weddings for a slight (Part III 6.5).

**Open.** Whether the dead man's brother is a man of the Harp and Crown or a man outside it. Whether Local 1201's officers knew of the strike beforehand (Part IV 2.7B). Who holds the Gannet Terminal, and whether the 50/50 is stretched to cover it.

### 3.4 Local 1201, the Firehouses and the Machine

**Tendency.** The Brigades touch the upperworld through three doors. **Local 1201** (Part IV 2.7B) is the nearest. The **Irish wing of the machine** (Part IV 4.3) is the second, because the old guard of that wing came from the same Kerrytown wards and parishes. The **firehouses and the Kerrytown Firemen's Association** (Part I 6.1, Part IV 2.7J) are the third. None of the three is "owned." Each is a place where Kerrytown men sit and where Kerrytown loyalties run.

**Tendency.** The Irish old guard in the machine is shrinking, and its men are anxious about the Italians and the Black wing. A Brigades man who also sits in a ward club is a common figure, and one who is both is torn when the machine's interest and the Harp and Crown's interest diverge.

### 3.5 The Troubles, at Institution Level

**Anchor [memory, to verify].** The conflict in Northern Ireland is at a high pitch in the 1970s. The Provisional IRA is the leading republican armed group, and American support for the cause runs partly through sympathetic civic and fund-raising bodies. The FBI and the Irish and British governments follow it. All of this is offstage.

**Tendency.** In Saint Jude the cause is a real thing to some Kerrytown families and a tavern slogan to others. It gives the Brigades a set of loyalties the Italians lack and a federal interest the Italians do not draw. Money, favors and shipping contacts move on the strength of those loyalties. The bible gives no more than this. It does not describe how anything moves, and it does not describe any attack.

**Tendency.** The cause cuts three ways inside the Brigades. Some men give out of belief. Some use it as a cover for profit. Some want nothing to do with it because they fear the federal heat on the docks. The Storyteller shows all three.

**Weather (1975 to 1990, moderate to strong, [memory, to verify]).** Federal attention to the American side of the conflict grows through the late 1970s and 1980s, with prosecutions and seizures reported as offstage news. The 1981 republican hunger strikes in Northern Ireland are an offstage event that may press the Kerrytown mood (strong, but only as mood). Any Saint Jude event is the run's to decide.

### 3.6 How the Brigades Meet the Families

**Tendency.** The Brigades pay no tribute to the Italians. They keep a working arrangement with the Pellegrinos (the 50/50), a courtesy toward the Sabatinis (the politicians) and a wary distance from the Ferrantes (trucking and the road). They regard the Vannuccis as new men. An Irish go-between in a quarrel is usually a priest, a funeral director or an old ward man.

**Grievances.** The 1973 strike. The decline of the docks. The Italian rise in the machine (Part IV 4.3). The sense that the Italians are "taking" the city that the Irish built.

**Tendency.** The Brigades prefer to deal with a family on terms of equals. A made man who treats an Irishman as a subordinate has made an enemy.

### 3.7 Narcotics

**Tendency (Design).** Kerrytown's old men regard narcotics as a poison to the parish and keep it off the Harp and Crown's business. Younger men and men outside the pub's circle do not. Heroin that reaches Kerrytown reaches it from outside and is sold by men the old guard disowns. This is a dial (section 9.2), and the Brigades' default is a hard "no" at the top and a leaking fence below.

**Open.** Whether any Brigades money finances a shipment through a front (section 9.3).

## 4. The Eastside Combine and the Black Underworld

### 4.1 The Institution

**Fixed (Part III 5.2; Part I 5.6).** The Eastside combine is the Black policy and numbers organization that has run Dunmore Avenue's game since the 1920s. Its seat is **Dunmore Avenue and the Eastside housing projects**. The Strip (Mercer Avenue) is Vannucci ground and is customer ground for the combine. Its leaders are older men who built it over two generations, often in a working arrangement with the Italian families.

**Fixed.** The combine is not a subordinate of the Italians and its leaders resent the arrangement (Part III 5.2). Saint Jude is not a city where the Black underworld is the junior partner by nature. It is a partner that has been squeezed.

**Tendency.** The "combine" is a name outsiders use. Inside, there is no single head. There are several **bankers** (men who hold the numbers money), their **controllers** and **writers**, and a loose council of the older ones who settle disputes. A banker may also own a bar, a funeral home, a block of tenements or a seat on a church board.

### 4.2 The Policy Game

**Tendency [memory, to verify].** The policy game is the old form of the numbers. A player bets a few cents on a number, chosen from a dream book or a hunch, and the winning number is drawn from a public figure. The game employs a chain: a **writer** takes the bet at a candy store or a barber's, a **runner** carries it, a **controller** collects the slips of a district, and a **banker** pays the winners and holds the money. The pay on a hit is far better than the odds in most legal games (Part III 7.2), the credit is personal and the writer is a neighbor. The game is not a crime to the people who play it, and it is a gift to the people who run it.

**Fixed (Part I 9.8).** For fifty years the numbers banks have employed hundreds and financed churches and shops on the Eastside. They are the Eastside's informal bank (Part IV 3.7).

**Tendency.** The banker's moral standing is mixed. He is a source of loans, a patron of the church, a payer of rent and funeral bills, and a man the neighborhood admires and fears. A pastor may bless him or refuse his gift (Part I 10.3). A police captain may take his envelope (Part II 3). A reformer may call him a parasite. All of this is true.

### 4.3 The Working Arrangement with the Italians

**Tendency.** The old arrangement runs on three exchanges. The Italians supply **capital** (a loan against a bad week, a bank for a large hit), **protection** (against police raids and against the Italian crews themselves) and **lawyers**. The combine supplies **the street** and **the customers**, and pays a share. The share is Open and is probably a negotiated slice of the net, as in Part III 7.1. It is not a tribute in the Italian sense, because the Italians cannot enforce it as they can on their own men.

**Tendency.** The families that touch the Eastside are the **Ferrante** (the Racks, the Eastside's white wards, and the pull of "the Eastside's money," Part III 4.3), the **Vannucci** (Mercer Avenue and the Strip, Part III 4.4) and, at a distance, the **Sabatini** through the lawyers and the courts. The arrangement is not with one family. It is a set of understandings with each, and a combine banker will play the families against each other when he can.

**Tendency.** The combine's leaders resent the Italians' share, the contempt of the Italian crews, and the fact that the best lawyers and the best credit are theirs to give or withhold. They are patient, because a war with the Italians would end with the combine's banks closed and its leaders in jail.

### 4.4 The Younger Generation

**Fixed (Part III 5.2).** A younger generation, veterans of Vietnam and children of the 1967 riot, are less patient. Vietnam veterans came home in 1970 to 1974 with training, weapons habits and anger, and often with addictions [memory]. The riot of July 1967 and the April 1968 fires (Part I 5.6) shaped who stayed and who left.

**Tendency.** The young men split in at least three ways. Some want to **replace the old bankers** and keep the game. Some want to **leave the game** for heroin and, later, cocaine, which pay better and are quicker. Some are **political**, drawn to the churches, the tenant councils and the civil-rights tradition, and want nothing from the Life. The Storyteller should show all three and let them argue.

**Tendency.** The old bankers fear the young. They fear them for the violence, for the heroin, and because the young do not respect the church's rule against hurting the block. A young man who is told to wait his turn is a young man who may go to a family for backing.

**Open.** Which individuals lead each of the three groups.

### 4.5 Pick-3 and the Informal Bank

**Weather (strong, from 22 May 1975, [verified] for the date, Part I 9.8).** The State's daily game begins and is a slow, strong threat. It pays less, takes no credit and cannot be played on the corner. The illegal game survives by paying more, extending credit and offering convenience.

**Tendency.** Many street banks adopt the State's own daily number as their number, so the State's draw becomes a public figure that the illegal game can ride on [memory, to verify]. That solves the banker's problem of a fair number and makes Pick-3 both rival and supplier. It also makes the illegal game honest in a way that old players trust.

**Tendency.** The effect is not a sudden death. It is a shrinking of the take in the Black wards over several years, with the biggest loss among the young, who prefer to play a clean state game from a store and will not trust a runner, and among the new Puerto Rican players (section 8.1), who have their own game. The old players hold. The bankers' loan business thins as the take thins. A banker who loses a third of his handle cuts his loans, his charity and his payroll. The Eastside's informal bank shrinks with him (Part IV 3.7).

**Open.** The size of the loss in any year, and whether a banker turns to heroin, loansharking or the Italians to replace it.

### 4.6 Retail Heroin and the Veterans

**Fixed (Part III 5.2).** The combine has a retail heroin trade that is growing in the early and mid 1970s.

**Tendency.** This is the most contradictory fact of the Eastside. The older bankers regard heroin as a threat to the block, to the church and to their own children, and some of them have forbidden it on their corners. The younger men and a few outsiders sell it anyway. In 1975 the retail trade is held by men of the neighborhood who buy from a wholesale source (section 9.3) and sell to addicts who are often their own friends and kin. Some of those addicts are Vietnam veterans. Some of the dealers are too.

**Tendency.** The cost is paid in the projects: burglaries, robberies, ruined families, the clinic's waiting room (Mercy-Eastside, Part I 5.6), police sweeps that fill the jail, and fear on the stairs. The Eastside's pastors, mothers and tenant councils fight it, and some of them lose a son.

**Open.** The size of the trade, which older men tolerate it, and which take a share.

### 4.7 The Kings, as Weather

**Fixed (section 1, #3; Part III 5.2).** The **Mercer Street Kings** do not exist in 1975. The name claims ground on Mercer Avenue, which is Vannucci ground, and it has to be earned.

**Weather (1980 onward, rising; 1985 to 1987, strong, [memory, to verify]).** The cocaine boom and, later, the crack boom change the Eastside's economy and its young men. An organization that sells cocaine on a scale the old bankers never did, and fights the Vannucci family for the Strip's customers, is a plausible product of that pressure. Its name, its leaders and whether it exists are the run's to decide. This Part does not name a founder and does not write its structure.

**Tendency.** If a successor to the old combine appears, the likeliest paths are that a younger faction takes over the combine's streets, that an outside crew takes the market and the combine's old men lose it, or that the Italians buy a new partner. Each is a pressure, not an outcome.

### 4.8 The Church, the Bank and the Hospital

**Tendency.** The Black underworld touches the upperworld through three Eastside institutions (Part IV 3.7, 4.3, 2.7H):

- **The churches.** Mount Zion Baptist, Bethel A.M.E. and a score of storefront Pentecostal congregations (Part I 10.3). Some take the bankers' gifts. Some refuse. A pastor's choice is a statement.
- **The Dunmore Savings Bank.** A community institution that fills part of the credit gap (Part IV 3.7). It is a place where a banker can hold clean money and where a poor man can borrow.
- **Mount Zion Hospital.** Founded by the Black community and in financial trouble (Part I 5.6). Its vendors, its workers' local (Part IV 2.7H) and its pharmacy are the Eastside's hospital touchpoints (section 9.6).

**Open.** Which pastors, bankers and officers are friends of which side.

### 4.9 Violence

**Tendency.** The older bankers use violence sparingly and through intermediaries, because their authority rests on the church and the block. The younger men use it faster and more publicly. The Italian families, when they hurt a combine man, do it through proxies or through the police. A killing on Dunmore Avenue draws the Black press (*The Courier*, Part I 11), the pastors and the NAACP branch, and it draws the machine's Black wing (Part IV 4.3).

### 4.10 Examples (not canon)

**Example.** A numbers banker in his sixties, a deacon at Bethel A.M.E., who lends to half his block and has refused heroin on his corners for ten years. His grandson has a different view.

**Example.** A Vietnam veteran of twenty-six, back four years, who sells heroin to men he served with and tells himself he is better than the man who sold it to them.

**Example.** A woman who runs a controller's book and does the arithmetic her husband cannot (Part III 11).

## 5. The Russians (the Bible's "Vorovskoy")

### 5.1 The Label

**Fixed (section 1, #4).** "Vorovskoy" is this bible's label. It comes from the Russian word for the thieves' world [memory, to verify]. In 1975 no one in Brighton Point uses it. People say "the Russians," "the émigrés" or "the new people." The Storyteller should too, except in the voice of a narrator who knows the word.

### 5.2 The Handful

**Fixed (Part I 5.7; Part III 5.3).** At the start of 1975 a handful of Soviet émigré men are in Brighton Point. They are recently arrived, and they prey on their own community: extortion of doctors, shopkeepers and professionals who have no reason to trust American police. The community is small, mostly Jewish emigrants of the early 1970s, with synagogues, a Jewish Community Center, a senior center and the first banya and Russian restaurants (Part I 5.7).

**Anchor [memory, to verify].** Soviet Jewish emigration to the United States opens in the early 1970s and is a trickle in 1975. It grows in the late 1970s, peaks near 1979 and falls sharply in the early 1980s as the Soviet Union restricts exit. A significant fraction of the new arrivals in New York City settle in Brighton Beach, on which Brighton Point is modeled. All of this is offstage.

**Tendency.** The handful are not a gang in the American sense. They are men whose reputations came with them, or who found a use for their strength in a place where the law means little to their neighbors. They hold no turf, run no business and answer to no one. They are frightening to the people they press and negligible to the Italian families.

**Fixed.** The Italian families treat them as foreign, untrustworthy and useful (Part III 5.3). Whether "useful" becomes a relationship is **Open**.

### 5.3 The Anchor and the Open Figure

**Anchor [verified in Part III 5.3, carried].** Evsei Agron arrived in the United States in October 1975, and by 1980 his extortion of the émigré community was worth tens of thousands of dollars a week. The Five Families of New York took a tax of about two cents a gallon on the Russian fuel-tax scheme in the early 1980s. Part V adds nothing to these facts and does not check them.

**Open (Part III 5.3).** Whether Saint Jude has an Agron-like figure, and if so when. The real arrival is October 1975 in New York City. A Saint Jude counterpart, if the run produces one, is a **stand-in** and not the real man. The real man is an offstage historical fact only.

**Design (default).** No such figure exists in January 1975. The run's pressure toward one rises from late 1975 and is strong by 1978 to 1980.

### 5.4 Weather

| Window | Condition | Strength | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1975 onward | Soviet emigration grows | moderate, rising | [memory, to verify] |
| 1977 to 1980 | The émigré community grows past a few thousand, and an organized extortion appears | moderate to strong | Design |
| About 1979 | Emigration peaks | strong | [memory, to verify] |

| 1980s | Federal attention to the émigré groups | moderate, rising | [memory, to verify] |
| Mid 1980s | Some groups move into narcotics, protection of the Italians' interests, and violence among themselves | Open | Design |

**Tendency.** A fuel-tax scheme, medical-billing fraud and insurance fraud are the familiar forms (Part III 5.3 and Part IV 3, 5.8). The bible names them and gives no method.

### 5.5 How They Meet the Families

**Tendency.** The Italians want two things from the Russians: a **tax** on what the Russians earn and **peace** in Brighton Point. The Russians want protection from the Italians, who can hurt them, and access to the Italians' lawyers and fences. A deal is struck by a go-between, who is probably a Jewish lawyer or accountant of Brighton Point (section 7.1).

**Tendency.** Whichever family is nearest takes the tax. The Ferrante family is nearest by geography (the Eastside's white wards, the Shore Parkway) and the Sabatini by money. Which family takes it is **Open**.

### 5.6 The Medical Billing Door

**Tendency.** Brighton Point's émigré doctors, dentists and pharmacists are the first targets of the handful and, later, the instruments of a different crime. A clinic that bills insurers for work not done, a laboratory that bills for tests not run, and a pharmacy that bills for pills not dispensed are the ordinary forms of the period. They touch the hospital pharmacies and the welfare-fund clinics of Part IV 2.6 and 5.10. The crime is legal fraud and not violence, and the Russians are one of several groups to do it. No method is given.

**Open.** Whether the Russians' medical side is led by the same men as their extortion.

## 6. The Iron Horsemen MC

### 6.1 The Institution

**Fixed (Part III 5.4).** The Iron Horsemen are an outlaw motorcycle club with a fortified clubhouse in the **Killbuck Flats**. The Flats are mill, chemical and refinery country (Part I 6.3), and the clubhouse is in a corner of it where the law rarely looks.

**Anchor [memory, to verify].** In the 1970s the great outlaw motorcycle clubs of the East (the Hells Angels, the Pagans, the Warlocks, the Outlaws) are growing, and they have long-standing relations with the Mafia families of their regions. They are offstage context only. The Iron Horsemen are a Saint Jude stand-in, not a chapter of any of them, and no real club's internal events are used.

### 6.2 The Structure

**Tendency.** A club has a president, officers, full-patch members, prospects and hangarounds. It is a brotherhood with rules, a vote, a rank order and a treasury. The rules are tight: a man's colors are his identity, a man who leaves loses them, and a member who informs is dead. There are women around the club (girlfriends, wives, "old ladies") who hold much of the club's real life together and are given little standing (Part I 12.4).

**Design.** Twenty to forty full members in 1975, with prospects and hangarounds adding as many again. Unsourced.

### 6.3 The Trade

**Tendency.** The club's trade, in the order of the Storyteller's use:

- **Muscle for hire.** Bar security, debt collection, intimidation and arson on contract. The Italians use the Horsemen for work they want done at a distance.
- **Methamphetamine ("crank").** The biker clubs are the period's main producers of amphetamine in the East [memory, to verify]. The bible gives no process. It tells the Storyteller only that the club sells it and the market is rural, working-class and youthful.
- **Stolen motorcycles and parts.** A trade that fits the club's own interest.
- **Contract burglary and arson.** For the fire-insurance fraud of Part IV 5.8.
- **Guns.** Through the southern highways (Part III 5.4). No detail.
- **Bar and club drugs.** The Horsemen's presence in a bar sells.

### 6.4 The Grievance

**Fixed (Part III 5.4).** The Italians use the Horsemen as muscle and disposable. The Horsemen hate being treated that way.

**Tendency.** The club regards itself as a free brotherhood and the Italians as old men with a corporate hierarchy. It is also frightened of the Italians' reach into the courts and the police. The result is a club that complies, serves, resents and, in its own time, pushes back.

**Tendency.** A Horsemen member who is hurt by a made man answers to the club, which answers to its president, who has to weigh the club's survival against its pride. A wise president takes a payment. A proud one starts a war.

### 6.5 How They Meet the Families

**Tendency.** The Horsemen are served by the **Vannucci** family (the Flats, the Ironworks, Part III 4.4) most, because the clubhouse is in Vannucci ground. The **Ferrante** use them for work on the road. The **Sabatini** and **Pellegrino** families use them rarely and at arm's length.

**Tendency.** The Horsemen pay a courtesy but not a tribute. A club that grows large pays the stronger power a rent in the form of work. A club that refuses to be used finds that its drug supply, its bar licenses (Part IV 4.7) and its friends in the SJPD (Part II 3) dry up.

| Late 1970s | Rural and working-class amphetamine use grows | moderate | [memory, to verify] |

## 7. The Merchant and Specialist Communities

### 7.1 Jewish Financiers, Bookmakers and Specialists

**Fixed (Part III 5.5).** Jewish men have been in the underworld of every Eastern port since the 1910s, as bootleggers, bookmakers, garment owners, accountants, lawyers and loan-sharks. They cannot be made. They can be rich, trusted and powerful, and they pay for the privilege by being useful. The relation to the Life is mutual need and unspoken contempt on both sides.

**Tendency.** They are a class and not a group. There is no Jewish "organization." There are bookmakers who run layoff banks, garment owners who borrow and lend, jewelry dealers who fence, accountants and lawyers who launder, insurance men who write the paper for a fire, and old men who held the books for a family two generations back. Their tie is trade and reputation, and their credit is their word.

**Fixed (section 1, #6).** The **Hunts Row** textile and diamond exchange (Part I 5.6) is the city's master fence in the sense of a market where stolen goods are bought, sold and re-labeled. It is a **mixed class of Jewish and Italian dealers** on Ferrante ground, protected and taxed by the family.

**Tendency.** The shape of the arrangement:

- **The dealer** holds a shop on Hunts Row, buys what is brought, resells through a legitimate-looking channel and keeps a margin.
- **The Ferrante family** protects the Row from the police and from other crews, takes a tax from the dealers and supplies the goods (hijacked trucks, burglaries).
- **The jeweler or furrier** who has lost goods to a burglar may find them again on the Row, and may be told by an Italian friend that he can buy them back.
- **The old Jewish dealers** and **the Italian newcomers** are not the same. The older men resent the family's growing share and the Italian crews' roughness.

**Tendency.** The garment and jewelry finance of Part IV 3 and 5 meets this class at three points: the **manufacturer's loan** (a garment owner borrows from a financier when the bank refuses, Part IV 3.7), the **surety and insurance** (the broker who writes a bond or a fire policy, Part IV 5.8) and the **accountant** (the man who knows both sets of books, Part IV 5.4).

**Tendency.** The relation to the Italians is a long, mixed and sometimes uneasy one (Part I 10.4). A Jewish financier may be an Italian boss's oldest friend and the object of a boss's private contempt. An Italian may marry his daughter to a Jewish lawyer's son and not be invited to the wedding.

**Period Mind.** The prejudices are spoken aloud and the Storyteller should not modernize them. Individuals vary within them (Part II 0.3). The reader should not leave with a stereotype of "the Jewish moneylender." The class is varied: a rabbi's son who is a bookmaker, a woman who keeps a family's books, a lawyer who has never taken a dishonest dollar, a fence who gives to the synagogue.

**Tendency.** The class changes through the decade. The old men die or move to the suburbs. The garment trade moves south and overseas (Part IV 2.7E), and Hunts Row's dealers lose their stock and their market. The younger Jewish professionals are lawyers and doctors, not fences. The Russians of Brighton Point (section 5) bring a new and different Jewish-adjacent presence.

**Open.** The names, the sums and which dealers answer to which family.

### 7.2 Docks Chinatown

**Fixed (Part III 5.6; Part I 6.4).** The Docks Chinatown is **a dozen blocks** of Chinese restaurants and laundries, a family association building, and a **small community** of about 1 percent of the city (Part I 6.4 and its population table) that has kept its business to itself. It has its own society, with **tongs, family associations and gambling halls**. Its underworld does not usually deal with the Italian families except by arrangement.

**Tendency.** A Chinatown is governed by a set of institutions that a visitor does not see: the **family or clan associations** (men of the same surname or home county, who give each other loans, burial and arbitration), the **district or benevolent associations** (men of the same region of China) and the **tongs** (secret-society-like bodies that in the early century fought over vice and gambling, and which by the 1970s are more commercial and political) [memory, to verify]. A newcomer relies on them for work, housing and law, because he does not speak English and does not trust the police.

**Tendency.** The gambling halls are the community's pleasure and its underworld. They offer games that Italians and Irishmen do not play, and they draw players from outside. The games are the Chinese games of the period (fan-tan, pai gow, mah-jongg and the lottery called *bok kop piu* in some cities [memory, to verify]). The bible does not describe any.

**Tendency.** The community is small, closed and cautious. It has little reason to deal with the Italian families and a great deal of reason to avoid them. It pays a courtesy to whichever family is nearest. In Saint Jude that is the **Pellegrino** family (the Docks, Part III 4.2). The arrangement is usually a quiet payment, a mutual respect, and a rule that neither side's men bring trouble to the other's streets.

**Anchor [memory, to verify].** The Immigration Act of 1965 ends the national-origin quotas and opens Chinese immigration. New Chinese communities grow in the 1970s, with a new generation of youth gangs attached to some tongs in New York's Chinatown. These are offstage context.

**Weather (1975 to 1990, [memory, to verify]).** Chinese networks become a major factor in Eastern heroin from the late 1970s. Whether Saint Jude's Chinatown has a part is **Open** (Part III 5.6). The community is small, and the more likely pattern is that a Saint Jude Chinatown is a **retail and transit point** for a New York network and not a source.

**Tendency.** If Chinatown enters the heroin trade, it enters it as a wholesale source for the Eastside combine or for an Italian family, and the price is that its own streets become a target of federal narcotics agents. A tong elder who sees the risk will oppose it. A young man who sees the money will not.

**Open.** Whether the community has any part in the heroin trade, and when.

## 8. Other Communities With a Crime Side

### 8.1 What This Section Decides

**Design.** Part III 5.7 left the question Open: which of the smaller groups exist in Saint Jude, and in what strength. This section decides by one test. A community gets an **entry** if Parts I to IV already give it a place, a population and an institution (a district, a parish or a church), and if its crime side would change something the Storyteller can show. A community that fails the test stays **Open**.

**Why no new major organization.** Parts I to III did not create a fifth major criminal organization, and this Part does not. A city of 1.1 million with four Italian families, the Irish, the combine, a biker club, a Jewish dealer class, a Chinatown and a Russian handful already has an underworld at the top of what a bible can hold. Every group below is **small** by design, and none can sit at a sit-down.

### 8.2 The Puerto Rican Blocks

**Fixed (Part I 2, 5.6 and the population table; about 6 percent of the city).** Puerto Ricans came from the late 1940s into the rooming houses of the Eastside and the Point. Part IV 4.3 notes that Puerto Rican politics is only beginning to claim seats. San Juan Bautista is their parish (Part I 10). They work in the Racks, the hospitals and the diners.

**Tendency.** The crime side of the blocks is a **numbers game of their own**, the *bolita*, a small-stakes game whose draw is a public figure in the old country or elsewhere [memory, to verify]. It is run by men from the blocks, in social clubs, bodegas and rooming-house kitchens. It is separate from the Eastside combine's policy game, though the two meet at the edges. The Puerto Rican game is smaller, more tightly kin-bound and more recently arrived.

**Tendency.** Small-time dealing in marijuana, pills and, later, cocaine and heroin runs through the same social clubs. Cuban exiles of the larger Northeastern colonies sometimes supply it (8.7). A bodega is often the drop, the bank and the bar.

**Tendency.** The blocks have no sit-down presence and no go-between of their own. They are reached through a parish priest, a ward worker (Part IV 4.3) or a bodega owner. The families regard them as a market to tax, a customer to supply and a neighbor to avoid.

**Weather (1975 to 1990, moderate).** Pick-3 hurts the *bolita* as it hurts the combine's game, but less, because the players distrust the State and the game is a social fact. The blocks grow through the 1980s (Part I 13), and the Puerto Rican share of the city's retail narcotics grows with them. Any organization that rises from the blocks is the run's to make.

**Open.** Every named man, which families tax the game, and whether a Puerto Rican crew takes ground from the combine.

### 8.3 Pescadores Row

**Fixed (Part I 5.1).** The old Portuguese fishing quarter at the neck of the Point. Narrow streets, azulejo-tiled fronts, three bakeries, two fish markets, Our Lady of the Sea, and a club. A fleet of about forty boats, the last working fleet in the harbor. The Portuguese keep to themselves and are underestimated.

**Tendency.** The Row's crime side is small and old. It is the **fish and the boats**: undeclared catch sold for cash to the fish market, fuel and gear passed off the books, and a boat that can carry a package out to sea and back. The fleet and the market are ground of the **Pellegrino** family (the fish market, Part IV 3.3), and the Row has an arrangement of long standing with them. The Row's own men are not criminals in any other sense, and the club is a place where a stranger is looked at.

**Tendency.** The danger to the Row is that a boat is borrowed for a purpose the owner did not choose, and that a family or the law then holds the owner responsible. The bible says no more.

**Open.** Whether the Row has a man the families go to, and whether a boat has already been used.

### 8.4 The Sixth Ward: Greeks, Lebanese and Armenians

**Fixed (Part I 6.4).** An old, shrinking Greek, Lebanese and Armenian quarter near the Basin, with three Orthodox churches, coffee houses and some of the best restaurants in the city. The diner business in Saint Jude is largely Greek (Part IV 3.3). It is also a political ward (Part IV 4.4) and a bloc (Part IV 4.3).

**Tendency.** The crime side is the **coffeehouse** and the **card room**: the old men's gambling, a small bookmaking line, and the credit that passes among relatives. It is mostly cash and mostly clean in the eyes of the law. The diners are a part of the cash economy that fronts and skims use (Part IV 3.5), and a diner owner who needs a loan or a partner is a man the families will meet.

**Tendency.** The Greeks and the Lebanese are traders, with their own lines of credit and import, and they are bound by church and kin. They are courted and sometimes pressed by the families. They do not form a criminal organization.

**Open.** Whether a Sixth Ward man is a go-between or a front.

### 8.5 Kosciuszko Park

**Fixed (Part I 6.4).** A Polish neighborhood in the north-west, between the Flats and the Heights. Red-brick bungalows, Polish bakeries and butchers, St. Stanislaus Kostka, the Polish Falcons hall, a stubborn pride, skilled and union men. "A reliable source of voters, steelworkers, cops and nuns."

**Open.** Whether Kosciuszko Park has a crime side. The test in 8.1 is not met, because Parts I to IV give it a place and an institution but nothing a crime side would change. **Tendency:** if it has one, it is a **tavern and a hall**, a bookmaker who takes the Falcons' bets, and a few sons who went to the Flats. It is more likely an exporter of cops and steelworkers than of criminals. The Storyteller should leave it largely clean.

### 8.6 Independents and Professional Criminals

**Tendency.** A city of this size holds independent hijackers, burglars, robbers, stickup crews, forgers and safe-men who work without a family. They are not a group. They are a labor market. They work for a crew when asked, sell to a fence when they have goods, and run when a family wants them gone. Their mortality is high. They are the Storyteller's cheapest supply of hired hands, scapegoats and rivals, and are always **Open** as individuals.

**Tendency.** A family calls an independent in when it wants work that cannot be traced to it, and punishes him when he works on its ground without leave. The independents know this and keep a lawyer.

### 8.7 Cuban Exiles

**Tendency [memory, to verify].** The Cuban exile colonies of the Northeast are concentrated in the real towns of Hudson County and in Miami, not in Saint Jude. They are **offstage**. Part III 5.7 and 5.2 assign them a role in cocaine and gambling, and Part V keeps it as a **connection** and not as a Saint Jude group: a Cuban supplier in a northern town, a Miami contact, a bodega owner who has a cousin. The Storyteller may introduce one man as an **Example**.

**Open.** Whether any Cuban exile is a resident of Saint Jude, and in what strength.

## 9. The Drug Markets

### 9.1 How to Read This Section

**Fixed.** A drug market is a chain: **source**, **wholesale**, **retail**, **user**. This section describes each chain as a market, by role, money flow and consequence. It does not describe how any drug is made, moved or hidden (Part I 0.5 principle 7). It names no price, apart from Design figures marked unsourced. **Part VI supplies the price book (sections 6 and 8).**

**Fixed (Part III 7.6, 9).** The Life's relation to narcotics is the most contradictory of all. Many old bosses hold that narcotics bring long federal sentences. Several families forbid their made men to deal. Others lend money to a front. The rule is cited and broken, and a boss may be in the trade himself.

**Flag (section 0.4).** The codex's narcotics material is not in the project, so this section is built from Part III 7.6 and 9 and from [memory].

### 9.2 The Per-Family Narcotics Dial

**Open (Part III 7.6, handoff).** Whether the families take drug money at all in 1975, and what each family's rule is. Part III does not settle it for all four. This section therefore makes it a **per-family, per-drug dial** and not a global rule.

**The dial.** Each family has a setting for each drug, from 0 to 3.

| Step | Meaning | What a made man may do |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Forbidden and enforced** | Nothing. A man who deals is shelved or killed (Part III 9). |
| 1 | **Forbidden to deal, finance tolerated** | He may lend money to a front man and take a return. He may not touch the product. The rule most of the old bosses live by. |
| 2 | **Permitted to crews, with a tax** | A crew may deal, and kicks up its share. The family does not run the trade. |
| 3 | **Run by the family** | The family wholesales, protects and takes a share. |

**Design defaults for early 1975.** The table is a starting setting only. The run, a boss's death, a federal case or a shortage can change any entry. A boss who sets a rule may also break it.

| Family | Heroin | Cocaine | Marijuana | Pills | Crank |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sabatini** | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 0 |
| **Pellegrino** | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| **Vannucci** | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| **Ferrante** | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1 |

**Reasons for the defaults (Tendency).**

- **Sabatini.** The politicians and the best lawyers. They fear the federal sentence most. They lend, they do not touch.
- **Pellegrino.** Old-line, bound to the docks and the parish. Heroin is dishonor. Pilferage and gambling are the trade.
- **Vannucci.** The Strip and its temptations, the fewest made men, squeezed between the others (Part III 4.4). The 14th Precinct Vice's price (Part III 4.4) raises the need for cash. **Narcotics is not large money for them. At 1975 prices their whole drug take is **under 2 percent** of their modeled net (Part VI 6.5). The reasons are risk appetite and the Strip's need for cash.**
- **Ferrante.** Trucking and the road, the best fence, and the pull of the Eastside's money (Part III 4.3). "Also cocaine wholesale, when the market comes." That is why cocaine is at 2 and rising.

**Counterargument.** A reader may say the Vannucci default is too high, since a family with 30 made men has the most to lose from a federal case. The strongest reply is that the smallest family has the most reason to take a risk. A rule against narcotics is a luxury of the rich. **Part VI 9.5 finds the Vannucci the richest per made man, so the reply should rest on risk appetite and the Strip's cash needs and not on poverty.**

**Tendency.** Dials move for four reasons. A **boss's death or succession** (a new boss reads the rule new). A **federal case** (a family that loses men to a narcotics conspiracy tightens the rule). A **shortage of other money** (the Pellegrino docks decline, Part IV 2.7A). A **market shift** (cocaine's rise lifts every cocaine dial, 9.5).

**Tendency.** A made man's own view differs from his family's rule. A man at step 0 may lend. A man at step 3 may refuse. The Storyteller shows both (Part III 10, obligation 8).

**Fixed (Part III 9).** The taboo row for dealing where a family forbids it is a price, not a wall. The boss who set the rule is the one who can excuse the breach, and he may be in the trade himself.

### 9.3 The Chain

**Tendency.** Every chain has the same four roles. Their weights differ.

| Role | What they do | Who holds it in 1975 |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Supplies the wholesale to the region | Offstage: overseas networks, New York wholesalers, Miami and the Cuban colonies, the biker clubs for crank |
| **Wholesale** | Buys in quantity, splits and sells to retail | A few Italian crews (by the dial), Cuban-linked or New York-linked suppliers, a Black wholesale man with a New York or Philadelphia source |
| **Retail** | Sells to the user | The Eastside men, the Puerto Rican blocks, small-time Italian and Irish dealers, the Strip's clubs, the Horsemen at roadhouses |
| **User** | Buys and consumes | Eastside addicts, young white suburbanites, veterans, musicians, mill workers, housewives with a prescription |

**Tendency.** The money flows up the chain in the form of cash and down in the form of product on credit. The profit is greatest at wholesale, the risk is greatest at retail, and the wrath of the law falls on whoever is closest to the street. The tax a family takes is on **net** (Part III 7.1), and it is taken from the crews at step 2 and from the family's own wholesale at step 3.

**Tendency.** A man at step 1 who finances a shipment (Part III 7.6) puts up cash to a front man, who buys from a source, sells to a wholesaler and returns the cash with a premium. The made man never sees the product. His exposure is a conspiracy count.

**Design (unsourced; Part VI to supply).** The markup from wholesale to street is large, and the street dealer is poorer than he looks. A heroin habit costs a user on the order of tens of dollars a day, which is more than an Eastside wage can bear, and which is the engine of the neighborhood's burglaries. No price is given here.

### 9.4 Heroin

**Source and supply (Anchor, [memory, to verify]; Part III 7.6).** The "French Connection" is broken in 1972 and 1973, and Sicilian heroin through Canada and New York continues. Mexican "brown" heroin rises in the mid 1970s. Southeast Asian heroin reaches the East in the late 1970s. Southwest Asian heroin arrives from about 1979 to 1980. The Pizza Connection case lies ahead: arrests in April 1984, a trial from September 1985 to March 1987, and prosecutors' claim of about $1.65 billion of heroin imported into the Northeast and Midwest from 1975 to 1984 [verified in Part III 7.6]. The supply is therefore **shifting**, and each shift is a pressure on prices and purity.

**Wholesale (Tendency).** In 1975, heroin reaches Saint Jude through New York and Philadelphia wholesalers. The Sicilian and French channels belong to men the Italian families know. The Black wholesale network is a younger, more flexible line that serves the Eastside. The Italian families at step 1 finance and step 2 tax. The Pellegrinos stay out.

**Retail (Tendency).** The Eastside combine's young men and a few outsiders. The Strip's clubs. The Puerto Rican blocks. Heroin retail is the **closest the Life comes to the street**, and its violence is retail violence.

**Users (Tendency).** Eastside addicts of all ages, veterans, musicians, and a thin line of white suburban users. The clinic waiting room (Mercy-Eastside, Part I 5.6) is the place where the user and the state meet.

**Cost to the neighborhoods (Tendency).** Burglary and robbery on the Eastside and the Racks fringe. Ruined families. Overdoses. Police sweeps. A hardening of the elders against the young. A wedge between the church and the street.

**Weather (1975 to 1990, [memory, to verify]).** **Heroin purity rises from the early 1980s. The real price of a pure gram is roughly flat from 1981 to 1990 (Part VI 6.1, verified); before 1981 it is Design. New sources change who supplies, more than what a gram costs.** The Eastside's heroin trade grows through the late 1970s (Part III 13.2). The 1980s bring cocaine and crack to the same corners, and heroin becomes one drug among several.

### 9.5 Cocaine

**Fixed (Part III 5.2, 7.6).** In 1975 cocaine is expensive, small-volume and in the hands of Cubans, Colombians and the rich. Wholesale cocaine from South America is rare and mostly goes through Miami and Cuban hands. It grows through the decade.

**Wholesale (Tendency).** A Cuban-linked or New York supplier. The Ferrante family's "cocaine wholesale, when the market comes" is the Italian presence (Part III 4.3). The Vannucci family at step 2 takes a share of its crews. The Eastside's older bankers resist it, and their younger men do not.

**Retail (Tendency).** In 1975, a small, wealthy, mostly white market: clubs on the Strip, nightlife, professionals. The street trade is small. The change to a mass street market is the Weather of 1980 and after.

**Weather (1980 onward, rising; 1984 to 1985, rock cocaine appears in the Northeast; 1985 to 1987, crack boom, strong; all [memory, to verify]).**

- **1980 onward.** Powder cocaine grows into a mass drug. The price falls, the volume rises and the suppliers multiply. The Italian families at step 1 are pulled by the money, and their dials move up (9.2). Part III 13 carries it as the "cocaine boom."
- **1984 to 1985.** Smokable cocaine in rock form appears in Northeastern cities.
- **1985 to 1987.** The crack boom. A cheap, quick and addictive form of the drug is sold on corners by young men with few other jobs. It changes the Eastside, the Strip and the police (Part III 13.2, Part I 13).

**Tendency.** The boom tests the drug rule to breaking (Part III 13.1). A family that held at step 1 finds its young men at step 2 and its older men furious. A family that went to step 3 finds that the federal case is built in months.

**Tendency.** The market for cocaine at the top is rich and white, and at the bottom is poor and Black. The same drug has two markets and two sets of consequences. The law falls harder on the second than the first, and the Storyteller should show it.

**Open.** The size of the 1975 market, when the dials move, and which family goes first.

### 9.6 Prescription Pills and Diversion

**Tendency (Part III 7.6, "a quiet business in the doctors' offices").** A prescription-pill market runs alongside the street drugs. It is quiet, legal on its face and large. Its forms in the period are the sedative, the stimulant and the painkiller [memory, to verify].

**Roles (Tendency).**

- **The doctor who writes freely.** A physician with a crowded office and a long waiting room, often in a poor district, whose prescriptions are sold on at a margin.
- **The pharmacist who fills them.** A neighborhood druggist who knows the doctor and the customer.
- **The hospital worker.** An aide, orderly or pharmacy clerk who removes stock from a hospital pharmacy or a ward for sale (Part IV 2.7H). This is a small, constant leak, not a ring.
- **The welfare-fund clinic and the billing door.** Prescriptions and visits billed to a fund or to an insurer (Part IV 2.6, 5.10 and section 5.6 above).
- **The street buyer.** Eastside users, a white working-class market, mill and truck-yard workers (the Flats), housewives and the elderly.

**Tendency.** The pill market is the one where **respectability is thickest**. A made man at step 1 may finance a doctor's office, a pharmacy or a clinic with a clean conscience. A reformer may find it harder to hang than street heroin. The police treat it as a medical-board matter, not a crime wave.

**Counterargument.** A critic could say that treating pills as "quiet" understates their harm. That is correct for the period's later years. Pills cost lives and families. They are quiet in 1975 because the period does not see the harm, and the Storyteller should let it show later.

**Open.** Which doctors and pharmacists are involved, and which hospital leaks.

### 9.7 Marijuana

**Tendency (Part III 7.6).** "A young man's trade, mostly open and loosely organized." It reaches Saint Jude by imported bulk, mostly Mexican and, later, Colombian, with a rising domestic share [memory, to verify]. The retail trade is large, informal and young: a student sells to a friend, a bar worker to a customer. The suburbs consume it as much as the city.

**Wholesale (Tendency).** A few crews, Italian and independent, who buy by the bundle and break it down. The Italians at step 2 tax it. The Pellegrinos at step 1 look away.

**Cost (Tendency).** Low in violence, high in arrests, with the law falling unevenly: a suburban white student is cautioned, an Eastside youth is jailed.

**Weather (1975 to 1990, [memory, to verify]).** Public attitudes soften through the late 1970s and harden again in the early 1980s. Penalties rise in the 1980s. The trade is large, steady and an entry point to the others.

### 9.8 Crank and Other Drugs

**Crank (Part III 5.4).** Methamphetamine, made and sold by the biker clubs. Its market is rural, working-class and young (section 6.3). Most of the families keep it at step 0 or 1, because it is the club's and the clubs are not their men. It is a smaller market in Saint Jude than in the Midwest and West [memory, to verify].

**Other drugs of the period [memory, to verify].** A sedative-hypnotic ("quaaludes") has a vogue in the late 1970s that fits the Strip's disco clubs (Part I 13). A hallucinogen sold as "angel dust" rises in the late 1970s. LSD and other hallucinogens persist from the 1960s. The bible gives each a line and does not build a market for any.

**Tendency.** These drugs are the youth and club trade. They are the least organized and the least profitable for the families, and they are a risk to every Strip club that sells them.

### 9.9 The Cost to the Neighborhoods

**Tendency.** The consequences are not evenly spread.

- **The Eastside.** The projects, the stairwells and the clinic bear the weight of retail heroin and, later, crack. The institutions (churches, tenant councils, the *Courier*, the NAACP branch) fight back. Violence among young dealers rises. Families break. The state of the neighborhood pushes more people out and leaves the poorest behind (Part I 13).
- **The Strip.** The cocaine and pill trade corrupts the clubs and the 14th Precinct (Part II 3). The Strip's money is high and its owners' lives are short.
- **The Hill, Kerrytown and Kosciuszko Park.** Younger men leave for the suburbs. The parishes lose sons to the drug, to the war and to the job market. The old families feel it as a decline.
- **The Flats.** Pills, crank and alcohol among mill and truck-yard men.
- **The suburbs.** Marijuana, pills, later cocaine. Money in, risk low.
- **The police.** A narcotics enforcement effort that feeds the Pad's temptations (Part II 3), builds arrest records and does little to the chain.

**Fixed.** The Storyteller should show the cost as a neighborhood fact and not as a lesson.

The sequence in outline: the heroin supply shifts after the early 1970s, cocaine rises from 1980, crack arrives about 1984 to 1985 and booms 1985 to 1987, federal pressure rises through the 1980s, and Pick-3 and the shrinking numbers game push the older bankers toward other money.

## 10. How the Groups Meet the Families and the Upperworld

### 10.1 Tribute or No Tribute

**Tendency.** The Italian families take tribute from their own made men and associates (Part III 3.3). They do not take tribute from an outside group in the same sense, because they cannot enforce it as they can on their own. What they take is a **share by arrangement**, and its form differs by group.

| Group | Pays the families | In what form | Nearest family |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brigades** | No | A courtesy and the 50/50 on cross-river items | Pellegrino |
| **Eastside combine** | Yes, a share | A negotiated slice of the net of the game, and a rent for protection and credit | Ferrante, Vannucci |
| **Russians** | In 1975 no. Later, a tax | A tax on the fuel-tax scheme, per the Part III 5.3 anchor | Open |
| **Horsemen** | A rent in the form of work | Muscle, arson, collection | Vannucci |
| **Jewish specialists** | Yes | Fees, a share of the fence and the layoff bank, a gift | Ferrante, Sabatini |
| **Chinatown** | A courtesy | A quiet payment and a rule against trouble | Pellegrino |

| **Pescadores Row** | Small | An old arrangement over the fish and the boats | Pellegrino |

### 10.2 Alliances and Grievances

**Tendency.** The alliances are of convenience and the grievances are of memory.

- **Pellegrino and Brigades:** the 50/50 and the 1973 strike.
- **Ferrante and combine:** the old working arrangement, with the Eastside's money pulling the Ferrante and the combine's resentment pushing back.
- **Vannucci and Horsemen:** muscle for cash, with the Horsemen's resentment.
- **Sabatini and the Jewish financiers:** two generations of lawyers and accountants.
- **Pellegrino and Chinatown, and Pescadores Row:** arms-length peace.
- **All families and the Russians:** foreign, useful, wary.
- **Combine and Brigades:** an old mutual wariness, since the Irish ran the city's police and the machine when the Black community had no share. Neither pays the other.

### 10.3 Settling a Dispute

**Tendency (extends Part III 6.3).** A quarrel between a family and an outside group runs on a ladder of its own, because the outside group has no seat and no oath.

1. A **message** by a go-between (section 2.4).
2. A **meeting** between the man with the grievance and the group's senior man, on neutral ground (Part III 6.4).
3. A **mediator** the group accepts: a priest for the Irish, a pastor for the Black underworld, a lawyer or an old financier for the Jewish dealers, an association elder for Chinatown, a trusted bar owner for the bikers.
4. A **ruling**: a payment, a division of a business, an apology, or a death.
5. **War**, which is rare and ugly. An outside group at war with a family is unlikely to win and may hurt the family's other business.

**Tendency.** A ruling by a mediator is binding in honor and breakable in practice (Part III 6.3). An outside group's ruling is more fragile, because the family can walk away and the group cannot.

**Where the Commission-style conventions reach.** The Iron Balance (Part III 6.1) binds the four families. It does not bind an outside group. The Gold Coast convention binds an outside group only through the families' revenge (2.3). The sit-down is open to an outside group only at the families' invitation.

### 10.4 The Taboo Table, Extended for Outside Groups

**Fixed (Part III 9).** A taboo is a price and not a wall. The reactions below extend the Part III table to acts between the families and the outside groups. They are ranges.

| Act | Who cares most | Typical reaction range | Who can excuse it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A made man kills an outside-group man without leave** | The family's boss, the group's senior man | from a payment and an apology to a sit-down to a war | both seniors |
| **An outside-group man kills a made man** | The made man's family and capo, the boss | a hunt for the killer, delivery of the killer, a payment, or a war | the made man's boss |
| **A made man takes a cut from an outside group's game on another family's ground** | The ground's family | a fine, a sit-down, a death | the ground's boss |
| **An outside group deals in a family's drug without the family's leave** | The family's boss | a warning, a beating, a fee, a war | the family's boss |
| **An outside group's man brings gunfire to Oakridge** | All four bosses | harsh and immediate, the offender is delivered | the bosses together |
| **An outside-group man informs on a family** | The family, the group | death for him, a hunt across the country | none |
| **A family informs on an outside group to the police** | The group | a war or a long cold peace | none |
| **A made man disrespects a priest, a pastor or an association elder in front of his people** | The group and, often, the community | an apology, a payment, the offender's disgrace | the community's elder |

**Tendency.** Each reaction is individual, as in Part III 9. A group's senior man, a boss, and a man who learns of the act each decide for himself.

### 10.6 Diversion and the Hospital Pharmacy

**Tendency.** Hospital pharmacies are the Eastside's and the Flats' nearest supply of legitimate pills (9.6). The hospital local (Part IV 2.7H) has a small number of compromised individuals and a much larger number of honest workers who are the first to be blamed. Mount Zion's financial trouble (Part I 5.6) raises the temptation of both the hospital and its workers. No family controls the local. A single aide, a single clerk or a single pharmacist may be compromised, and the Storyteller should treat it as an individual's act.
