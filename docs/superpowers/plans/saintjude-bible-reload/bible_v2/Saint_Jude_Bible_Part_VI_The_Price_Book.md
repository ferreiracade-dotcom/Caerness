## 0. How to Read This Part

### 0.1 What Part VI Is

**Fixed.** Part VI is the numbers layer: prices, wages, rates and flows as of early 1975, with a drift to 1990. It gives the engine defaults and gives the Storyteller ranges. It does not decide what any man does with the money.

### 0.2 Conventions

Tags follow Parts I to V: **Fixed**, **Open**, **Weather** (dated, with strength), **Anchor**, **Tendency**, **Design**, **Example**. Every figure carries one of three marks:

- **[verified]** with the source named: checked against a source in this drafting session.
- **[memory, to verify]**: from memory, not checked. All are listed in section 11.3.
- **Design**: invented to fit, with a stated range. A Design figure is never presented as real.

Dollars are **1975 dollars** unless a column says nominal. Section 1.1 gives the deflator. Figures are **net, never gross** (Part III 7.1). Tribute and kick-ups are on net through the Ladder (Part III 3.3). **Every flow in sections 2 to 8 is a share of a named pool. The shares of one pool add to 100 percent. Where an outside group takes part of a pool, the families' share of that pool falls by the same amount.** Ranges come with a default: the engine reads the default and the Storyteller may draw inside the range.

The four-family structure, the geography, Part IV's rules and the Part V narcotics dial defaults are not reopened. Where the money makes one of them look odd, the section says so as a flag and leaves the rule alone.

### 0.3 What This Part Will Not Do

It gives no method for making, moving, cutting or hiding any drug, weapon or explosive (Part I 0.5, principle 7). It invents no named secret, conspiracy or plot hook. No real person acts or speaks. Real organizations are offstage context. It does not write the narration rules.

### 0.4 How Well It Is Sourced

**Flag.** Parts I to V were written from memory. Revision 1 checked about a dozen items: the deflator, the federal minimum wage, the longshore wage and guaranteed income, one national house price, the 1991 drug price and purity end-point, New Jersey tobacco tax collections and a few dates. **Revision 2 checked more: median family income, the New Jersey and federal minimum wages, the prime rate and the 30-year mortgage rate, the New Jersey sales tax of 1970, the New Jersey Lottery dates and its parimutuel prizes, the price and purity of heroin and cocaine at the smallest purchase level for 1981 to 1990, the 1978 federal cigarette-trafficking act, New Jersey's one-party consent and the year of the State income tax. Section 11.2 lists them. The drug check overturned revision 1: the real price of a pure gram of heroin did not fall 88 percent from 1975 to 1990. From 1981 on it is roughly flat (section 6).** **Still no source was found** for a 1975 to 1980 drug price, an illegal-gambling handle, a New Jersey city's budget, a union fund rate, a funeral cost, an official's pay, or a sales figure or margin by trade. **Searches for several of these returned only links or image tables and are recorded as failed in 11.3.** Those are Design in this draft, tied to the sourced anchors where one exists. A reader should treat sections 2 to 8 as a coherent model and not as a record.

### 0.5 The Results in Brief

1. **Prices.** The CPI roughly doubles from 1975 to 1985 and reaches 2.4 times by 1990. A 1975 dollar is worth 65 cents in 1980 (section 1.1).
2. **Pick-3.** At the default, Pick-3 costs the numbers banks 14 percent of handle and **24** percent of net by 1980, and 20 and **34** percent by 1990 (section 2.3).
3. **Drugs.** The city's drug market is about $54M a year in 1975 and the Italian families' take is about **$0.54M** of it, **about 1.5 percent** of their revenue (section 6). The Part V dial matters more for risk than for income. **The verified ONDCP series shows the real price of a pure gram of heroin roughly flat from 1981 to 1990, with purity up fourfold.**
4. **A heroin habit** costs more than a minimum-wage worker earns (section 6.6). It cannot all be paid by theft.
5. **The scale test.** The revenue model does not rule out 180 to 190 made men and does not prove it. At the default living standard it supports roughly **110 to 360, with 190 just above the skeptic case (about 170)**. Pellegrino is the weak family**, Ferrante close behind,** and Vannucci the rich one (section 9). **Revision 2 applied the edits this implied to Parts I, III, IV and V (11.5).**
6. **The Ladder** carries only about a fifth of a family's spending; bosses live on their own rackets (section 9.6).

## 2. Gambling

All figures are 1975 dollars, net to the bank or the family unless a column says handle. Handle means the money bet. Nothing in this section is a method.

### 2.1 What is sourced and what is not

**Anchor [verified, Reuter and Rubinstein, *Illegal Gambling in New York*, 1982, abstract].** Numbers margins were smaller and more variable than the popular picture. Clerks and managers cheated, bets were unevenly spread, and balances were hard to collect. Bookmaking margins were often very low, and bookmakers had repeated financial trouble. The Mafia financed numbers but did not control entry, regular pricing or individual operations. In bookmaking its role was mainly financing, and it was not the only lender.

**Anchor [verified, Christian Science Monitor, 30 May 1984].** Officials gave the reasons illegal games survive a state lottery: bets of a few pennies, credit, higher payouts and untaxed winnings. A Massachusetts estimate put the lottery's cut into illegal numbers profits at 10 to 15 percent, with no formal study behind it. New York lottery officials saw no effect on New York City rackets. A New Jersey legislator said there was no evidence the lottery had hurt them.

**Flag against Part III 7.2.** Part III 7.2 put the net to the bank at 10 to 20 cents on the dollar. The two sources above say real margins were lower and more variable. The default here is 8 cents (range 3 to 15). The arithmetic chain is in 2.2. **Part III 7.2 has been changed to match in the Part VI pass.**

No handle figure for any real city's illegal numbers was found this session. Every handle below is **Design**.

### 2.3 Pick-3

A winning ticket paid a share of a prize pool, so the payout varied with play. A Rutgers mathematics seminar notice says the early payouts were unusually high and fell over the game's first ten years while the rules did not change [verified in outline]. Revision 1 had a fixed $500 for $1 [memory]; that is withdrawn.** The State's rake of about 50 percent (Part III 7.2) stays Design, so the State's expected return is about 50 cents on the dollar, against the street's 60 cents, plus the street's credit, pennies and untaxed wins. **The game was first called Pick-it and was renamed Pick-3 in 1987 [verified, same].**

## 9. The Scale Test

**Question.** Can the plausible net revenue of the four families support about 190 made men (Part III 3.2 as first written: Sabatini 50 to 60, Pellegrino 45 to 55, Ferrante 40 to 50, Vannucci 30 to 40, with 5 to 8 associates per made man)? The result is reported as the model gave it. No input was changed to reach 190. Revision 2 restated the need at Part III 3.2 as revised (Pellegrino 40 to 50), so the midpoints are Sabatini 55, Pellegrino 45, Ferrante 45 and Vannucci 35, which is 180 men. **Revision 3 (October 2026) rebuilt this section from an explicit allocation table (9.3A), applied the audit's corrections and lowered the Pad (7.5). Every table below is recomputed.**

### 9.1 The short answer

1. **Revenue does not rule out 190 at the defaults. It also does not prove it.** At the Design defaults the four families net **$35.9M** a year after the Pad, about **$200,000** per claimed made man (180 men). At a default standard of living that supports about **360** made men, at a comfortable standard about **230**. About 180 to 190 needs **52 percent** of the default criminal revenue at the default standard, **81** percent at the comfortable one and **31** at the lean one (computed at 180).
2. **The defaults are mine, and the only outside evidence points down.** Reuter and Rubinstein (section 2.1) found numbers and bookmaking margins smaller than the popular picture and the Mafia a financier more than a controller. If the criminal sectors are half the default, about **170** men are supported at the default standard (**100** at the comfortable one). If they are at the low case, about **110** (**53** comfortable).
3. **Supportable range, default living standard: roughly 110 to 360. Central reading: 170 to 360. 190 sits just above the skeptic case, near the bottom of the central reading.** Leave Part III 3.2's total provisional. The omissions in 9.8 (violence, trials, forfeiture, leakage, laundering, attrition) are not netted and each lowers the count. **Read 360 as a ceiling, and the skeptic case, about 172 at the default standard before those costs, as the better guide.**
4. **Pellegrino is the weak link, with Ferrante close behind. Vannucci is the opposite.** See 9.5 and 9.7.
5. **The Ladder pays little.** Kick-ups under Part III 3.3 carry only about a fifth of what a family spends (9.6). Bosses live on their own rackets.

A sector model of annual net revenue in 1975 dollars, early 1975, built from sections 2 to 8. Each sector is a default with a low and a high, all **Design** (the sources are in the sections). Revenue is allocated to the four families by a **Design** share per sector, **set out in full in 9.3A**. The Pad (section 7.5) is subtracted per family. The result is compared with a **required-spend model**: what the claimed number of made men and their associates spend at a given standard of living.

### 9.8 What the model leaves out

- The cost of violence, trials and forfeiture; the 1984 forfeiture law and later cases (section 10).
- Leakage upward: leaders skim.
- The made man's own legitimate work and his wife's.
- Income taxes and the cost of laundering (Part III 7.8).
- Freelance crime that is not family income.
- Made men who are not earners: old men, shelved men, and the boss's circle.
- The outside groups' share: they hold the Eastside heroin pool and the rest (section 8).
- Mortality and attrition: the roster is a stock, not a flow.

Each omission lowers the supportable count, except the made man's own work, which raises it. I did not try to net them.

### 9.9 Verdict and what was adjusted

Revision 1 listed eight edits to earlier Parts. **Revision 2 applied them. 11.5 gives the table.**

1. **Part III 3.2.** Kept as provisional and **now stated as Open and tied to revenue**: the engine draws a family's count inside its range from its revenue, and a revenue shock shrinks the count over years by attrition, not at once. The total now reads 180 to 190.
2. **Part III 3.2, Pellegrino.** **Default lowered to 40 to 50, default 45; a count above 50, up to 55, needs a dated cause (Part III 3.2). This was my call and Cade accepted it.** The family is the weakest per man (9.5). The alternative was to leave 45 to 55 and let the run find the weakness.
3. **Part III 4.4, Vannucci.** **Count kept at 30 to 40, and 4.4 rewritten as squeezed on territory and not on income. This was my call and Cade accepted it.** The alternatives were to raise the count toward 40 to 50 or to cut the carting and Strip shares. I took the option that did not reopen the family sizes.
4. **Part III 7.2.** The bank's net is now 3 to 15 cents on the dollar, default 8 (section 2.1).
5. **Part III 3.3.** Now says that the Ladder carries only about a fifth of a family's spending.
6. **Part I 9.7.** A soldier's ordinary week is about $600, his good week $1,500, and a capo's ordinary week nearer $1,700.
7. **Part V 9.2.** The Vannucci rationale is now risk appetite and the Strip's need for cash.
8. **Part I 9.1.** **Not a correction.** Revision 1 flagged the job total as high. That flag is withdrawn (1.3). Part I 9.1 now notes that the total counts jobs located in the city.

**Counterargument to the whole test.** A revenue model is a poor way to size a fictional organization. The Life's membership is set by history and honor, not by what the rackets earn, and the real families carried many more men than their income supported by letting soldiers work other jobs. The reading that follows is that 190 is a social fact first and an economic fact second. The model's hard messages are narrower: at the default standard the supportable count runs from about **110** (low case) to about **360** (default case), and a comfortable standard needs most of the default criminal revenue.
