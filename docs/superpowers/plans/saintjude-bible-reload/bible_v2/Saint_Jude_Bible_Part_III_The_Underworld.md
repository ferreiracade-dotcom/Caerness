# Saint Jude: The Setting Bible

## Part III: The Underworld

Baseline: the start of 1975. Section 0 of Part II (the Period Mind) governs everything here. Dated items after 1975 are **Weather**.

---

## 0. How to Read This Part

**Fixed / Open.** Everything in this Part describes the underworld as an institution, with its customs, ranks, money, and the shape of its habits. It does not decide what any man will do. Nothing here is a plot, a secret, or a pressure. Those are born in the simulation from men and circumstance.

**The codex is changeable.** The names, ages, secrets, and stories in the old codex are source material, not canon. Any of them may be changed, dropped, reversed, or invented again. The suggested starting seats in section 4.6 are suggestions only. A man named there can turn out to be an honest man, a fool, a coward, a saint, or dead in 1974.

**Tendencies, not rules.** Where this Part says "the Family does X," it means the Family usually does X and individual men, bosses, and moments will vary. A custom that has a name can still be broken. The consequences of breaking it are the interesting part, and section 9 sets them out.

**Sizes and sums are design estimates.** Membership figures, tribute rates, and prices are tagged **design** and are meant to be changed. Where a fact about the real world is used, it is tagged **[verified]** or **[memory]** as in Part II.

**A note on scale.** The old codex gave four families a combined 265 made men. The largest real families, in New York, had a few hundred members each, and a city of about a million (Philadelphia, say) held one family of roughly a hundred. Four families in a city of 1.1 million (2.4 million metro) with a major port is a stretch. This Part therefore sets the four families at **30 to 60 made men each**, **about 180 to 190 in all (see 3.2)**. Even so, that is generous. The port, the rail yards, and the highways justify it, and the four are described as small, crowded, and quarrelsome because of it. If you want them bigger, say so.

**A note on names.** The old codex's Castellano and Moretti families are renamed **Pellegrino** and **Ferrante**, because Castellano is the name of a real Gambino boss (Paul Castellano, boss from November 1976 until he was shot on 16 December 1985 [verified]) and Moretti is the name of a real New Jersey mobster of the 1940s and early 1950s. The old names may survive in the codex and the repo, and Code needs to carry the rename through. Pellegrino means "pilgrim," which suits a family of the Saint Jude docks.

## 1. The Thing

### 1.1 What It Is Called

**Fixed.** The organization has many names, and which one a man uses tells you who he is.

- **"This thing of ours"** (*questa cosa nostra*, *la cosa nostra*, *cosa nostra*, "our thing"). What the members call it among themselves. The public learned the name *La Cosa Nostra* from the testimony of Joseph Valachi in 1963 [memory]. Members who dislike the publicity still use the phrase in private.
- **"The Family"** or **"the Life."** What the men inside call the local organization and the way of life.
- **"The outfit,"** **"the combination,"** **"the mob,"** **"the syndicate."** What outsiders, newspapers, and the old Irish call it.
- **"The Mafia."** A word used mostly by outsiders, the press, and the police. Italian-Americans of the older generation bristle at it as an insult to the whole people. Made men do not use it for themselves.

**Fixed [memory].** Two phrases carry the whole social structure. A made man is introduced to another made man as **"a friend of ours"** (*amico nostro*). An associate who is vouched for but not made is introduced as **"a friend of mine."** A man who cannot be introduced either way is nobody to the Life. The distinction is so sharp that a man will stand in a room with ten friends of his and not be allowed to speak when "a friend of ours" walks in.

### 1.2 What It Is

**Fixed.** The Family is an inherited body of obligation, held together by blood, oath, fear, money, and the Church. It is not a corporation. It has no written constitution, no payroll, and no headquarters. It is a network of men bound to one another and to a head, who in turn is bound to his men.

**The Father.** A man's Family is, in the Life's own telling, his second family and, for some men, the first. The Boss is the **father** of it. This is accurate as the Life describes itself [memory]:

- He is called "the Boss," "the old man," "the Don," or by his surname with a "Mr.", and he is treated with filial deference.
- He settles quarrels as a father does, between brothers, and his ruling is accepted.
- He gives to those who are in need. He sends the doctor when a man's wife is ill, pays for the funeral, finds the job for the son, lends the money, and stands godfather to the child.
- In return he expects obedience, silence, a share of the earnings, and a man's life if it is asked.
- Both halves are fatherhood to the men inside. The man who feeds your mother in her old age is the same man who may one day order you killed. Neither act is thought to cancel the other.

Real men differ in how much of this they feel. A hard-headed capo may regard the language as a tool for controlling young men. A young soldier may weep at the boss's funeral. The Storyteller shows both, and does not tell the player which is the truth.

**The Brotherhood.** Among the made men, the bond is that of brothers. Each is "a man of honor" or "a man of respect" (the older phrases, *uomo d'onore* and *uomo di rispetto*), and all owe each other fidelity. In practice there is also envy, rivalry, debt, and grievance. "Brothers" is a word for the duty, not for the feeling.

### 1.3 Honor and Money

**Tendency.** The romantic version and the real one are both true, and they share the same men.

The romantic version holds that the Life is a code: loyalty, silence, respect for elders, the protection of the weak in one's neighborhood, justice done outside a corrupt court, a man's word as law, no hands on women and children, and the dignity of a man who needs nothing from the state. It has a literature (*The Godfather*, the novel of 1969 and the film of March 1972, with *Part II* released in December 1974 [memory]), and its heroes are the men of the old school who are said to have lived by it.

The real version holds that the Life is a business of extortion, gambling, loan-sharking, theft, and murder, that the code is applied when convenient, and that most men are in it for the money and the status.

**Fixed.** *Both* sit inside the same man. A capo can weep at a wake and have the dead man's brother's legs broken the following week. A young soldier can believe in the old code with a whole heart and still skim from his crew. A boss can keep faith with a dying friend and cut a rival's throat. The Storyteller never reduces any man to the cynic or the believer, and lets the player discover which obligations that particular man actually keeps.

### 1.4 The Godfather in the World

**Fixed [memory].** By 1975, *The Godfather* (the book, the film, and now its sequel) is part of the world, and everyone has seen it. This matters to how the Life behaves.

- **Older Sicilian-born men** (the "Mustache Petes," in the American slang) mostly dismiss the film as theater, though many saw it three times.
- **Younger Italian-Americans**, especially in the 1970s and 1980s, borrow its style. Black coats, the phrase "an offer he can't refuse," the kiss on the cheek, and the grave courtesy of the "Don" are all, in part, imitations of an imitation.
- **Real mobsters** are on record saying the film is wrong about nearly everything (the dignity, the family loyalty, the refusal of narcotics) and also saying they loved it [memory]. Cade's romantic version is therefore part of the world as well as part of the game: men of the Life *want* to be what the film shows them.
- **The police and the press** use the film as shorthand and are often misled by it.

The Storyteller can use all of this. A young soldier can try to be Michael Corleone in a restaurant and be laughed at by an old man. An old man can hold the same courtesy that the film borrowed from men like him.

## 2. Courtesy and Ceremony

The romance lives here. The Storyteller should write these scenes with weight and care, since they are the scenes players remember.

### 2.1 Deference

**Tendency [memory].** In the older families, deference to a boss and to an elder is real and physical:

- **The greeting.** A man approaches a boss with his head slightly bowed and takes the boss's hand in both of his, or kisses it, or kisses the boss on the cheek. The old Sicilian formula is *Bacio le mani*, "I kiss your hands," said to an elder in sign of respect. Kissing a ring is the film's invention, but an old man with a ring who allows his hand to be kissed fits the period, and the Storyteller may stage it for an old-school boss where it suits the man. A modern boss will not tolerate it and will pull the hand away.
- **The standing.** Men stand when the boss enters. They do not sit until he sits. They do not speak until spoken to, and they answer questions and do not volunteer opinions. A man who interrupts a boss is giving offence.
- **The seating.** The boss sits at the head or with his back to the wall. The underboss and the consigliere sit near him. The captains by seniority. The soldiers at the lower end, or stand.
- **The title.** "Don" is used by Sicilian-born men and by those who want the tone. "The boss" or "the old man" is more common. A man in conversation with another family's boss uses "Mr." and the surname, or "the gentleman," and never a nickname to his face.
- **The silence.** A man does not speak of the Family in front of women, children, or strangers. He does not use names on the telephone. The common phrases are *that thing*, *our friend*, *the gentleman*.

### 2.2 Gifts, Favors, and the Ledger of Obligation

**Tendency.** The Life runs on favors. A favor given to a man puts him in your debt, and a debt is remembered. A boss's favor is the greatest bond of all, because it can be called in with a life. The Life has no written contracts. It has a memory.

- **The envelope.** A gift of money at a wedding, a christening, a funeral, a Christmas, or a man's coming out of prison. It is a mark of respect and often a payment.
- **The sit-down at the table.** Being invited to a boss's Sunday dinner is a sign of rising favor. Being excluded is a sign of falling favor.
- **Asking permission.** A younger man asks his sponsor, his capo, or his boss before he marries, before he moves house, before he starts a business, and before he changes his job. The asking itself is the courtesy. The answer is nearly always yes.

### 2.3 Godparents

**Fixed.** The Catholic bond of the *compare* and *comare* (godfather and godmother) is real and strong. The relationship between a man and the sponsor of his child is spiritual kinship, and it is used. A boss is often godfather to the sons of his captains. A made man's sponsor is called his *compare* (a different use of the same word), and is bound to the recruit for life. Betraying one's *compare* is the worst betrayal in the Life.

### 2.4 Feasts and Funerals

**Fixed.** The Life shows itself publicly at feasts and funerals.

- **The Feast of San Rocco** (16 August), the Hill's great procession, with dollar bills pinned to the saint's statue, bands, a stage, food stalls, and the families' men visible in the crowd [memory: feast processions with money pinned to the statue are a real Italian-American custom]. Bosses and politicians attend and are seen to attend.
- **The Table of Saint Joseph** (19 March), the Sicilian feast of the poor, with altars of bread and food (see Part I).
- **The wake.** A made man's wake lasts two or three days at the funeral parlor, with a long procession of men, floral arrangements shaped like the dead man's trade, a priest saying the rosary, and other families' bosses paying their respects. The wake is also neutral ground, and a place where the Life's politics are carried on in murmurs. An enemy comes to a wake and kisses the widow's hand.
- **The funeral Mass and burial.** The Archdiocese decides case by case (Part I, section 10).

### 2.5 The Making

**Fixed.** To be "made" is to be inducted as a member of the Family. In the Life's own telling, there are four things a man must be: Italian by his father's blood (the usual rule; some families insist on both parents, and individual cases are **Open**), proposed by a sponsor, proven in the Family's work, and approved by the Boss. The Family votes in the sense that the Boss consults his administration, and a single capo's objection can sink a man.

**Tendency [memory].** The ceremony, as reported by Valachi and by later witnesses, runs along these lines. Details differ between families.

- It takes place in a private room, with the Boss, the Underboss, the Consigliere, and some captains present, and the sponsor beside the candidate.
- The candidate has been told only that he is going to meet "some friends," and learns what is about to happen only when it begins.
- The Boss explains what the Family is. He tells him the rules, and says that once in, a man leaves only by dying ("blood in, blood out" is a modern phrase; Valachi reported *I enter alive and I will have to get out dead* [memory]).
- The Boss pricks the candidate's trigger finger and lets the blood fall on a holy card of **Saint Jude, patron of lost causes and of the city**. The card is set alight, passed between the candidate's cupped hands, and he swears, in dialect or in English, that he will burn like the saint if he betrays the Family.
- A gun and a knife may lie on the table, symbols of the life he will lead.
- He is told that the Family comes before his own blood family, his wife, and his faith. In practice the Church and the mother remain in first place, and the Boss knows it.
- He kisses the Boss, and the others, and is introduced to the room as "a friend of ours."

**Tendency.** In the older families, a man was expected to have "made his bones" (killed a man for the Family) before he was made. This was never universal and became less so, and the practice is **Open** by family. After the FBI's use of undercover agents begins (Weather, section 13), the rule is revived in some families as a defense, since an agent cannot be allowed to commit murder [memory].

**Tendency.** The books, the lists on which a family may add new members, are not always open. After Apalachin in 1957, many families closed their books for years. In February 1976 the heads of New York's five families agreed to admit new members for the first time in about two decades. Carlo Gambino opposed it, fearing informants, and was outvoted four to one; each family was to make ten [verified: an FBI memo of 17 February 1976, reported in the press]. Other cities follow the trend in their own time, as the old men die and the ranks thin. Whether a Saint Jude book is open in a given year is **Open**, a thing the simulation decides. The codex put the ceremony around the Feast of San Rocco, and that is a pleasing local custom.

### 2.6 The Sit-Down

**Fixed.** A sit-down is a meeting to settle a dispute. The parties come under a guarantee of safety, meet on neutral ground, state their grievances in turn, and accept a ruling from the persons present. It can be called by anyone with standing, and refused only at risk. Its rules are well known to everyone in the Life:

- Who calls it names the place, and the other side accepts or names another.
- No one comes armed to the table, though the cars outside hold weapons.
- A ruling is binding, and breaking it after the sit-down is a worse offence than the quarrel.
- A man who cannot be satisfied may ask for a ruling from a higher table (his boss, then the other boss).
- A sit-down also serves as a way to lure a man, and everyone knows it, so the guarantee of safety is only as good as the host's reputation.

The neutral grounds in Saint Jude are listed in section 6.

## 3. Structure, Ranks, and Real Power

### 3.1 The Ladder

**Fixed [memory].** Every family has the same structure.

| Rank | Also called | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Boss** | the old man, the Don, the head, "Mr. X" | Final authority. Settles disputes, approves inductions and killings, receives a share from every crew. Often insulated from the street. |
| **Underboss** | *sotto capo*, the number two | The boss's deputy for operations. Runs the captains, carries orders down, and often has the family's violence at his disposal. |
| **Consigliere** | the counselor, the adviser | The elder statesman. Has no crew of his own. Advises the boss, represents the family at sit-downs, and mediates. |
| **Capo** | *caporegime*, captain, skipper | Runs a regime (crew) of soldiers and associates in an area or a line of business. The workhorse rank. |
| **Soldier** | made man, wiseguy, button, "a friend of ours" | An inducted member. Has standing, protection, and the right to an associate crew. |
| **Associate** | "a friend of mine," connected guy, earner | An unmade criminal attached to a made man. Does the work. No standing of his own. |

**Tendency.** Below the associates are the people who pay and do not belong: bookies who rent protection, shopkeepers who pay a "tax," cousins who drive, girlfriends who carry, bartenders who look away. They number in the thousands and are the Life's real surface.

**Open.** The names, the exact numbers, and whether a family has a formal Underboss or Consigliere at any given date. Men die and the seats fall vacant. An **acting boss** or **street boss** may run a family while the real boss is in prison, ill, or in hiding, and a **panel** of captains may share the work [memory].

### 3.2 Sizes (Design)

**Design.** At the start of 1975, approximate made membership:

| Family | Made men | Associates per made man | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sabatini | 50 to 60, default 57 | 5 to 8 | richest, most political, oldest men |
| Pellegrino | **40 to 50, default 45. A run may push the count to 55 only by a dated cause (Part VI 9.9, item 2)** | 5 to 8 | docks and trucks; the biggest street presence |
| Ferrante | 40 to 50, default 44 | 6 to 8 | the Racks and the highways; many gunmen |
| Vannucci | 30 to 40, default 36 | 5 to 7 | smallest, hardest, most closed |

Total around **180 to 190** made men (**the four ranges sum to 160 to 200**) and roughly 1,200 to 1,500 associates. These are estimates and the simulation may move them. **The count is Open and tied to revenue (Part VI 9). The engine draws a family's count inside its range from its revenue, and a revenue shock (a lost Club contract, a docks decline, a Pick-3 hit) shrinks the count over years by attrition, not at once. Part VI's scale test does not rule out about 190 and does not prove it. The Pellegrino default is lowered because their modeled revenue is thinnest per man (Part VI 9.5). That is a design call made in the Part VI pass and **accepted by Cade**, **and a count above 50, up to 55, needs a dated cause (the table above)**. Read "associates" as people who touch a made man's business, not as people he supports.** The defaults in the table are the peaks of the engine's draw and sum to 182, inside 180 to 190. Part VI's scale test uses the range midpoints (55, 45, 45 and 35, which sum to 180). A family's strength is not its head count alone. See section 3.4.

### 3.3 Money Flows Upward

**Tendency [design].** Earning is done by associates and soldiers, and money is passed up as tribute, the "envelope." The usual lines:

- An **associate** pays his soldier a fifth to a quarter of what he earns.
- A **soldier** pays his capo roughly a sixth to a fifth of his net.
- A **capo** pays the boss a tenth to a seventh of his regime's net, and his own soldiers' kick-ups add to what he keeps.
- The **boss** pays no one, but pays for peace, lawyers, and upkeep, and receives a share of every venture the family touches.

The base is **net**, never gross. An associate who runs a numbers bank does not tithe on the money bet, only on the money left after paying winners, runners, and the Pad. (The arithmetic is in section 7.) A man who hides a score and does not kick up is "holding out," and it is a grave offence (section 9). **The Ladder shows who owes whom. It does not explain where a boss's money comes from. In the Part VI scale test the kick-ups carry only about a fifth of what a family spends (Part VI 9.6). A boss's wealth rests on rackets he runs himself: the docks, the Club, carting, the Strip, the fronts. Do not make the envelope the only source of a boss's income.**

**Fixed.** Payday is Friday. The Life keeps a week.

| Day | Business |
|---|---|
| Monday | The count. Bookies and numbers banks tally the weekend. Winners are paid by noon. |
| Tuesday | Losers settle by evening, or the interest starts to grow. |
| Wednesday, Thursday | The work. Sit-downs, collections, union visits, scores. |
| Friday | The envelope goes up. |
| Friday night | The wiseguy's night out (with his girlfriend, where he has one). |
| Saturday night | The wife's night, at the same restaurants. The waiters have never seen the man before. |
| Sunday | Mass, then the long family dinner. |

### 3.4 Real Power and Formal Rank

**Tendency.** A man's rank does not tell you how much he can do. Several levers matter more.

1. **Money.** A capo who brings the boss more than any other cannot be ignored, whatever his title. A consigliere who is broke loses respect, whatever his title.
2. **Muscle.** Who are the shooters loyal to? An order to kill a capo is carried out only if the killers fear or love the giver more than the target.
3. **Access.** Bosses isolate themselves. Whoever drives the old man's car, sits in his kitchen, or carries his messages shapes what he hears.
4. **Slow-walking.** Subordinates seldom defy a superior openly. They delay, claim they could not find the man, lose the message, and let the plan fail.
5. **Personal and institutional loyalty.** One man is loyal to the Family as an idea. Another is loyal to the one man who paid his mother's hospital bill. When these two clash, the second usually wins.
6. **Friends outside.** A capo with a judge, a union president, or a politician in his pocket has standing that no soldier can match.

### 3.5 Who Can Be Made

**Fixed.** The Life is for Italians by blood, in the usual rule, and the Storyteller shows that plainly. A Jew, an Irishman, a Pole, a Black man, or a Puerto Rican can be rich and powerful as an associate, but cannot be made and cannot sit at a family table. This is a fact of the world, not a verdict on anyone in it. There are partnerships that last decades across the line, genuine affection, and also contempt, and the Storyteller shows all of it. Individual exceptions (an Italian father but a mother of another people, an adopted man) are **Open** by family.

**Fixed.** The Life has an internal prejudice of its own. Sicilians look down on Neapolitans and Calabrians, Calabrians on everyone, northern Italians on all of them. Men from the old country look down on Americans-born. These distinctions are used in jokes, insults, marriage arrangements, and promotion.

### 3.6 Leaving

**Fixed.** There are four ways out of the Life.

- **Death.** Natural or otherwise. Most men die in bed or in the hospital, a few in the street.
- **Prison.** A made man doing time keeps his standing if he is silent and loses it if he talks.
- **Retirement.** An old man can step back, usually with the boss's blessing, and keep his house and a small income.
- **Flight.** A man who runs will be hunted if he knows too much, or left alone if he does not. After 1971 he may enter the witness program (Part II, section 6.7).

## 4. The Four Families

Each family is described as an institution. Sections 4.1 to 4.4 list tendencies, strengths, and tensions, none of them fixed secrets. Section 4.6 lists suggested seats.

### 4.0 The Regional Texture

**Design (approved).** Each family has a region of Italy in its blood. This gives it a dialect, a diet, a feast, a temperament, and a set of prejudices. The Italians themselves hold these prejudices as truths.

| Family | Region | Texture as the Life tells it |
|---|---|---|
| **Sabatini** | Campania (Naples, Salerno), with a downtown Genoese veneer | urban, political, talkative, business-minded; "the diplomats"; distrusted by the others for smoothness |
| **Pellegrino** | western Sicily (the Castellammare coast) and the Palermo hinterland | clannish, intermarried, formal, patient; the "old country" men; dockside |
| **Vannucci** | Calabria (Aspromonte and the coast) | closed, suspicious, blood-bound; few outsiders; harder in the way men fear |
| **Ferrante** | Abruzzo and Molise, with Neapolitan street blood | rough, plain-spoken, restless; the family born of a quarrel; many gunmen |

**Tendency.** Surnames are no guide. Names were changed at Ellis Island, adopted from patrons, or taken from a town by a man who wanted a better one. A Sabatini may have a Sicilian grandmother. The cast may play against the table.

### 4.1 The Sabatini Family

**Seat.** Crown Harbor and North San Lorenzo. The Vesuvio Social and Athletic Club on North Hill is the working office.

**Trade.** Construction, concrete, bid-rigging, city contracts, zoning, the building trades, high-end loansharking to developers and merchants, the Master Layoff bank (kept in Crown Harbor), restaurants and nightclubs downtown, casinos if they come. The family has the best lawyers and the most friends in City Hall, the building department, and the courts.

**Character.** The "diplomats." They like business and avoid war. Their bosses have been politicians for two generations. Younger men feel the family has grown fat and slow. Elder men think the young are in a hurry to die.

**Strengths.** Money and friends. **Tensions.** Age at the top, the quarrel between those who still believe the code and those who think it a fashion, and the temptation to sit still while others take the street.

**Open.** What they actually own, how deep the friends go, who is loyal.

### 4.2 The Pellegrino Family

**Seat.** The North Piers, the fish market, South Hill. The Pier 4 diner is the boss's morning office.

**Trade.** The waterfront: longshoremen's locals, checkers, pier hiring, cargo theft, the customs bribes, trucking (Tri-State Freight in the codex), the fish and produce market, and a share of whatever crosses the Narrows. Also loansharking among dockworkers, union welfare funds, and gambling on the piers.

**Character.** The "Hooks," as the downtown men call them, and the most visible. Working-class in origin, proud of it. A family of men who came up as longshoremen, and who feel keenly that the Sabatini men are "paper men." Their rule is the fist and the favor. They hold a long, bitter, partly cooperative history with the Irish on the South Piers.

**Strengths.** Control of the port, a tough street presence, a deep labor bench. **Tensions.** Containerization is shrinking the docks they live on. The 1973 massacre of the independent strikers (Part I, section 2) left a lasting hatred in Kerrytown. The family's grip depends on the unions, and the unions depend on the work.

### 4.3 The Ferrante Family

**Seat.** The Racks, the Eastside rail yards, the I-95 trucking depots, the East Slope of the Hill. The Pompeii Social Club on the East Slope, a block from Hunts Row, is the stronghold.

**Trade.** Garment trucking, cargo hijacking, fencing (the Hunts Row textile and diamond exchange is the city's master fence, a mixed class of Jewish and Italian dealers working on Ferrante ground, which the family protects and taxes; see 5.5 and Part V), chop shops, a car-crushing yard in the Eastside rail yards, wholesale gambling, loansharking to garment manufacturers, interstate trucking, and the Teamsters. Also cocaine wholesale, when the market comes.

**Character.** The family born of a war, who still call themselves "the independents." They hold that the Sabatinis stole the Racks and that they took it back. Plainspoken, restless, with a surplus of gunmen who have to earn something. They think the other families have grown soft.

**Strengths.** Men with guns, control of the roads, the best fence in town. **Tensions.** A habit of provoking, a temper for border disputes, and the pull of the Eastside's money and drugs.

### 4.4 The Vannucci Family

**Seat.** Mercer Avenue (the Strip), the Ironworks, the Killbuck Flats, parts of South Hill. Starlight Cabaret on Mercer is the crown jewel.

**Trade.** Nightclubs, strip joints, after-hours gambling, prostitution, peep shows, cash businesses; scrap metal, steel haulage, waste hauling and illegal dumping, slaughterhouse and rendering, chop shops, labor racketeering in the mills. Also a quiet relationship with the Iron Horsemen MC.

**Character.** The smallest and closest. They split from the Pellegrinos in 1967 and have not forgiven or been forgiven. A family where blood is thick: sons, nephews, and cousins hold the seats. Secretive, watchful, and quicker to violence than to talk. The Strip makes their money and corrupts their men.

**Strengths.** Cash flow, discipline, and fear. **Tensions.** Fewest made men, **squeezed on territory between the Sabatini politicians and the Ferrante expansionists, and not on income. In the Part VI revenue model the Vannucci net the most per made man of the four families (carting, waste and the Strip), so they are a small, rich family with many associates. Their pressure is to hold ground, not to find money.** The Strip's temptations, the drug trade, and the 14th Precinct Vice's price.

### 4.5 The Families' Quarrel

**Fixed.** The four are not partners. They are the heirs of two original combinations, the Sabatini and the Pellegrino, and of the splits that made the Ferrante (1958) and the Vannucci (1967). They regard each other as usurpers. Their peace is the Iron Balance (section 6): a standing mutual fear, bound by the cost of war and the closeness of federal eyes.

## 5. The Outside Groups

The four Italian families are not the whole underworld. Each group below is an institution and does not decide any man's conduct.

### 5.1 The Charlestown Brigades (Irish)

**Seat.** Kerrytown and the South Piers. The Harp and Crown, a pub, is the headquarters.

**Trade.** Dock work and the South Pier crane crews, tugboat yards, cargo theft, a share of the cross-river work in an old 50/50 arrangement with the Pellegrino family (tugs and lighterage, and ship-side pilferage on vessels that work both banks in one call; the Gannet Terminal is not part of it and its control is Open, Part IV 2.7B), union locals, and the arms trade to Ireland.

**Character.** Older than the Italians by a generation in the city, and prouder. The Irish have a different relation to violence than the Italians: the family is the neighborhood, loyalties are older and rooted in the parish and the county, and the cause of Ireland is a real thing. The Brigades take an interest in Belfast. In the 1970s the Troubles are at their height, and money, arms, and men move through the port to the Provisional IRA, some for belief and some for profit [memory]. The FBI and the Irish government both care.

**Tendency.** The Brigades are not a single mind. Some of their men want steady port profits, some want blood for the 1973 strike, some want the cause, some want the quiet life. They are led by older men who remember when the Irish ran the city.

### 5.2 The Eastside Combine and the Black Underworld

**Seat.** Dunmore Avenue and the Eastside housing projects. The Strip (Mercer Avenue) is Vannucci ground (Part I, section 5.5) and is customer ground for the combine, not its seat.

**Trade.** The policy (numbers) game in the Black neighborhoods, which has run since the 1920s; retail heroin and, increasingly, cocaine, in the early and mid 1970s; gambling, bars and after-hours clubs, small loans, real estate, and political influence in the Black wards. In the codex this became the **Mercer Street Kings**.

**Character.** In 1975 the Black underworld of the Eastside is old, rooted, and run by men who built it up over two generations, often in a working arrangement with the Italian families: the Italian families supply capital, protection, and lawyers; the Eastside supplies the street and the customers. It is not a subordinate, and its leaders resent the arrangement. A younger generation, veterans of Vietnam and the 1967 riot, are less patient. The heroin trade (the retail "Black Mafia" era in some cities [memory]) is growing.

**Weather.** The Kings, as an organized cocaine and later crack empire, are a **creature of the 1980s** (Part V). The name claims ground on Mercer Avenue, which is Vannucci ground, so it has to be earned by territory and does not exist in 1975. In 1975 they are not what the codex pictured. Wholesale cocaine from South America is rare and expensive, and mostly goes through Miami and Cuban hands.

### 5.3 The Vorovskoy (Russian)

**Seat.** Brighton Point.

**Trade.** In 1975, a handful of Soviet émigré men, recently arrived, who prey on their own community through extortion of doctors, shopkeepers, and professionals, who have no reason to trust American police. The Brighton Point syndicate's fuel-tax scheme and its wider crime come later (Weather, section 13).

**Anchor [verified].** Evsei Agron arrived in the United States in October 1975, and by 1980 his extortion of the émigré community was worth tens of thousands of dollars a week. The Five Families of New York took a tax of about two cents a gallon on the Russian fuel-tax scheme in the early 1980s.

**Settled.** A handful of Russian émigré men are already in Brighton Point at the start of 1975 (Part I, section 5.7). "Vorovskoy" is this bible's label: in 1975 people say "the Russians" or "the émigrés." **Open.** Whether they are led by an Agron-like figure; how the Italian families treat them. The old families treat them as foreign, untrustworthy, and useful.

### 5.4 The Iron Horsemen MC (Outlaw Bikers)

**Seat.** A fortified clubhouse in the Killbuck Flats.

**Trade.** Methamphetamine ("crank"), stolen motorcycles, contract arson and burglary, guns through the southern highways, bar security, drugs on the roads. In the 1970s the great motorcycle clubs of the East (the Hells Angels, the Pagans, the Warlocks, the Outlaws) are in a period of growth [memory], and they cooperate with, fear, and serve the Italian families.

**Character.** Used as muscle and disposable. They hate being treated that way.

### 5.5 Jewish Financiers, Bookmakers, and Specialists

**Fixed.** Jewish men have been in the underworld of every Eastern port since the 1910s, as bootleggers, bookmakers, garment owners, accountants, lawyers, and loan-sharks. Meyer Lansky's partnership with the Italians is the model [memory]. In Saint Jude they hold:

- Bookmaking and layoff banks.
- Garment-district and jewelry-district finance.
- Arson and insurance fraud.
- Law and accounting.
- Fencing of jewelry and furs.

They cannot be made. They can be rich, trusted, and powerful, and they pay for the privilege by being useful. The Storyteller shows that their relation to the Life is both mutual need and unspoken contempt on both sides.

### 5.6 Chinatown

**Fixed.** The Docks Chinatown (Part I, section 6) has its own society, with tongs, family associations, and gambling halls. Its underworld does not usually deal with the Italian families except by arrangement. Whether it has a presence in the heroin trade is **Open** [memory: the Chinese networks of heroin become a major factor in the East from the late 1970s].

### 5.7 Other Groups

**Tendency.** A city of this size holds Puerto Rican numbers men (the *bolita*), Cuban exiles who work the cocaine and gambling trade (mostly offstage, in the real Hudson County towns to the north [memory, to verify]), Greek and Lebanese merchants with their own lines, independent hijackers and burglars, and the professional criminals who work for hire without a Family. Each is a minor power with its own style. Part V (section 8) decides which get an entry.

**Open.** Which of these exist in Saint Jude and in what strength.

## 6. Diplomacy, the Iron Balance, and Neutral Ground

### 6.1 The Iron Balance

**Fixed (design).** Saint Jude has no Boss of Bosses, no governing body, and no written agreement among its four families. Their peace is **the Iron Balance**, an old standing understanding resting on three things: the cost of an open war, the nearness of federal eyes, and the memory of earlier wars (the 1929 to 1931 harbor wars, the 1946 Four-Corner War, the 1958 Ferrante Schism). A boss who starts a war pays for it in dead men, lost earnings, and prosecutors, and every boss knows it.

**Tendency.** The Balance is not a love. It is a mutual deterrence kept up by many small acts: a sit-down called before a quarrel grows, a go-between sent to say a thing, a favor owed and remembered, a funeral attended. It frays when a family is weak, a boss is sick, an ambitious underboss sees an opening, or a war breaks out nearby.

### 6.2 New York and the Other Cities

**Fixed (design).** Saint Jude's families are independent of New York. They pay no tribute to any outside body and take no orders from one. They have no seat on the New York Commission, which was set up in 1931 and rules the New York families [memory]. They deal with New York as respected neighbors and, where they must, as equals, through the New York families' bosses and their consiglieres.

**Neighbors in the real State.** Saint Jude does not stand on empty ground. The real New Jersey mob holds footholds around it, and the central coast is not as thin as it looks. **Naming.** The bullets below record the real history under its real place names. In Saint Jude's world, read **the northern Monmouth shore and bayshore as the city's own ground, inner Monmouth and Middlesex as Marsh County, and the Ocean County coast as Shore County** (Part I, section 3.1a), and treat any real town inside the city's footprint as replaced.

- **The DeCavalcante family** is based in Elizabeth, Union County, with operations in Essex, Middlesex, Monmouth and Ocean counties and the shore town of Toms River [verified]. Sam DeCavalcante was nominal boss from 1964, jailed in 1969, and on his release in the mid 1970s appointed John Riggi acting boss [verified].
- **The Genovese New Jersey faction** works the Newark-Elizabeth waterfront, Hudson County and the northern counties, and reached Middlesex, Monmouth and Ocean [verified]. Vito Genovese himself lived at Atlantic Highlands, a real bayshore town in Monmouth County, from 1946, guarded by armed men [verified]. (That town lies inside the city's footprint and does not exist in Saint Jude's world. Read his house as a guarded estate on the Heights.) In February 1972 the State Commission of Investigation held a public hearing on Genovese loansharking in Ocean County that exposed the acting leader, Frank Tieri [verified]. Tony Provenzano, a powerful Genovese capo through the Teamsters, is the man Jimmy Hoffa was going to meet when he disappeared in July 1975 [verified]. Jerry Catena, long the New Jersey boss, was in prison from 1970 and was released in 1975, then retired to Florida [verified, with conflicting dates].
- **The Lucchese New Jersey crew** is based in Newark, with Joseph Abate as formal boss from 1964 and Anthony Accetturo rising; it reached Monmouth and Ocean [verified].
- **The Philadelphia family**, under Angelo Bruno from 1959, holds South Jersey and Atlantic City as its fief, and refused New York a share of it [verified].
- **The Gambino family** is present across the State [verified].

**Tendency.** In Saint Jude's world these outside families have crews and friends in the hinterland: the Marsh, Shore and Pine county suburbs, the resort towns, the road houses. The four Saint Jude families regard the city proper as theirs, and the surrounding counties as shared ground with understandings, favors and occasional quarrels. A man from Elizabeth who retires to the Shore is a neighbor to be received with courtesy. A crew that opens a book in the city without leave is an invasion. These are the Iron Balance's outer ring (6.1).

**Real people.** Real mobsters and officials are named here as history and as offstage presences. The Storyteller does not write them as characters speaking or acting in scenes. The Saint Jude equivalents, men of the same kind, do that work in their place.

**Tendency.** Contact runs through these channels:

- **Joint ventures** that cross the line: the port's cargo, the Teamsters, a union pension fund, a casino.
- **Disputes** over a man or a sum that falls in both territories.
- **Personal ties.** A boss who came up with a New York boss, a family that intermarried, a son who went to New York to learn.
- **Courtesy.** The newly raised boss pays a call, a death is marked, a feast attended.

**Open.** Which New York family each Saint Jude family is closest to, and what such friendships are worth. New York has no interest in a war in Saint Jude as long as the containers move on time. If the containers stop, it will take an interest. Philadelphia, Boston, Chicago, and the smaller cities are beyond the first ring and matter less.

### 6.3 Sit-Downs and Go-Betweens

**Tendency.** Quarrels between families are settled in a graduated way:

1. A **message** carried by a friend of both.
2. A **meeting** between the two men who have the quarrel.
3. A **sit-down** among the bosses or their consiglieri, in neutral ground, with a mediator the parties accept.
4. A **ruling** (a division of a business, a payment, a death) or a refusal.
5. **War.** Almost always a last resort.

A **mediator** is chosen for reputation. Often the consigliere of a third family, a retired boss, a New York friend, or a priest. A ruling a mediator gives is binding in honor and breakable in practice.

### 6.4 Neutral Ground

**Fixed.** There are places where the families can sit without fear. These are neutral by custom, not law.

- **Gino's Espresso Bar and the Villa Roma** (Central San Lorenzo): the open-air back terrace of Gino's by day, the Villa Roma restaurant by night. Owners who are friends of everyone and members of no one.
- **Russo and Sons Funeral Home** (San Lorenzo): the black hearses, velvet drapes, and long wakes make it the great gathering place of the Hill. A wake is the safest meeting in the city, since no man disturbs a dead man's house.
- **St. Rocco's** and **Santa Maria della Corte**, at Mass and on feast days. No man commits violence in a church.
- **Oakridge**, the Gold Coast Sanctuary (Part I, section 5): the convention, backed by self-interest and by a borough's own police, that no gunfire, sirens, or bodies come where the bosses' wives, mothers, and children live.
- **Hospitals** and the **sickroom** of a dying man. By custom, a man who is dying is visited and not touched.
- **Weddings** and **christenings**.
- **Prison visiting rooms**, which are bugged, and so used for the one thing that cannot be said.

**Tendency.** Neutral ground holds as long as nobody wants badly enough to break it. The Storyteller can break it, and the breaking is a major event.

### 6.5 Messages and Signals

**Tendency.** The Life communicates in hints. A man learns what he needs by who speaks to him, who does not, who comes to his wake, and who sends a basket. "Coffee with a friend" is a sit-down. A phone call that is ended fast is a warning. A man who is invited to dinner and not seated is being told something. The Storyteller uses these small moves heavily. A reader who understands the signals is rewarded.

## 7. Rackets and Money

All sums are 1975 dollars unless stated, and are **design** estimates. **Part VI gives the price book.**

### 7.1 Net, Not Gross

**Fixed.** Every Family kick-up is calculated on **net operating take**, not gross. Gross is the money that passes through. Net is what is left after winners are paid, runners are paid, the Pad is paid, rent is paid, and the lawyer is paid. The Life's first lesson is that a man can have large gross receipts and be broke.

### 7.2 Gambling

**Sports book.** Bettors lay $11 to win $10 (the "eleven to ten" or "vig"). When the book has equal money on both sides, it pays winners $10 for every $11 collected from losers. For every $220 wagered in a matched pair of bets, the book keeps $10, about **4.5 percent of the handle**. Everything else a bookmaker earns comes from unbalanced action, in which he holds the risk and wins or loses with the game, or lays off the excess with a bigger book for a fee. A bookmaker's costs are his runners, his wire room, a telephone, and the Pad. His backer, a made man, supplies a bankroll, protection, and a layoff line, and takes a share (often half of the net) [design].

**Numbers (policy).** The daily number is a three-digit figure drawn from public results: the last digits of a racetrack's total handle, or, where the state runs a daily numbers game, its drawing. In Saint Jude the traditional source in season is the parimutuel handle at Bayview Downs, which races from March through November (Part I, section 11.3). In the winter months the banks take their number from an out-of-state track's published handle or another public figure [design]. A bettor wagers a small sum (a nickel, a dime, a quarter, a dollar) on a number. The true odds against a winning number are 999 to 1. The street pays **600 to 1** (500 to 1 in some cities), so the bank keeps about **40 cents of every dollar** wagered when play is spread evenly, and less when players favor certain numbers (dates, dream-book numbers, repeats like 777) [verified: the 600 to 1 payout, the 999 to 1 odds, and the roughly $600 paid out per $1,000 wagered]. Out of that hold the bank pays its commissions. In the cities where the figures are recorded, the runner who takes the bets kept **10 percent** of the play [verified for Cleveland in the 1950s and Atlanta in 1944]. The writer or controller above him takes **5 to 10 percent** of the play (design; an earlier draft's "up to 25 percent" cannot be a share of the play against a 40 percent hold, and may have been a share of the bank's hold). That leaves about 20 to 25 cents of each dollar before the Pad and the occasional heavy hit. Net to the bank, after the Pad, bad debt, cheating and the occasional heavy hit, is **3 to 15 cents on the dollar, default 8 (Part VI 2.1 and 2.2). The earlier figure of 10 to 20 is withdrawn, because Reuter and Rubinstein found numbers margins smaller and more variable than the popular picture [verified, NCJ 83192, abstract]**, which is why the numbers are the poor man's gold mine: enormous in aggregate, tiny per bettor, almost impossible to stamp out.

**Fixed [verified].** The first modern state lottery began in New Hampshire in 1964, and New York held its first drawing on 20 July 1967. New Jersey's lottery **was created on 20 November 1969 [verified, Wikipedia, New Jersey Lottery]**, and its daily three-digit game, **first called Pick-it and renamed Pick-3 in 1987,** began on 22 May 1975 **[verified, same]**. State daily games take a rake of about 50 percent, against 20 to 40 percent for the street game. Saint Jude's State is New Jersey (Part I, section 3.1a), so this is the State's own game. It begins five months into the baseline year, on 22 May 1975, and is **Weather** inside the run, not part of the 1 January 1975 state. **Pennsylvania's daily number began on 1 March 1977 [verified, Wikipedia, Pennsylvania Lottery]. New York's Numbers game appears in New York's open-data catalogue from 1980 [verified in outline]; its exact start date is still Open. New Jersey's lottery prizes were parimutuel, not a fixed 500 for 1, so a winning ticket paid a variable sum [verified, Wikipedia, New Jersey Lottery].** A legal numbers game takes business from the street bank, but the street pays better (600 to 1), allows credit, pays no tax, and delivers the winnings to the door. The families lose less than the state expects. After 22 May 1975 many street banks take the State's own daily number as their number, so Pick-3 is both a rival and a supplier of an honest public figure [memory, to verify]. In Massachusetts in the 1970s the Winter Hill Gang moved its bookies toward sports betting as the state lottery began, and the numbers game did not die [verified in outline].

**Other games.** Dice and card games in back rooms (the floating crap game), the sports pool, the football card, the horse parlor, poker with a house take, the clubs and after-hours bars, the Friday-night high-stakes game where a rich man loses to a made man's cheat. The "casino question" (Part I, section 9) lies ahead.

### 7.3 Loansharking

**Fixed.** The loan shark is the Life's bank. The loan is made in cash, with no paper, to men who cannot get credit elsewhere, and is collected by threat.

| Type | Borrower | Typical rate | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wholesale** | A made man or capo, to put money on the street | about 1 percent a week | capital for others to lend |
| **Commercial** | Garment owner, restaurateur, contractor | 2 to 3 percent a week | larger loans, with equity at stake |
| **Street** | Gambler, small hustler, worker | about 5 percent a week | "five points": $50 a week per $1,000 borrowed |
| **Six-for-five** | A man who needs a few dollars today | 20 percent a week | borrow $5 Monday, owe $6 Friday |

**The Principal Rule.** On most street loans, the borrower pays the **vig** (interest) weekly and the principal does not shrink, unless he pays the whole of it at once. A man who borrowed $10,000 at three points pays $300 every week, as long as it lasts. He is never "paying down." He is renting the money, and the debt is a permanent source of income to the lender and a leash on the borrower.

**The Bust-Out.** The lender who has a debtor with a business may let the debt grow, then take the business. The debtor is made to buy goods on credit from the lender's suppliers, sells them for cash, and the creditors are left holding unpaid bills, and the lender takes the cash and then the shell [memory].

**Tendency.** A borrower who pays is valuable and a borrower who cannot pay is more valuable, since he can be made to do things.

### 7.4 Labor and Business

**Fixed.** The Life's largest and steadiest income is the corrupt union and the shaken business.

- **Union locals.** Dock, trucking, construction, garment, hotel and restaurant, hospital, laundry, sanitation. Rackets include kickbacks for labor peace, no-show jobs, shakedowns of contractors, control of pension and welfare funds (a source of loans to friends), sweetheart contracts, and strike threats.
- **The Concrete Club.** A cartel that rigs bids for concrete and construction work and takes a percentage (about two percent in the codex's version) from the contractors who pay to be allowed to win. The real New York cartel of this kind was exposed in the mid 1980s [memory, to verify]. In Saint Jude it is the Sabatini family's creature.
- **Carting and vending.** Trash hauling, linen supply, jukeboxes, vending machines, cigarette machines. Each route is a territory and a monopoly, protected by the family. A route can be sold.
- **Funeral homes, restaurants, bars, and nightclubs.** Cash businesses and the Life's meeting rooms.
- **Protection.** A "tax" levied on bars, restaurants, shops, and bookmakers for the right to be left alone. Not the cartoon of a thug asking for money, but a negotiated annual or weekly sum in return for a quiet life, labor peace, and credit. A shopkeeper may pay gladly.
- **Real estate.** Slowly, the profits are put into buildings, which are legal and safe.

### 7.5 Theft and Fencing

**Hijacking and cargo theft.** The port, the rail yards, and the highways make Saint Jude a thieves' city. A load worth $200,000 at retail may be sold to a fence for **25 to 35 cents on the dollar**, or $50,000 to $70,000 [memory for range]. Out of that:

- A **finder's fee** (usually about 10 percent of the fence's price) goes to the tipster, the "Finger," the man who told the crew which truck, which night, which driver.
- The **driver** may be paid to give up the load, or forced to.
- **Storage and costs** (a garage, a rented truck) are paid.
- The **crew** shares the rest by an agreed formula, with a cut to their made man.

On a $200,000 load sold at 35 percent ($70,000): finder $7,000; costs and driver $7,500; remainder $55,500 for the crew and their sponsor. These are illustrations and not rules.

**Fencing.** The master fence buys, stores, repackages, and resells. The best fences are patient, polite, and rich. They are as important to the Life as any boss.

**Cars and chop shops.** Stolen cars are stripped for parts or shipped overseas. A crusher or a baler destroys what cannot be sold.

**Untaxed goods.** Cigarettes, liquor, gasoline. Where one state taxes more than the next, a truckload from the low-tax state is worth running. Around 1982, North Carolina taxed cigarettes at 2 cents a pack, New York at 15 cents (23 cents in the city), and New Jersey at 23 cents, which made I-95 "tobacco alley" and paid a smuggler up to a dollar a carton; New York claimed to be losing about $70 million a year in the 1970s [verified, from a 1982 report]. **The Contraband Cigarette Trafficking Act was enacted on 2 November 1978 (Public Law 95-575). It reaches the possession or sale of more than 60,000 cigarettes without state tax stamps, and its trafficking provision took effect about 1 March 1979 [verified in outline; the effective day is from memory].** It cut the traffic down [memory]. The exact 1975 and 1980 rates are not checked, so the Storyteller should treat the margin in 1975 as smaller and growing. **New Jersey's tobacco-tax collections run flat at $167M to $171M from 1975 to 1980, then rise from $177M in 1982 to $215M in 1983, which fits a rate rise in 1982 or 1983 [verified, FRED NJTOBACTAX; the rate itself is inferred].**

**Burglary, robbery, arson, insurance fraud.** The Life uses specialists and does not often do these itself. A skilled arsonist, a safecracker, a forger, a jewel thief, a document man is freelance and sells to whoever pays.

### 7.6 Narcotics

**Tendency [memory].** The Life's relation to narcotics is the most contradictory of all.

- Many old bosses hold that narcotics are a dirty business which brings long federal sentences. Several families forbid their made men to deal. This is a rule often cited in the Life and often broken. Which families forbid it, and for which drugs, is an Open per-family dial and not a global rule (Part V, section 9.2).
- Others take the money and keep their hands clean by lending it. A made man puts up cash to a trusted front man, gets a return when the shipment arrives, and never sees the product.
- Heroin is the old trade, from Sicilian and French connections. The "French Connection" was broken in 1972 and 1973 [memory], and Sicilian heroin through Canada and New York continues. The Pizza Connection case lies ahead: coordinated arrests in April 1984, a trial from September 1985 to March 1987, and prosecutors' claim of about $1.65 billion of heroin imported into the Northeast and Midwest from 1975 to 1984, distributed through pizza parlor fronts [verified].
- Marijuana is a young man's trade, mostly open and loosely organized.
- Cocaine in 1975 is expensive, small, and in the hands of Cubans, Colombians, and the rich. It grows through the decade. Crack arrives in Northeastern cities only in the mid 1980s (Part V).
- Prescription pills are a quiet business in the doctors' offices.

**Tendency.** The drug rule is applied with a weather eye. A man who deals in the neighborhood's own street, in front of the old women, is a disgrace. A man who finances a shipment through a front is "doing business." The Storyteller shows the elders' hypocrisy and the young zealot's disgust together, and lets them clash.

### 7.7 Vice

**Fixed.** Prostitution (the call girl, the massage parlor, the street, the hotel bar), pornography (peep shows, bookstores, film distribution; the film *Deep Throat* of 1972 was financed by Anthony Peraino, a member of the Colombo family, whose son produced it [verified]), strip clubs, after-hours bars. The Strip's economy depends on it.

### 7.8 Laundering and Fronts

**Fixed.** Cash must be made respectable.

- **The front business.** A pizzeria, bakery, bar, nightclub, laundromat, jukebox route, funeral home, parking lot, or car dealership puts dirty cash into its register as sales. The more cash the business naturally handles, the better. The fronts are taxable and audited, and an unexplained rise in sales brings the IRS.
- **The skim.** The reverse trick: untaxed cash taken from the top of a legal business before it is counted. The profit is large and the risk is a federal tax case.
- **Banks.** Since the Bank Secrecy Act of 1970, banks file reports on cash transactions above $10,000 (Part I, section 9). Careful men deposit smaller sums and use friendly bankers.
- **Real estate, restaurants, and the long game.** The sons go to college and go into law, medicine, and construction. The money is made clean by being made old.

**Tendency.** A man's wealth in the Life is a tangle of hidden cash, front income, borrowed money, and promises. It cannot be reduced to a single figure, and the Storyteller does not tell the player one.

## 8. Violence and Its Handling

The Storyteller narrates violence at the level of a good novel (Part II, section 0): real physical weight, real consequence, no skipping and no wallowing, and no instruction.

### 8.1 Why and When

**Tendency.** Violence in the Life is not constant. A boss would rather talk, bribe, wait, or tax. Killing is expensive, hard to hide, and brings the police and the federal government. It is used when:

- A man has **informed** or is believed to have informed.
- A man has **stolen** from the Family or **held out**.
- A man has **killed or struck** a made man without leave.
- A man has **disrespected** a boss or a made man in a way that cannot be taken back.
- A **rival** is in the way and cannot be bought or frightened.
- A **debtor** has defied the lender.
- A man **knows too much** and is about to talk.
- A **message** is needed for others to hear.

Beatings, threats, burnt cars, broken windows, a dead dog on a doorstep, and a few words in a crowded room are cheaper than killing and often work.

### 8.2 Sanction

**Tendency.** A killing of a made man, or of anyone important, needs the **boss's approval**, given through the underboss or the capo. A man who kills without leave may be killed for it. A killing of an associate is a matter between his made man and the capo. A killing of a civilian is a matter of the crew's own judgment, and a mistake is expensive. Reality is looser than the rule. Men do kill without leave, and the consequences depend on the act (section 9).

### 8.3 Who Does It

**Tendency.** Most men in the Life never kill. A few do, and these are valued, feared, and kept at a distance. Boss and underboss use trusted shooters. A capo may have his own crew of "workers." A shooter is often a man of otherwise quiet life, a family man, a church attender. Some enjoy it and are dangerous to their own side. Some do it with a numbed calm. Some are broken by it. The Storyteller treats each killer as an individual (Part II, section 0.3).

### 8.4 The Weight

**Tendency.** In the Life, a killing is not a trauma and it is not nothing. A man may eat his dinner afterward. A man may be unable to. A man may go to confession, or light a candle, or send flowers to the widow, or sleep badly for a month, or never think of it again. Whatever he does, the Storyteller shows his reaction as his own, drawn from his character, his age, and what he has already done.

**Tendency.** The victim's death has weight too. A widow, a mother who knows, a brother who will want revenge, a priest who is asked to bury him, a neighborhood that goes quiet. A killing sends ripples through a family and a street, and the Storyteller follows them.

### 8.5 Torture and Interrogation

**Tendency.** The Life does not usually torture for sport. It sometimes does so to find a man, a sum, a name, or a betrayal, and the Storyteller gives the scene its full weight: pain that is physical and ugly, a man who is a person, a questioner who may be calm, angry, or sick, and a result that is uncertain, since a man in agony says what he thinks his questioner wants. The scene is written as a novel would write it. It is neither skipped nor loved.

### 8.6 After

**Tendency.** What is done with a body is a decision with consequences.

- A body **found** is a message. It brings the police, the press, and the family, and tells the world a thing is done.
- A body **hidden** is a disappearance. It brings fewer police, but rumor fills the hole and a body that does not stay hidden is worse.
- Cars, rivers, quarries, pits, fires, and the building trades have all been used, and the Storyteller may use the ones that fit the scene without describing any step-by-step.
- **The legal reality** (Part II, section 6): a hidden body does not mean no case. Informants, circumstantial evidence, and a patient prosecutor can make one.

### 8.7 The Gun, the Knife, and the Rest

**Fixed.** The Life prefers the pistol or revolver, the shotgun, the garrote, the knife, the club, and the baseball bat. The submachine gun is a Prohibition image. Bombs and car bombs are rare in the mid 1970s, used more by the Irish, the bikers, and the arsonists, and become better known later [memory]. **Guns come from the bikers, the gun shows and the armorer, an **Open** role: whoever supplies a family's guns, invented when needed.**

## 9. Taboos: Acts and Their Consequences

**Fixed.** Nothing in the Life is *impossible*, **with one exception: women are never made (section 11, Part I 12.2), and that rule carries no price.** There are acts that bring heavy consequences. A taboo is a fact of the world: some people will care very much if it is broken, and they will react in a range depending on who did it, who saw it, what the circumstances were, and what the offender is worth. **A taboo is never a wall that stops the player or an NPC.** It is a price.

| Act | Who cares most | Typical reaction range | Who can excuse it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Killing a police officer** | Every cop; the Commissioner; the FBI; the Mayor; the bosses, who want no heat | from a sudden law-enforcement storm, with raids, arrests, and the Pad suspended, to the killer being delivered to the police or killed by his own Family | the boss, if the officer was a threat; never the police |
| **Killing a reporter** | The press, the public, the Mayor, the Governor, the federal government | from a front-page outcry and a task force to quiet if the victim was minor and the story is buried | the boss, rarely |
| **Killing a made man without sanction** | His capo, his Family, the boss | from a death sentence on the killer to a payment and a quiet | the boss of the dead man's Family |
| **Striking a made man** | The same | from a beating in return to a sit-down and an apology | the boss, the capo |
| **Killing a made man of another family** | Both bosses; the Iron Balance | war, a sit-down, a payment, or the killer's surrender | both bosses |
| **Holding out** (hiding a score, shorting the envelope) | The capo, the boss | a beating, a fine, a demotion, shelving, or death if the sum is large | the capo, the boss |
| **Informing** | Every man in the Life; the Family | death, a hunt across the country, a curse on the family | none; a boss may let an informant live out of calculation, but only for his own reasons |
| **Dealing narcotics where the Family forbids it** | The boss, the older capos | warning, loss of status, shelving, death | the boss, who may be in the trade himself |
| **A made man's wife or daughter** (adultery, seduction, violence) | The man, his Family | from a killing to a beating to a quiet divorce | the offended man |
| **Violence in the Oakridge sanctuary** | All four bosses | harsh, immediate: the offender is delivered to the injured party | the bosses, together |
| **Violence in a church or at a wake** | All; the Church | disgrace, a ban from the Family, a death | the boss |
| **Publicly disrespecting a boss** | The boss, his men, the rival family | a beating, a payment, a public apology, death if the insult is deep and the boss is proud | the boss |
| **Touching a civilian woman or child** | The Family, as a matter of honor | contempt, expulsion, death; but the Life has men who do it | the boss |
| **Betraying a *compare*** | The sponsor, the sponsor's family | the worst vengeance the Family knows | none |
| **Lying to a boss** | The boss | depends on the lie | the boss |

**Tendency.** The reactions are individual. A boss with a taste for ceremony may want a killer's family to watch. A pragmatic boss may fine him. A boss who loves the offender may exile him instead. The man who learns of the act may have his own reasons to care or not to care. The simulation lets each decide.

**Anchor [verified].** On 2 June 1976, a reporter in Phoenix, Arizona, Don Bolles, was hit by a remote-controlled car bomb while investigating organized crime, and died on 13 June. The reaction was a national press effort, the Arizona Project of Investigative Reporters and Editors, which put 38 journalists from 28 newsrooms on his work and published a 23-part series in 1977. This is the consequence a reporter's murder brings: the killing buys not silence but a hundred reporters.

**Tendency.** The same holds for police. A cop killed makes every cop an investigator. The Life knows it.

## 10. The Mixed Men Inside the Family

**Fixed.** The old codex's four molds are discarded (Part II, section 0.3). A man's relation to the Life is a bundle of separate obligations, each held to its own degree. The Family's reaction to a failure depends on which obligation is failed.

| Obligation | What it asks | The Family's reaction to failure |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Loyalty to the compare and crew** | to stand with one's sponsor and men | the worst offence; vengeance |
| **2. Care of widows and men in prison** | to pay the envelope and keep faith | grumbling at first; ruinous to morale over time; the "slow rot" |
| **3. Silence before the law** | to say nothing | death for failure |
| **4. Honesty toward other made men** | to not cheat them | war, a sit-down, or the offender's shelving |
| **5. Respect for the boss and the ritual** | to defer and to kiss the hand | contempt if neglected; ruinous only in a proud family |
| **6. One's own limits** (on drugs, women, children, kinds of violence) | to hold the line a man sets himself | his own conscience, his friends' judgment |
| **7. Faith and family** | to keep the Church, wife, and children | a man's private matter; gossip |
| **8. His word on a deal** | to keep what he says | loss of trust |

**Tendency.** Every man is weak on some of these. The Storyteller never tells the player a man's whole standing in advance. The player learns it by watching him.

**Tendency (change).** A man's stance on each obligation is a state, not a seed. It moves:

- Toward **harder**: after a death, a betrayal that cost him dearly, a priest, a brush with prison, the birth of a son, a boss who showed faith.
- Toward **softer**: after a long prison term, a sickness, a family quarrel, a broken promise by the Family, the death of a friend.
- Toward **cynicism**: after seeing the code used as a weapon against a loyal man.
- **Back to belief**: after seeing money wreck a crew and a family.

A young believer may become the coldest cynic in the room. An old cynic may go to Mass every day in the year before he dies. The Storyteller remembers who the man was.

**Fixed.** The Family reacts to a failure not by a rule but by the persons who learn of it. A boss may forgive a man who is useful. A capo may crush a man he never liked. A soldier may stand up for a friend who is going down. Everyone else watches.

## 11. Women in the Life

Section 12 of Part I is the base. These are the underworld's own details.

**Fixed (Part I 12.2).** Women are not made. They are not capos, soldiers, or consiglieri. The Life is a man's world, and women are kept out of it by custom and by the men's wish.

**Fixed.** And yet nothing in the Life works without them.

- **The wife** runs the house, rears the children, keeps the family's face before the Church and the neighbors, and often knows more than she says. In a crisis she is the lion.
- **The mother** is the great authority. A boss defers to his mother in private, and in some cases in public. A mother who disapproves of an act can sometimes stop it.
- **The widow** is the Life's conscience. The Family's treatment of her is the standard by which the Family is judged. By convention the capo pays a weekly envelope when a man is in prison or dead. In practice the sums shrink with time, and the "slow rot" of the envelope is a leading reason a man in a cell decides to talk.
- **The sister, the daughter.** Their marriages are alliances. The Life arranges the weddings of its daughters with care and uses them to tie families together.
- **The *comare* (mistress)** is a recognised fact. A made man's second household is tolerated and managed. A mistress who talks is a danger, and one who is neglected may talk.
- **The woman outside the Life** who works in it: the bookkeeper, the bank teller, the funeral director, the restaurant owner, the travel agent, the court clerk, the nurse, the hairdresser, the numbers banker's wife who actually does the sums. Their power is real and unspoken.
- **The informer.** A wife, a mistress, or a mother may inform, out of love, fear, or revenge. It happens rarely, and it is catastrophic.

**Rule of Plausibility.** (Part I, section 12.4) Women gain power in the Life through four routes only: **family** (a widow who holds a husband's business, a mother with a boss son), **the Church**, **small business**, and **institutions that are already female-dominated** (the beauty parlor, the parish society, the hospital's nursing staff). A woman may be a figure to fear in these places. She may not become a capo.

## 12. Daily Life and the Course of a Man's Years

### 12.1 The Social Club

**Fixed.** The Family's rooms are the **social and athletic clubs** of the Hill and the other neighborhoods: a storefront with the windows painted over or curtained, a name on the glass (the *Vesuvio Social and Athletic Club*, the *Pompeii*), a bocce court out back, an espresso machine, a card table, a television set for the fights and the ball games, a back room for the men who matter. Women do not enter.

**Tendency.** Cards are scopa, briscola, pinochle, and poker. Men talk about the neighborhood, the horses, the fights, the Yankees (or the local teams), the Church, and the dead. The business is done in the back or on the sidewalk. The bugs are in the walls and the men know it. The clubs are also the pensioners' rooms of the old men, and an old man who plays cards in a club all day may be nothing, or may be everything.

### 12.2 The Boy Becomes a Man

**Tendency.** The typical path:

1. A **neighborhood boy** in an Italian parish hangs on a corner. He runs errands for a man in the club, takes numbers slips, carries a package.
2. At fifteen to twenty he is an **associate**. He is "put with" a made man, which means that man takes responsibility for him. Without that, he is nobody.
3. He **earns**. He books, loan-sharks, hijacks, collects. He passes up his share.
4. He **proves himself**: silent when arrested, steady under threat, willing to do a hard thing when asked. In the old families, to have killed.
5. The sponsor **proposes** him. The capo and the boss consider. If the books are open and the Family approves, he is **made**.
6. As a soldier he builds a crew, kicks up, and waits. A few become capos.
7. A very few become bosses. Most die as soldiers or in prison.

**Tendency.** Boys from outside the Italian Catholic neighborhoods may rise as associates, and not further.

### 12.3 The Old Man's Years

**Tendency.** A made man who lives past fifty has seen the Life change twice. He may have outlived his friends, his sponsor, and his enemies. He may sit in a club, tend a garden, or play with grandchildren. He may be shelved, retired, or in prison. He may be a boss at seventy. His influence depends on his friends and on what he is thought to know.
