# Saint Jude: The Setting Bible

## Part I: The City

Baseline: the start of 1975. Dated items after that are **Weather**. Section 0 of Part II (the Period Mind) governs everything here.

---

## 0. How to Read This Bible

### 0.1 Fixed, Open, and Weather

Every section marks what is settled and what is not.

- **Fixed:** established fact. It cannot be contradicted without a cause inside the world (a death, a sale, a fire, a repeal).
- **Open:** the Storyteller and the simulation may invent here freely, as long as the invention fits what is Fixed, the date, and the money. A name, a face, a shop, a feud, an owner can all be Open.
- **Weather:** a standing condition of the period (a falling tax base, a new law coming, a market shifting). Weather is not a plot. It pushes on many people at once and each person answers it in their own way. Weather has dates and rough strength. It never has an ending.
- **Anchor:** a real-world fact that the setting borrows. Anchors are accurate to the real world and give the Storyteller something true to lean on.

Everything that is not an Anchor is invented for Saint Jude.

### 0.2 The Five Tiers of Truth

This keeps the old codex's scheme, because it is good. **The five tiers say how binding a statement is. The tags in 0.1 say how to treat it, and the marks [verified], [memory, to verify] and Design say where a figure came from. One statement can carry all three.**

1. **Established Canon.** Objective fact about places, institutions and history. Cannot be contradicted without cause.
2. **Institutional Convention.** A strong shared custom backed by self-interest. It can be broken. Breaking it has consequences that come from the people who learn of it.
3. **Operational Tendency.** What people usually do because it is efficient or safe. Exceptions are common.
4. **Street Belief.** What people say is true. It may be wrong, and acting on it can hurt them.
5. **Ordinary Civic Reality.** The life of a million people who are mostly not in a conspiracy. This tier is not decoration. It is most of the city.

### 0.3 What Is Not In This Bible

There are no scripted plots, no named secrets, and no fixed crises. Characters, rackets and feuds named in this Part are starting material or examples. Where an example is given it is labelled **Example**, and the Storyteller may use it, change it, or ignore it.

### 0.4 Dates

1 January 1975 is the **simulation seed**: the state of the world from which the engine runs forward. The default player entry is **1980**, after five simulated years. A story may open at any later date, and the simulation will have run the city forward to it. Anything dated after 1975 in this Part is **Weather**, not history, and the run decides which of it lands in Saint Jude and how.

### 0.5 Standing Principles

These govern the whole bible and the Storyteller. The full text is in Part II, section 0.

1. **Period plausibility.** People think, speak, and judge as people of 1975 and after would, including about death, violence, race, sex, and the law. Nothing is added because it is fashionable now, and no modern morality is applied to the characters.
2. **Plain period attitudes.** Bigotry and other period attitudes are written as the characters' own, unsoftened. Writing them is not endorsing them.
3. **The romantic version is allowed.** The movie-mafia version of loyalty, code, and honor lives alongside the real money-grubbing one.
4. **Mixed men.** No one is wholly good or wholly bad. Each man holds each obligation to his own degree.
5. **People change.** A man's stance moves with events, in either direction.
6. **No steering.** The Storyteller does not push the player toward legitimate business, skip or sanitize crime, or make characters react out of the narrator's own disapproval. Reactions belong to the individual character.
7. **Novel-level detail.** Violence and crime carry real physical and emotional weight, as in a novel, without step-by-step how-to.

## 1. The Name and the City's Voice

### 1.1 What People Call It

The city's name is **Saint Jude**. In print it is spelled out. People who live there say "Jude": *down in Jude, a Jude cop, I'm from Jude.* Visitors who say "the Saint" are marked as visitors.

The police are the **SJPD**. The county is **Killbuck County**, and the city and the county are one government.

### 1.2 Jude's Day

The Feast of Saints Simon and Jude falls on **28 October** (Anchor). It is the city's patron day and a local holiday, with a procession from the cathedral in Kerrytown. Banks and the courts close at noon.

### 1.3 The Motto and the Joke

The city seal carries a Latin motto, *In rebus desperatis spes*, "hope in desperate things". Residents know the saint is the patron of lost causes and enjoy it. A common local line: *Nothing's lost in Jude for good, it just changes hands.* Visitors who ask about it are told the city is proud of its saint and a little ashamed of its sense of humor.

### 1.4 Bellacorte

**Bellacorte** is an old Italianate quarter inside the city, north-west of downtown. It is a district, not a nickname for the whole. The name comes from the *corte*, a stone courtyard built in 1884 as the exchange and meeting hall of the Genoese shipping houses. See section 5.4.

## 2. How Saint Jude Began

### 2.1 The Refuge on the Killbuck

**Fixed.** Saint Jude began as a small Roman Catholic settlement on the south bank of the Killbuck River, a few miles from the sea, in the 1640s. A band of Catholic families and one chaplain, driven out of other colonies where Catholic worship was forbidden or only tolerated, took land from a chartered grant and settled where the river bends. It was a refuge in the sense Maryland was a refuge for English Catholics (Anchor), though much smaller and much poorer.

Across the river, on the north bank where the water was deeper, the **Dutch** kept a trading post. The Dutch word for a creek is *kil* (Anchor: Catskill, Peekskill). The river's name comes from the Dutch trading name for the stream, *Bokkenkil*, goat creek, from a herd the traders let run on the north bank. English speakers made it Killbuck. This etymology is invented for Saint Jude.

The Dutch post was called *Fort Kroon* and, after the English took the Dutch colonies in 1664 (Anchor), was renamed **Crown Harbor** for the Crown. The two settlements faced each other across the river for two centuries. The north bank became commerce. The south bank stayed faith.

### 2.2 Why Jude

**Fixed.** The settlers dedicated their first chapel to **Saint Jude**, the apostle. For centuries Jude was the neglected apostle, because his name was confused with Judas Iscariot and so few people prayed to him (Anchor: the confusion and the neglect are the traditional account of why Jude became the patron of desperate cases). The settlers chose him on purpose. They were a neglected people in a poor place and the choice was about themselves.

Tradition says the settlement nearly ended in its second winter, with food gone and the chaplain ill, and that it survived on a catch of fish no one could explain. People in Kerrytown still tell it. No one in Saint Jude can say whether it happened.

### 2.3 The Chapel Becomes the Cathedral

**Fixed.** The original chapel stood where **St. Jude's Cathedral** stands today in Kerrytown. The present cathedral was built from 1868 to 1884 on the same site, in brownstone and granite, and the chapel's foundation stones lie under the north transept. When the Archdiocese of Saint Jude was erected in the nineteenth century, St. Jude's became its cathedral.

### 2.4 The Waves

**Fixed.** The city grew in layers. Each layer built its own parish.

- **Portuguese fishermen** came in the early 1800s from the Azores and the mainland, fishing out of the north bank below the Point. They settled **Pescadores Row** and built a church of their own.
- **The Irish** came in the 1840s and after the Famine in great numbers. They took the south bank, the docks and the old parish. Kerrytown is theirs. They inherited St. Jude's, and with it the dock hiring.
- **The Germans** and **the English and Scots** came through the nineteenth century into trade, brewing, banking, and the Protestant churches downtown.
- **Genoese and Ligurian merchants** came from the 1850s as shipping agents and importers. They built the corte and the quarter around it.
- **The Italians**, mostly from the south, came from the 1880s until the doors closed in 1924 (Anchor). They took the hill north of downtown and called it San Lorenzo. They built **St. Rocco's** because the old parish was Irish and did not want them. That quiet rivalry between the cathedral and St. Rocco's, between the Irish docks and the Italian hill, is inherited by the families who live there now.
- **Black Americans** came north in the Great Migration from about 1916, from Virginia and the Carolinas, and a smaller older community was already there. They built the Eastside.
- **Jews** came from the 1880s, mostly from Eastern Europe, into the garment trade and the retail streets.
- **Poles**, **Ukrainians**, and **Lithuanians** came for the mills and the stockyards.
- **Puerto Ricans** came from the late 1940s into the rooming houses of the Eastside and the Point.
- **Soviet Jewish emigrants** begin to arrive after 1971. They are a small, new community in 1975.

## 3. Land, Water, and Weather

### 3.1 The River and the Sea

**Fixed.** The **Killbuck River** rises in the hills of inner Marsh County about thirty miles inland and runs south-east to the Atlantic. Its lower course widens into **the Basin**, a deep tidal harbor about four miles long, and then narrows to **the Narrows**, a channel about a mile wide between two low headlands. The Narrows is the mouth. Beyond it is open ocean.

The tide runs strong. The river is tidal as far as the falls, twenty-two miles up. Ice sometimes fouls the Basin in the hardest winters.

### 3.1a Where Saint Jude Sits on the Real Map

**Fixed (decision).** Saint Jude is a fictional city set into the real State of **New Jersey**, on the central Atlantic coast. It stands where the northern Monmouth County shore stands in the real world, from the Sandy Hook peninsula (our Brighton Point) south and inland along the bayshore. In this world the city occupies that ground, as Gotham or Springfield occupy a place on a map that has no such city. The real towns there do not exist: Highlands, Atlantic Highlands, Middletown, Keansburg, Hazlet, Union Beach, Keyport, Aberdeen and Matawan on the bayshore; Sea Bright, Monmouth Beach, Long Branch, West Long Branch, Oceanport, Eatontown, Deal, Allenhurst, Loch Arbour, Asbury Park, Ocean Township, Neptune, Neptune City, Bradley Beach, Avon-by-the-Sea and Belmar on the shore; Red Bank, Little Silver, Fair Haven, Rumson, Shrewsbury, Tinton Falls, Interlaken and Holmdel along the river and inland; Freehold, Marlboro, Colts Neck, Manalapan and Englishtown in the inner Monmouth country; and Old Bridge, Sayreville and South Amboy in Middlesex. The Storyteller may borrow their names for neighborhoods. Real places beyond that ground, such as Perth Amboy, New Brunswick, Edison and Wall, exist as they are. This is ordinary practice in fiction.

**Design (size).** The city covers about 105 square miles of land (range 90 to 130), about 10,500 residents per square mile in 1975. That matches Baltimore, the civic model (about 850,000 people on about 80 square miles in the mid 1970s [memory, to verify]). It is far denser than the real towns it replaces, which held a fraction of 1.1 million [memory, to verify]. That is a deliberate fictional choice.

**Fixed.** The suburban counties are the real ground around it: **Marsh County** is the inner Monmouth and Middlesex country to the west and north-west, **Shore County** is the Ocean County coast to the south (Point Pleasant and the beach towns are real places here), and **Pine County** is the Pine Barrens inland to the south-west. The State capital is **Trenton**, about an hour inland to the west and south-west, which replaces the "two hours" of the first draft.

**Naming rule.** Real county and town names appear in this bible only in [verified] history. Inside Saint Jude's footprint (the northern Monmouth shore and bayshore, and the inner Monmouth and Middlesex country) real municipalities do not exist. The Storyteller and the engine use Marsh, Shore and Pine counties and the city's own neighborhoods, never place a real town inside a fictional district, and never assume a real town survives where the bible has replaced it. Real places outside the footprint (Newark, Elizabeth, Trenton, Atlantic City, Point Pleasant, Toms River and the like) exist as they are.

**Fixed (approximate, [memory]).** Newark and the Port of Newark and Elizabeth lie about 35 to 40 miles to the north, New York City about 45 to 50, Philadelphia about 65 to 70 to the south-west, and Atlantic City about 65 to the south.

**Fixed [verified].** Containerization began at Port Newark on 26 April 1956 (the *Ideal X*), and the Elizabeth Marine Terminal opened on **15 August 1962 [verified, Wikipedia, Container port]**. The big shipping lines chose Newark and Elizabeth first. Saint Jude's Gannet Terminal, opened in 1968, is therefore a second-tier container port, a choice that suits a city in decline.

**Fixed [verified].** The State has a real **State Commission of Investigation** (**created in 1968, working from 1 January 1969**, with subpoena power and public hearings, but no power to prosecute). Saint Jude's State Commission in Part II is that body. Governor **Brendan Byrne** held office from 15 January 1974 to 19 January 1982, and the real State officials appear in the news as offstage figures. The Storyteller replaces them as the simulation runs. The officials who sit in Saint Jude itself (the Mayor, the Police Commissioner, the Killbuck County Prosecutor, the FBI's Special Agent in Charge) are ours to invent.

**Fixed.** The real New Jersey mob already holds ground around Saint Jude, and Part III, section 6.2, sets out who. Saint Jude's four families are an addition to that map, the way a fifth borough would be.

**Weather.** The Atlantic City casino question is the real one: New Jersey voters approve casino gambling in November 1976 and Resorts opens on 26 May 1978 [verified]. It is confined to Atlantic City, about 65 miles south.

### 3.2 A Map in Words

Orient to the sea. East is the Atlantic. The river comes in from the west.

**North bank, from the sea going upriver:**

1. **Killbuck Point.** The headland on the north side of the Narrows. The North Piers.
2. **Pescadores Row.** The old Portuguese fishing quarter at the neck of the Point.
3. **The Eastside.** Behind the Point, going inland: the Racks (garment and wholesale), the rail yards and produce terminal, the housing projects, and the Black business and church corridor of **Dunmore Avenue**.
4. **Crown Harbor.** The downtown, at the head of the Basin.
5. **Bellacorte.** North-west of downtown on rising ground.
6. **San Lorenzo, the Hill.** North of downtown and Bellacorte.
7. **Mercer Avenue.** Running north and south on the east edge of San Lorenzo, between the Hill and the Eastside.
8. **The Heights.** The ridge in the north-west, inside the city line.
9. **Brighton Point.** A windswept peninsula on the Atlantic coast, north-east, joined to the Eastside by the Shore Parkway.

**South bank, from the sea going upriver:**

1. **The South Piers** and **Kerrytown**, with the cathedral.
2. **The Gannet Terminal**, the container port, on the South Flats.
3. **The I-95 freight corridor**, truck depots and rail sidings, linked to the Point by the **Narrows Bridge**.
4. **The Ironworks and Killbuck Flats**, going upriver on both banks, mostly south.

**Beyond the city:** **Oakridge** (north-west suburbs), **Greenwood** (west), **the Pines** (south-west, thirty miles), **Point Pleasant** and the Shore (south along the coast, forty minutes).

### 3.3 Ground

**Fixed.** The soil differs by district, and that matters to anyone who wants to hide something.

- **Crown Harbor, the Hill, Bellacorte:** rock close to the surface. Deep foundations go to **bedrock caissons**. This is why concrete work downtown is expensive, slow, and political.
- **The Flats and the Eastside lowlands:** marsh fill over soft ground, a century of dumping, slag, and industrial waste. Foundations are piles. The ground holds poison.
- **Kerrytown, the Point, the Gannet Terminal:** filled land at the water's edge.
- **The Pines:** sand and acid bog over a high water table. Graves dug in soft bog soil last, and the ground freezes hard in January.

### 3.4 Climate and Seasons

**Fixed.** A humid North-Atlantic coast. The ocean softens extremes without removing them.

- **Winter:** cold, wet, grey. Nor'easters arrive a few times a season. Ice on the Basin in the hard years.
- **Spring:** raw and short. Heavy rain, the first crowds on the Shore.
- **Summer:** hot and humid. Heat waves of a week or more, with the Hill and the Eastside much hotter than the Heights. Hydrants are opened.
- **Autumn:** the best weather and the busiest season on the docks. Hurricane season runs through October and sometimes reaches the coast.

### 3.5 The City's Ordinary Calendar

The city keeps a civil and religious calendar in addition to the criminal one.

- **January:** the new year's visits on the Hill; the Epiphany blessing of the waters at the Point (the Portuguese).
- **March 19:** the **Table of Saint Joseph**, the Sicilian feast, on the Hill (altars of bread and food for the poor).
- **Lent and Easter:** fish on Fridays in every parish, a heavy week for the fish market and the bakeries.
- **June:** the Blessing of the Fleet at Pescadores Row. School lets out.
- **July 1:** the municipal fiscal year begins. Layoffs and budgets land here.
- **August 16:** the **Feast of San Rocco** on the Hill, with a procession, bands, and bills pinned on the statue.
- **Labor Day to October:** the port's high season.
- **October 28:** Jude's Day.
- **November:** Election Day (the first Tuesday after the first Monday).
- **December:** the hijack season on the highways; envelopes due before Christmas.

## 4. The City, the County, and the State

### 4.1 The Shape of the Metropolis

**Fixed.** The **metropolitan area** has about **2.4 million** people. The **City of Saint Jude** proper, which is also **Killbuck County**, has about **1.1 million** in 1975, down from a peak of 1.35 million in 1950 and still falling (Weather: the decline continues through the decade).

**Design (outside the city).** The metropolitan area outside the city, about 1.3 million people in 1975, is not split by county in this bible. The engine holds it as one suburban remainder, and its birth, infant-death and age figures follow the 1975 United States rates until a revision of this Part sets them.

**Design (population path).** The city loses about 0.8 percent of its people a year from 1975 (range 0.4 to 1.5), which is about 1.06 million in 1980, 1.02 million in 1985 and 0.98 million in 1990. The rate continues the 1950 to 1975 decline (1.35 million to 1.1 million). Those who leave are mostly working and middle class; the poor, the old and the newest arrivals stay. The engine draws the rate once and holds it unless the run moves it.

**Tendency (reversal).** The run may slow, stop or reverse the fall, but only for a cause it can date: a port or industrial revival, a wave of new arrivals, a building or casino boom, a fall in crime, a city that pays its bills. The rate moves toward its new value by at most a third of a point a year, because people move over years and not at once. Before 1990 growth is capped at 0.5 percent a year. Newark, Baltimore, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Detroit, St. Louis and Buffalo all lost people between 1980 and 1990 [verified, HUD, State of the Cities 1999, table 9], so a reversal before 1990 is rare and should be earned. Who arrives matters as much as how many: new arrivals to the Eastside and the blocks (Part V) change the city differently from a return to the Heights. The 1975 metropolitan total of 2.4 million is Fixed. Only its later path is Open, and the suburbs take most of those who leave the city.

The city is close to Baltimore in shape (Anchor, as a model): an old port on a tidal river, row houses, ethnic neighborhoods, heavy industry on the waterfront, a Catholic origin, and a city government that is also its own county. The mob geography in the codex was modelled on Newark, Boston and Philadelphia. The civic geography follows Baltimore.

The city is surrounded by suburban counties.

- **Marsh County** (west and north-west): Oakridge, Greenwood, and the commuter towns.
- **Pine County** (south-west): the Pines and the outer exurbs.
- **Shore County** (south along the coast): Point Pleasant and the beach towns.

**Open.** The suburbs, their politics, and their police are available for invention.

### 4.2 The State

**Fixed.** The city lies in the real State of **New Jersey** (section 3.1a), an industrial, urbanized Mid-Atlantic state with its legislature at Trenton. The State has a **State Police**, an **Attorney General**, a **State Racing Commission** (which governs Bayview Downs), an **Alcoholic Beverage Control** board (which licenses every bar), and a **State Lottery** (see section 9.8).

**Open.** The State's officials after January 1975, and everything the simulation decides about State politics.

### 4.3 City Government

**Fixed.** Saint Jude has a **strong-mayor** government.

- **The Mayor** is elected every four years. The mayor appoints the police commissioner, the fire chief, the heads of Buildings and Inspections, Sanitation, Public Works, and the Law Department. The mayor sets the budget.
- **The City Council** has fifteen members, elected by ward. Ward leaders control precinct captains, who control the vote. Councilmen control zoning votes, paving, and license approvals within their wards.
- **The Board of Public Works** (three members, two appointed by the mayor and one elected citywide) awards every major city contract: dredging, bridge repair, paving, school construction. This board is where the Concrete Club and its competitors do business.
- **The Zoning Board** and **the Department of Buildings and Inspections** control what can be built and what it costs to build it. An inspector can close a job site in an hour.
- **The Licensing Board** issues taxi medallions, peddler permits, and entertainment licenses. The State ABC board separately licenses liquor.
- **The Municipal Court** (traffic, misdemeanors, preliminary hearings) and **the Superior Court** (felony trials, civil suits) are courts that sit in the city. **Superior Court judges are appointed by the Governor with the consent of the State Senate, for an initial term of seven years and, if reappointed, until age 70 [verified]. Municipal Court judges are appointed by the city [memory, to verify]. A judgeship is not won at the polls. It is won through the state senators whose districts lie inside the county and the county party chairman, who tell the Governor's counsel whom the party wants, so every judgeship still has a sponsor. The Senate has been elected from 40 single-senator districts since 1973 [verified], so a city of this size holds several seats (Design: about six, range four to eight) and no single senator speaks for it.**
- **The County Prosecutor** for Killbuck County is **appointed by the Governor with the consent of the Senate, for a five-year term [verified]**. This is the office that prosecutes murder, robbery, and assault. It is also the office that decides which cases of police or union corruption to bring. **The Prosecutor serves at the pleasure of the Governor's party as much as the county's, and a change of Governor can change the office.**
- **The Port Authority** is a separate public body (see 8.1). It controls the Narrows Bridge, the Killbuck Tunnel, the Gannet Terminal, the airport, and its own police.

### 4.4 The Machine

**Fixed.** The city has been run for a century by a Democratic machine. In 1975 the machine is old, tired, and still functioning. It was Irish through most of the twentieth century. The Italians have been a rising force since the 1950s and the Black community's share of power is growing against stubborn resistance. Reform candidates win about one election in three.

The machine works as follows. A ward leader finds jobs, cases of coal (now, cases of Scotch), and favors for his people. Those people deliver the vote. In return the party gives the ward leader city jobs to hand out. City jobs, including police, fire, sanitation, and clerks, are the machine's currency. That is why public-employee unions are powerful here.

**Weather.** In the mid-1970s the machine's money is running out. The tax base is falling, federal aid is shrinking, and the city is borrowing to cover operating costs.

### 4.5 The City's Money Problem

**Weather, strong, beginning 1975.** Saint Jude is in the same trouble as other old Northeastern cities. The tax base has shrunk as factories close and middle-class families move to the suburbs. Debt is high. In 1975, cities across the Northeast are laying off police, fire, and sanitation workers and cutting services (Anchor). New York's fiscal crisis of 1975 is the famous case.

For Saint Jude this means, by default:

- Hiring freezes at the SJPD and the Fire Department.
- Bridge, school, and street maintenance falling behind.
- A mayor desperate for private building and a bond market that does not trust the city.
- Poorly paid civil servants, with all that implies for the Pad.
- Developers who can say "no one else will build here."

**Open.** How hard the crisis bites, whether the city defaults, which services are cut, and who profits from the cuts.

### 4.6 Elections

**Fixed.** Mayoral elections are in odd-numbered years on a four-year cycle, the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November, with a party primary in September. The 1975 mayoral election falls on Day One's calendar year (Open: who runs, who wins). Council stands on its own cycle. **The County Prosecutor and the judges are appointed, not elected (section 4.3).** State and federal elections follow the national calendar.

## 5. The Districts

Saint Jude has eight **districts** (the old codex's list, expanded), each containing several **neighborhoods**. A district is a unit of identity and of rackets. A neighborhood is a unit of daily life.

### 5.1 District One: Killbuck Point and the Piers

**Character.** The working port. The port straddles the Narrows: the North Piers lie on the headland of the north bank and the South Piers lie across the water below Kerrytown (section 6.1). "Down the Point" means either. Cranes, warehouses, tugboat yards, ship chandlers, cold storage, a customs house, and a long strip of bars where seamen and longshoremen drink. The air smells of diesel, wet rope, and fish.

**The neighborhoods.**

- **The North Piers (Piers 1 to 6).** The old finger piers on the north bank. In 1975 they are half dead. **Pier 4** and **Pier 6** were rebuilt as container piers in 1971 and are the only parts that make money. The others handle breakbulk (crated goods, coffee in sacks, cars) with gangs of men, and lose trade every year to containers.
- **Pescadores Row.** The old Portuguese fishing quarter. Narrow streets, azulejo-tiled fronts, three bakeries, two fish markets, a church (**Our Lady of the Sea**), and a club. The fleet of about forty boats is the last working fleet in the harbor. The Portuguese keep to themselves and are underestimated.
- **The Fish Market.** The wholesale seafood market on the north bank is a family-controlled monopoly, worth about as much as a small steel mill.

**The people.** Longshoremen (mostly Italian on the north bank, mostly Irish on the south), tugboat crews, checkers and clerks, customs brokers, chandlers, Portuguese fishermen, union stewards, and bar owners.

**The economy.** Cargo handling, warehousing, fishing, ship repair, customs brokerage, and the union hiring that controls all of it.

**The institutions.** Local 402 of the Longshoremen (north bank). The Killbuck Waterfront Commission's field office. The Customs House. The Harbor Police. The Pier 4 Diner, which opens at 4 a.m.

**Underworld.** The **Pellegrino family** is strongest here. See Part III.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **Containerization.** One crew with a crane unloads a ship in a day that took a hundred men a week. The union's share of hiring falls year by year. The fight over who gets the remaining jobs, and who gets paid for jobs that no longer exist, is the central labor problem of the port.
- **The Waterfront Commission** registers workers and can strike a man off the register. That makes it a prize to capture, a body to evade, and a source of leverage.
- **Pilferage and hijacking.** A container is a sealed box that can be opened later, somewhere else. Containers change how theft works but do not stop it.

**Open.** Everything about named people, names of ships, the state of the individual piers, whether the fleet survives.

### 5.2 District Two: Crown Harbor

**Character.** The downtown. Art Deco towers from the 1920s, a few glass boxes from the 1960s, department stores losing customers to suburban malls, steakhouses, banks, law firms, the federal complex. The Basin is on one side and the Hill rises on the other. At 6 p.m. the commuters leave and the streets empty.

**The neighborhoods.**

- **The Civic Center.** City Hall (1911), the Municipal Court, the Superior Court, the County Prosecutor's offices, the SJPD Headquarters, and the Hall of Records.
- **The Federal Complex.** The **Federal Building** (twelve floors, 1966), the **Federal Courthouse**, and the **federal detention center** (name **Open**; fourteen stories, opened 1974, joined to the courthouse by a tunnel). The FBI field office and the U.S. Attorney's Office are in the Federal Building.
- **The Financial Blocks.** **First Harbor National Bank** is the city's biggest bank, and there are four others. Insurance companies and brokers, and the law firms that serve them.
- **Steakhouse Row.** Four blocks of expensive restaurants where deals are made over lunch.
- **The Basin Front.** A line of old warehouses being converted, slowly, to offices.
- **Union Station.** The grand 1886 station, now half empty. Rail passenger service is shrinking.

**The people.** Lawyers, bankers, judges, civil servants, federal agents, journalists, reporters from both papers, hotel workers, shop clerks, and downtown's night population: bar staff, cab drivers, parking-garage workers.

**The economy.** Government, finance, law, insurance, retail, hotels, and construction (always construction).

**The institutions.** City Hall, the courts, the banks, **the Saint Jude Club** (the old WASP downtown club that does not admit Catholics until 1969 and admits few since), **the Chamber of Commerce**, and the newspapers' headquarters.

**Underworld.** The **Sabatini family** is strongest here in construction, bid-rigging, and the money world. The streets are neutral in the sense that violence here draws attention from everyone at once.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **Downtown is hollowing.** Offices are moving to the suburbs. Retail is dying. Property values are soft. Anyone who can finance a tower sees a chance, and anyone who can finance it with dirty money is an opportunity for those who have some.
- **Federal money.** Urban renewal and community-development grants pay for building. Whoever controls the contracts controls a lot.
- **The city's credit.** Whether banks will lend to a city that cannot pay its bills.

**Open.** Everything else.

### 5.3 District Three: San Lorenzo, the Hill

**Character.** The great Italian-American neighborhood. A district of brick row houses on steep streets, with small shops on the ground floor and families above them. Bakeries, pork stores, espresso bars, social clubs with painted-over windows, bocce courts in back lots, laundry on lines. The streets are very safe, and people are very watchful. Strangers are noticed within the hour.

**The neighborhoods.**

- **North Hill.** Older, better off. The Vesuvio, St. Rocco's, the main shops, the oldest families.
- **South Hill.** More working class, closer to the docks. Dockworkers' families and Pellegrino loyalists.
- **The East Slope.** Newer and rougher, bordering Mercer Avenue and the Eastside.
- **The Market.** An open-air pushcart and storefront market on Via Garibaldi (locals call it *the Via*), selling produce, cheese, fish, live poultry.

**The people.** Italians, mostly Southern in origin (Campanian, Sicilian, Calabrian, Abruzzese), with a Neapolitan majority. Three generations in a house. Many do not speak English at home. The grandchildren go to college or to the suburbs, and the Hill ages.

**The economy.** Small shops, the building trades (Hill men are bricklayers, hod carriers, plasterers, and laborers), the docks, public jobs, and the family businesses: bakeries, funeral homes, restaurants, tailors, shoe repair, import shops. A strong cash economy.

**The institutions.** St. Rocco's parish and school; the Sons of Italy lodge; the social clubs; the Feast of San Rocco committee; the Italian Credit Union; Mercy General Hospital (on the Hill's edge); and the Elevated line (see section 8).

**Underworld.** Home to the families. The social clubs on the Hill are the working offices of the mob: the **Vesuvio Social and Athletic Club** (North Hill), the **Pompeii** (East Slope, near the Racks), and others. Every family has a presence here. Treat this as the one district where the four families cannot avoid each other.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **Out-migration.** Young families leave for the suburbs. Houses are sold. The Hill's stock of men for the mob thins, and recruits come from further afield.
- **The feast economy.** San Rocco in August is the high point of the year.
- **The Elevated line** noise and the vibrations are a nuisance and a protection (see Gino's terrace).
- **Real estate.** A hill of row houses with old owners is a source of cheap property if you know who is about to die.

**Open.** Everything about named people, shops, and clubs.

### 5.4 District Four: Bellacorte

**Character.** The old Italianate quarter, north-west of downtown. The oldest Italian presence in the city and the most respectable. Brownstone terraces with arched windows and iron balconies, a few small palazzi, and in the center, **the Corte**: a courtyard of pale stone about two hundred feet on a side, enclosed by arcades, with a fountain at its center and a statue of Columbus (put up in 1892). On a spring evening the Corte is the most beautiful place in Saint Jude.

**History.** The Genoese and Ligurian shipping houses built the Corte in 1884 as their exchange and meeting hall. By 1900 it held the Italian consulate, the Banco di San Giorgio (long since merged), the Italian Hospital, a theater, and the offices of the Italian-language newspaper. The quarter's people were northern Italians, doctors and lawyers, shipping agents, importers, cafe owners, musicians. They looked down on the peasants from the south who came in the 1890s and built San Lorenzo on the hill above them. Both sides remember. The quarter's families are respectable in the way that old money is respectable. They do not say the word "mob."

**The people.** Old Italian-American professional families (Genoese, Piedmontese, Tuscan, some Neapolitan), lawyers and doctors, importers, opera and music teachers, old women who remember when the consul came to dinner. Younger families are leaving. The Corte's buildings are slowly converting to offices, restaurants, and apartments for the young and for retirees.

**The economy.** Professional services, importing and wine, restaurants, banking, art dealing, and real estate. Not poor, not rich. Old.

**The institutions.** The Corte and its arcades. **The Italian Hospital** (now a Catholic hospital, **St. Anne's**, merged 1957). **Teatro della Corte**, a 900-seat opera house now running second-rate touring productions and Italian films. **The Società Ligure**, a benevolent society. **Santa Maria della Corte**, the parish church. **The Italian Consulate**. A branch of First Harbor National.

**Underworld.** Not heavy. The old families have always held that the mob is a southern problem. But the mob needs lawyers, accountants, doctors, and restaurateurs, and the quarter supplies them. Many of the men who sit in the quarter's best restaurants on Fridays are guests of men who live elsewhere. Respectable Bellacorte is where dirty money goes to be cleaned.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **Gentrification before the word.** Cheap old buildings, a beautiful setting, and a young professional class looking for a place to live. The quarter will be bought piece by piece.
- **The class divide inside Italian Saint Jude.** The old northern families and the southern families of the Hill have never fully married or merged. Mob families who make money want houses here and are sometimes refused.
- **The Corte itself** is public property but is leased to a private association. A lease renewal is always political.

**Open.** Everything about named people and buildings.

### 5.5 District Five: Mercer Avenue (the Strip)

**Character.** A long boulevard of theaters, nightclubs, diners, boxing gyms, hotels, bars, and (increasingly) adult businesses. In the 1930s and 1940s it was the city's theater district. By 1975 the theaters are movie houses and the marquees advertise things that were not allowed ten years ago. At night the neon is bright and the street is crowded. At 5 a.m. it is empty and grey.

**The neighborhoods.**

- **The Theater Blocks.** Old vaudeville houses turned to cinemas and peep shows.
- **The Supper Club Row.** Larger clubs with floor shows and a dress code. **The Starlight Cabaret** is here. In 1975 it is a supper club with a house band and a Friday floor show, not a disco (disco does not take over until 1977 or later; the pink martini sign is older than the music).
- **The Motel Blocks.** Hourly motels at the north end near the Heights Road.
- **The Gym District.** Boxing gyms, cheap diners, and fight posters. The **Civic Arena** stands at the south end.

**The people.** Night workers: musicians, dancers, bouncers, waiters, bartenders, cab drivers, cooks. Hustlers, hookers, runaways. Gamblers. Cops. The ordinary families who live in apartments over the shops and do not go out at night.

**The economy.** Entertainment, liquor, sex, gambling, food, and cash. The most cash-heavy district in the city.

**The institutions.** The **14th Precinct**, on Mercer and Ninth, a soot-stained 1920s stationhouse. The Civic Arena. The Mercer Avenue Merchants' Association. Several churches that still hold daily Mass for the staff of the clubs.

**Underworld.** The **Vannucci family** is strongest here. Others have interests. Vice cops from the 14th take money from all of them.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **Decline and deregulation of vice.** Courts loosen obscenity rules in the 1970s. Peep shows, massage parlors, and adult theaters multiply. Money is in this.
- **Disco** (about 1977 onward) makes nightclubs very profitable.
- **The street is a stage.** Reputation is made and unmade here. See Part III 2, Courtesy and Ceremony.
- **Redevelopment threats.** Every few years a plan is floated to clear the Strip.

**Open.**

### 5.6 District Six: The Eastside

**Character.** East of the Hill and downtown, running to the sea. A district of many parts: the garment district, the rail yards and produce terminal, public housing, and the long commercial corridor that is the heart of Black Saint Jude. It was the city's gateway for waves of poorer arrivals and has been the city's poorest district since the 1950s.

**The neighborhoods.**

- **The Racks (Hunts Row).** The garment and wholesale district. Loft buildings of eight to twelve stories, full of sewing shops, furriers, fabric and button wholesalers, diamond and gold dealers on one block, and dress factories. Clothing racks roll through the streets on freight dollies all day. Controlled by union locals of the garment workers and the truckers who carry the goods.
- **The Rail Yards and Produce Terminal.** A vast freight yard and a produce market where trucks arrive at 2 a.m. The Penn Central's bankruptcy in 1970 and the consolidation into Conrail in 1976 (Anchor) change who owns the sidings.
- **Dunmore Avenue.** The heart of Black Saint Jude, running about two miles. Black-owned banks, insurance offices, barbershops, funeral homes, churches, nightclubs, the Black weekly paper (*The Courier*), and Mount Zion Hospital, a Black-founded hospital now in financial trouble. A rich culture, and for forty years, the center of the numbers game.
- **Freeman Terraces and Kinsley Homes.** Public housing towers, built in the late 1950s as slum clearance. By 1975 they are poorly maintained, with failing elevators, heating, and security.
- **The Crosstown.** The I-95 Crosstown Expressway, built through the heart of the Eastside in the 1960s, displacing about 4,000 families. A wall of concrete.

**The people.** Black Saint Jude (about 65% of the Eastside), Puerto Ricans, poor whites, Jewish garment owners and a few remaining families, Italian and Jewish trucking firm owners, and the factory and warehouse workers of all kinds. The rail yards and the Racks bring many people in daily who do not live there.

**The economy.** Garment manufacturing (shrinking as work goes south and overseas), wholesale, freight, produce, public jobs, small retail, church- and family-owned businesses. High unemployment: a young Black man in the Eastside faces an unemployment rate that is double the city's.

**The institutions.** **Mount Zion Baptist**, **Bethel A.M.E.**, the **Dunmore Avenue Merchants and Businessmen's Association**, **Mercy-Eastside Clinic**, the *Courier*, the **NAACP branch**, the **Eastside Tenants' Council**, the union halls of the garment locals and the Teamsters.

**Underworld.** The **Ferrante family** is strongest in the Racks and the freight yards. The **Eastside combine** (the Black policy and numbers organization that has run the Eastside's game since the 1920s, and which in the codex became the **Mercer Street Kings**) is strongest in the Dunmore Avenue district and the projects. The two have an old, tense working relationship.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **The long fall of the garment trade.** Fewer shops each year.
- **The 1967 memory.** The Eastside burned in July 1967 and again after King's death in April 1968. The riots shaped everything: who left, who stayed, who profits from rebuilding, who politicians promise things to.
- **The numbers and the lottery.** The state lottery's daily number game threatens the numbers banks that have been the Eastside's biggest informal employer and bank.
- **Heroin.** The drug trade is a serious problem in the projects. Who supplies it is a matter of money, politics, and violence.
- **Federal and state money** for housing, health, and job programs goes through Black institutions and ward leaders, which gives them real power.

**Open.**

### 5.7 District Seven: Brighton Point

**Character.** A windswept peninsula on the Atlantic coast, north-east of the Eastside and linked by the Shore Parkway. Brick apartment blocks, boarding houses, an old boardwalk, a pier with a shuttered amusement park, and a mile of beach. Shabby and cheap in the off-season, packed in July. In 1975 it is quiet. It will not stay that way.

**History.** A seaside resort in the 1920s, fading since the 1950s. Elderly Jewish and Eastern European retirees fill the apartment blocks. From 1971, Soviet Jewish emigrants begin to settle here, drawn by cheap rents, an existing Yiddish- and Russian-speaking community, and the sea (it reminds them of Odessa). Locals call the area **Little Odessa**, a nickname that spreads in the late 1970s.

**The people.** Elderly Jews, Eastern Europeans, Soviet émigrés (a few thousand and growing), Irish and Italian families in older houses, and beach-town workers.

**The economy.** Small shops and restaurants, fur and jewelry dealers, taxi companies (new émigrés drive cabs), summer jobs, retirement money. Cash-heavy.

**The institutions.** The synagogues, the Jewish Community Center, the **Brighton Point Senior Center**, banya (bathhouse) and Russian restaurants beginning to open, and the boardwalk.

**Underworld.** In 1975 the **Vorovskoy** are a handful of men in their thirties who arrived in the last four years: jewelers, taxi-company owners, and a few ex-convicts from Soviet camps. Their first business is the community itself: extortion of émigré doctors, shopkeepers, and professionals, who have no reason to trust American police and a lifetime's habit of paying (Anchor: Agron's extortion of the émigré community, from late 1975). They also run fraud (medical billing, insurance), fencing of fur and jewelry, and contract work. They do not yet threaten the Italian families. A smart Italian capo sees a source of income. A smart Russian sees a source of protection.

**Weather.** Soviet Jewish immigration to the United States rises through the late 1970s (Anchor). The gasoline "burn company" tax-evasion scheme (an early 1980s innovation) is not yet possible. The Russian community grows from a few thousand in 1975 to tens of thousands by 1985, along with its criminal element.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **The aging retirees** and the boardwalk are an easy mark.
- **The Shore Parkway** carries everything.
- **Real estate.** A decaying resort next to a growing city.

**Open.**

### 5.8 District Eight: The Heights (the Gold Coast)

**Oakridge** lies outside the city line, in Marsh County (3.1a, 4.1). It is described here because the Heights' money, clubs and families reach into it.

**Character.** The money. **The Heights** is the old-money ridge inside the city, north-west, with Victorian and Georgian mansions on large lots, a golf club, the oldest Protestant churches, and a few Catholic mansions. **Oakridge** is the suburb beyond the city line, in Marsh County: Tudor and split-level houses on winding streets, willows, private Catholic academies, country clubs. Together they are the **Gold Coast**.

**The people.** Old Protestant families (English, Scots, German), a few old Jewish families, corporate executives, doctors, judges. And, behind gates, the wealthiest mob bosses, who have bought houses here over the last thirty years and are tolerated more than accepted.

**The economy.** Professional and corporate salaries, inherited wealth, real estate, and the kinds of businesses (car dealerships, banquet halls, real-estate agencies) that make the Gold Coast an efficient place to launder cash.

**The institutions.** The **Saint Jude Club**, the **Oakridge Country Club** (the old WASP club, where some Italian-American members arrive in 1970 after a lawsuit), the **Hibernian Club**, the **Oakridge Borough Police**, private academies (**Bishop Carroll**, **Oakridge Day**), and a number of Episcopal and Presbyterian churches.

**Underworld.** The **Gold Coast Sanctuary** is a **convention** (Tier 2). The families have an understanding that gunfire, sirens, and bodies do not occur where their wives, children, and mothers live. The understanding is old, backed by every boss's self-interest. In practice it is also protected by geography and law: Oakridge is outside the SJPD's jurisdiction. It has a small borough police force of about twenty officers, led by a chief who is a local man, and the county prosecutor in Marsh County is a different **gubernatorial appointee** from the Killbuck County Prosecutor. A killing in Oakridge brings the Marsh County prosecutor, the State Police, and the FBI. Everyone in the underworld knows that. The convention can be broken. It has been broken in other cities. When it is, the retaliation comes from every direction.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **White flight finishes** its work. Suburbs grow, the city shrinks.
- **The old WASP families** are selling.
- **Succession** in the big houses. Bosses are aging, their children are lawyers.

**Open.**

## 6. The Rest of the City

The eight districts do not exhaust Saint Jude. Other neighborhoods have their own lives.

### 6.1 Kerrytown (the Bricks)

**Character.** Triple-decker wooden tenements, corner pubs, parish halls, and boxing clubs on the south bank in the shadow of the South Pier cranes. The oldest neighborhood in the city and the one that remembers the most. The streets run down to the river. **St. Jude's Cathedral** stands at their head.

**The people.** Irish, mostly third and fourth generation, some Polish and Lithuanian, and a growing number of Puerto Rican and Black families on the edges. Dockworkers, firemen, cops, nuns, nurses, teachers, tavern keepers.

**The economy.** The docks. The city payroll. Taverns.

**The institutions.** **St. Jude's Cathedral** and school; the cathedral rectory; the Hibernian halls; **Local 1201** (South Pier longshoremen); the **Kerrytown Firemen's Association**; the **Harp and Crown** and thirty other pubs.

**Underworld.** The **Charlestown Brigades**, an Irish dockside organization, are the strongest. Italian men rarely enter after dark without a reason. The Irish have a different relation to violence: the family is the neighborhood, the loyalties are older, and the Brigades take an interest in the politics of Belfast.

**Seams.** The 1973 massacre. Courts and busing. The Irish parish is losing its young to the suburbs.

**Open.**

### 6.2 The Gannet Terminal and the Freight Corridor

**Character.** The container port, opened in 1968 on filled land on the South Flats. A flat, bright, windy landscape of stacked boxes, gantry cranes, and truck lanes. Few people, many machines. Beyond it the **I-95 freight corridor**: depots, trucking yards, the Narrows Bridge approaches, and diners that never close.

**The economy.** Container handling (a few hundred jobs where the old piers needed thousands), trucking, drayage, customs brokerage, and warehousing. The future of the port, and a new set of fights over who gets the work.

**Underworld.** Contested. The Pellegrinos have the North Piers but not the Gannet Terminal; the **Port Authority**, the shipping lines, and the unions each control pieces. Trucking is largely Teamsters, which means many hands.

**Open.**

### 6.3 The Ironworks and the Killbuck Flats

**Character.** Upriver on both banks, a vast landscape of steel mills (Killbuck Steel, with a blast furnace that has not been cold since 1902), chemical works, oil refineries, meatpacking plants and stockyards, scrap yards, and rail lines. Red slag heaps and black ponds. The river here is brown and oily. The air tastes of sulphur. Marshland lies just beyond the last plant.

**The people.** Steelworkers (Poles, Slovaks, Italians, Black migrants), chemical workers, packinghouse workers, scrap men, and the families that live in rows of company houses. The workforce is unionized and proud, and watching the mills lose ground to imports and the Sun Belt.

**The economy.** Heavy industry, shrinking. The mills employed 22,000 in 1965 and 14,000 in 1975. Meatpacking is also falling as it moves west. Scrap, chop shops, and waste hauling fill gaps.

**The institutions.** **United Steelworkers locals** with the biggest union halls in the city. The **Packinghouse local**. **St. Stanislaus**, a Polish parish. **Slag Hill Park**. The **Killbuck Flats Volunteer Fire Company**.

**Underworld.** The **Vannucci family** is dominant here (steel scrap, waste hauling, a slaughterhouse and rendering plant called San Lorenzo Provisions, trucking, chop shops). The **Iron Horsemen MC**, a biker club, has a fortified clubhouse in the Flats.

**Seams (Weather).**

- **Closing mills.** A mill that closes leaves 3,000 men without a job, a tax base without a payer, and a polluted site without a buyer.
- **Toxic waste.** The Flats are full of chemical waste. A waste-haul racket is both a profitable crime and a public health crime. The late 1970s bring national attention to toxic dumping (Anchor: Love Canal, 1978).
- **The marshes** and the rendering plant make this the traditional place to lose things.

**Open.**

### 6.4 Kosciuszko Park, Greenwood, and the Smaller Worlds

A city this size contains communities where no family holds a seat or a crew. Each has a crime side of its own, taken up in Part V. (Pescadores Row is in section 5.1.)

- **Kosciuszko Park.** A Polish neighborhood in the north-west, between the Flats and the Heights. Red-brick bungalows, Polish bakeries and butchers, a big church (**St. Stanislaus Kostka**), an active Polish Falcons hall, and a stubborn pride. Skilled workers and union men. A reliable source of voters, steelworkers, cops, and nuns.
- **Greenwood.** A middle-class neighborhood on the western line of the city, half in Marsh County. Jewish professionals and merchants who left the Eastside and the Racks in the 1950s and 1960s. Synagogues, delicatessens, and a country club of their own. Doctors, lawyers, accountants, and retailers. This is where the Racks' owners live.
- **Fenwick (College Hill).** The university district, on the north bank west of Crown Harbor, between Bellacorte and Greenwood. **Saint Jude's College** (Augustinian; the Friars; a nationally ranked basketball team), **Saint Jude State University** (a public campus), a medical school and the teaching hospital, bookshops, bars, and student apartments. Radical politics in 1970, quieter by 1975.
- **The Sixth Ward.** An old, shrinking Greek, Lebanese, and Armenian quarter near the Basin, with three Orthodox churches, coffee houses, and some of the best restaurants in the city. The diner business in Saint Jude is largely Greek.
- **The Docks Chinatown.** On the north bank behind the North Piers, a dozen blocks of Chinese restaurants and laundries, a family association building, and a **small community, about 1 percent of the city,** that has kept its business to itself.
- **The Shore.** Point Pleasant and the beach towns: a faded 1950s resort with a boardwalk, motels, an arcade, and an off-season population of retirees and cheap-room renters. Talk of casinos there is live in 1975. The Atlantic City-only vote of November 1976 (Weather, see 9.9) will settle it.
- **The Pines.** Thirty miles south-west in Pine County: pine barrens, abandoned cranberry bogs, sandy roads, hunting cabins, and a very low population. Also a state park, a few retirees, and rumors.

**Open.**

## 7. The People of Saint Jude

### 7.1 Who Lives Here

**Design (each share within about 20 percent of its figure).** These shares are design estimates for the city proper in 1975. They are rough, because the real cities they are modelled on were rough. Treat them as texture, not census.

| Community | Share of the city | Where they are strongest |
|---|---|---|
| Black | about 30% | The Eastside, Dunmore Avenue, parts of the Flats and the South Bank |
| Italian-American | about 17% (20% of the metro) | The Hill and the Bellacorte quarter, parts of the Point, the suburbs |
| Irish-American | about 12% | Kerrytown, the South Piers, the firehouses and the police |
| Polish, Slovak, Ukrainian, Lithuanian | about 8% | Kosciuszko Park, the Flats, the Ironworks |
| Old Protestant stock (English, Scots, German, Dutch) | about 10% | The Heights, Oakridge, Crown Harbor, old Germantown |
| Jewish | about 4% | Greenwood, the Racks, Brighton Point |
| Puerto Rican and other Latino | about 6% | The Eastside rooming houses, the Point, parts of the Flats |
| Portuguese | about 2% | Pescadores Row |
| Greek, Lebanese, Armenian | about 2% | The Sixth Ward, diners everywhere |
| Chinese and other Asian | about 1% | Docks Chinatown |
| Soviet Jewish émigrés | about 0.2% | Brighton Point |
| Mixed, other | about 7.8% | Everywhere |

The suburbs are whiter and richer. In 1975 the city is roughly 60% white and 30% Black, and the white share is falling.

### 7.2 How People Live

**Fixed.** Saint Jude is a city of neighborhoods. Most people live their whole lives within a few blocks of the parish where they were baptized. A man is "from the Hill" or "from Kerrytown" before he is from anywhere else. Neighborhood identity is strong, often hostile, and sometimes violent. Interracial and interethnic boundaries are real and some streets are not crossed.

- **Housing.** Row houses in the old districts, owner-occupied where families have stayed. Triple-deckers in Kerrytown. Public housing towers in the Eastside. Mansions on the Heights. Split-levels in Oakridge. Apartment blocks in Brighton Point. Many working families own their house outright, bought in the 1940s and 1950s.
- **Work.** Most families depend on a union job, a city job, or a family business. A father works in a mill or on the docks; his brother drives for the city; his cousin runs a bakery.
- **School.** Catholic schools educate perhaps a third of the city's children. Public schools carry the rest. Court-ordered desegregation of the public schools is in force or under appeal in 1975 and is bitter. Parents have strong opinions about every school.
- **Church.** Church attendance is high in the Catholic and Black Protestant communities. A parish is also a social club, a school, a charity, and a political unit.
- **The corner.** Boys grow up on the corner. The corner has a bar, a social club, a candy store, a barber. The older men on the corner know everything that has happened on the block for forty years.

### 7.3 The Veterans

**Fixed, Weather.** The Vietnam War ends in April 1975 (Anchor). Between 1965 and 1975, thousands of Saint Jude men served. Many came home addicted, injured, angry, or unemployed. Some came home with skills the city's rackets will use: weapons training, explosives, logistics, driving, and a flat acceptance of violence. Most are ordinary working men who need jobs the city does not have.

### 7.4 Crime in the Ordinary City

**Fixed.** Most crime in Saint Jude has nothing to do with the mob. Burglary, car theft, mugging, domestic violence, bar fights, arson-for-profit in decaying buildings, and heroin. The murder rate is high by the standards of the 1960s and rising. The city's police are overworked and badly paid. Fear of crime is changing where people live and shop.

## 8. Getting Around, and What Keeps the City Running

### 8.1 The Port Authority

**Fixed.** The **Killbuck River Port Authority** is a public corporation chartered by the State and the neighboring counties. It owns and runs:

- the **Gannet Terminal** and the rebuilt container piers (Piers 4 and 6) on the north bank,
- the **Narrows Bridge** (opened 1931; six lanes),
- the **Killbuck Tunnel** (opened 1957), linking downtown to the south bank,
- the **airport** (Killbuck International, in Marsh County, opened 1948),
- and its own **Port Authority Police**, about 600 officers with a separate chain of command and separate corruption.

It is run by a board appointed by the Governor and the Mayor, with a salaried executive director. It borrows by issuing bonds that are paid from tolls, fees and rents. It has no voters. It has a great deal of patronage, a great many contracts, and a long record of being run for the benefit of whoever controls the board. The Port Authority is a battleground between the Mayor, the Governor, and the unions.

**Anchor.** The Port Authority of New York and New Jersey is the model. This is a sister body. The real Port Authority runs the Newark and Elizabeth terminals and the New York area airports and crossings, and does not operate in Saint Jude.

**Open.** The executive director, board members, any bond deals, police scandals.

### 8.2 Roads and Bridges

**Fixed.**

- **I-95** runs north to south through the south bank freight corridor, crosses the Narrows Bridge, and runs on the **Crosstown Expressway** through the Eastside, north to the suburbs. It is the artery of the whole East Coast and the main route for trucks, drugs, and cash.
- **I-295** is the beltway, finished in 1971, linking the suburbs.
- **The Shore Parkway** links the Eastside to Brighton Point and the beach towns.
- **Route 9** is the old coast road south to Point Pleasant, with diners and motels.
- **The Narrows Bridge** is a steel suspension bridge with a toll plaza on the south end. Tolls are collected in cash by Port Authority collectors (E-ZPass and any electronic record do not exist). The bridge is a choke point and every driver passes the booths.
- **The Killbuck Tunnel** is a two-tube vehicular tunnel under the Basin. Trucks with certain loads may not use it.

**Weather.** Roads and bridges are underfunded in the city's budget crisis. Potholes, closed lanes, and bridge inspections are a growing story.

### 8.3 Transit

**Fixed.** The **Saint Jude Transit Authority** runs buses, trolley-buses on a few lines, and two rapid-transit lines:

- **The Hill Line** (the Elevated, "the El"), built in 1904 on iron trestles, runs from Crown Harbor along Via Garibaldi through San Lorenzo, then out along Mercer Avenue to the Strip. It is noisy, slow and rattles the windows of the houses beside it.
- **The Basin Line** is a short subway from Union Station under Crown Harbor to the Federal Complex and the Basin Front.

**Commuter rail.** Trains from the suburbs arrive at Union Station. After the Penn Central bankruptcy in 1970 and the formation of Conrail in 1976 (Anchor), passenger service is run by a state agency and is under-funded.

**Taxis.** About 3,500 licensed taxis (medallion cabs) in the city, plus many unlicensed "gypsy" cabs in the Eastside. The taxi business is a cash business and a licensing business.

### 8.4 Hospitals, Utilities, Services

**Fixed.**

- **Mercy General** (Catholic, on the edge of the Hill) is the city's busiest trauma center, with an emergency room that sees many gunshot wounds. State law requires that doctors report them within hours.
- **Saint Jude University Hospital** (Fenwick) is the teaching hospital.
- **St. Anne's** (in the Bellacorte quarter, the former Italian Hospital) is smaller and quieter.
- **Mount Zion Hospital** (Eastside), **Beth Israel** (Greenwood), **Marine Hospital** (the Point, for seamen).
- **Killbuck Power and Light** supplies electricity. **Bell Telephone of the State** supplies telephones and is a regulated monopoly (Anchor: the Bell System is broken up in 1984).
- **The Department of Sanitation** collects household garbage. Commercial waste is hauled by private firms. This is where the waste-hauling cartels live.
- **The Fire Department** is one of the city's biggest employers and one of its best organized unions.

## 9. The Economy of the City

### 9.1 The Big Picture

**Fixed.** Saint Jude in 1975 is an old industrial port in slow decline. Its major trades are:

| Sector | Approximate jobs in 1975 | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Government (city, county, state, federal, Port Authority) | 120,000 | flat to falling |
| Health and education | 100,000 | rising |
| Manufacturing (steel, chemicals, food, garment, printing) | 130,000 | falling fast |
| Port and transport (docks, rail, trucking, airport) | 55,000 | falling |
| Retail and wholesale | 110,000 | flat, moving to suburbs |
| Finance, insurance, real estate, law | 50,000 | flat |
| Construction | 35,000 | cyclical |
| Services, hotels, restaurants, entertainment | 90,000 | rising |

**Design (each sector within about 15 percent of its figure).** These are design estimates for city-proper employment. **The total is about 690,000 jobs, or 0.63 jobs per resident. It counts jobs located in the city, not jobs held by residents. Commuters from Marsh, Shore and Pine counties fill a large share of them, as in any regional center, so the ratio is not a contradiction. It is Design and is not checked against a real city [memory, to verify]. Part VI uses shares and ratios and builds no wage bill from this total.** They are not exact, but they fix the order of magnitude: **government and health are now larger than industry.** The city's money comes less from making things and more from public payrolls, health care, and the port.

### 9.2 The Port

**Fixed.** The port is the city's reason for existing and its oldest business.

- In 1950 about 18,000 longshoremen worked the finger piers. In 1975 about 7,000 do, and the number is falling.
- A **container ship** can be unloaded in a day by one crew with cranes. A breakbulk ship takes a week and a hundred men.
- The remaining piers handle coffee, cars, cocoa, bananas, sacked goods, and military cargo. The Gannet Terminal and Piers 4 and 6 handle containers.
- **The unions** (Local 402 on the north bank, Local 1201 on the south) negotiate a guaranteed annual income and "container royalty" payments for lost work. Hiring is by a pool system administered under the Waterfront Commission.
- **The shipping lines** want fewer men, faster turnaround, and no strikes. They pay to avoid trouble.

**Open.** Everything else.

### 9.3 Heavy Industry

**Fixed.** Killbuck Steel, two refineries, a chemical works, a shipyard, meatpacking, and printing. All shrinking. Plants that close do not reopen.

### 9.4 Garment and Wholesale

**Fixed.** The Racks produce dresses, coats, and fur. About 25,000 people work in 1,200 shops in 1975, down from 40,000 in 1960. Most workers are women, many immigrant, many Puerto Rican, Black or Italian. The shops are small, undercapitalized, and operate under tight contracts with large buyers. Owners are mostly Jewish and Italian. The trucks that carry the goods belong to Teamsters, and trucking is the real chokepoint of the trade.

### 9.5 Construction

**Fixed.** Construction is the city's last great industry, cyclical and political. Building the downtown towers, the hospital extensions, the bridge and highway repairs, and the public housing all run through the Board of Public Works, the Buildings Department, and the building-trades unions. Concrete is the choke point. In bedrock Crown Harbor a tall building needs a deep foundation, a long pour, and a ready-mix supplier on a schedule. Whoever controls the concrete controls the job.

**Weather.** Federal grants (Community Development Block Grants from 1974, Anchor) replace the old urban-renewal money. They are smaller and more political.

### 9.6 Money, Banks, and Credit

**Fixed.** **First Harbor National Bank** is the city's biggest. There are four other banks and several savings banks and credit unions. The **Italian Credit Union** on the Hill and the **Dunmore Savings Bank** in the Eastside are community institutions. Banks lend cautiously to the Eastside (redlining is the established practice and is just beginning to be challenged, Anchor).

Cash dominates the street economy. A great many businesses, from bars to bakeries to bookmakers, deal almost wholly in cash. The bank deposit slip is a record. Currency Transaction Reports exist on paper under the Bank Secrecy Act of 1970 (Anchor), but banks file few and enforcement is weak, so cash moves with little record. The IRS is the main legal threat to cash men.

**Weather.** Interest rates rise through 1979 and 1980 (Anchor **[verified, FRED monthly prime rate]: 15.25 percent in January 1980, 19.77 in April, 20.35 in December, and a peak of 20.50 in August 1981**). Small businesses and real-estate developers are squeezed. People who lend at their own rates do very well. **The 30-year mortgage rate is 9.60 percent in the first week of 1975, 12.85 in January 1980, 17.30 in January 1982, and peaks at 18.63 in October 1981 [verified, FRED, Freddie Mac weekly series].**

### 9.7 Prices and Wages

**Part VI carries the price book. The figures below are kept for a first feel. Where Part VI differs, Part VI governs.** In 1975 a working man's wage is $3 to $6 an hour **(Part VI 1.3: the federal minimum is $2.10 [verified]; a longshoreman's basic rate is $6.80 [verified])**. A new car is about $4,500. A working-class row house is $18,000 to $30,000 **(Part VI 1.4 default $22,000, Design; the national median new home is $39,300 [verified, Census], and a new suburban house is a different good from a used city row house)**. A beer in a neighborhood bar is 50 cents. A meal at a diner is two dollars. A cop earns $12,000 to $16,000 **(Part VI 7.3, from Part II 2.2: $11,500 at the start and $14,500 after three years, Design; the $16,000 end of the range is a senior or overtime figure and is not priced in Part VI)**. **A soldier's ordinary week is about $600 net, and his good week might be $1,500. A capo's ordinary week is nearer $1,700 (Part VI 9.2).**

### 9.8 The State Lottery

**Fixed.** The State started a lottery in the early 1970s (Anchor: several Northeastern states began lotteries in 1967 to 1971). It was a weekly game with long odds and slow payouts. It did not compete with the illegal numbers game.

**Weather (strong, from 22 May 1975).** The State's daily three-digit game, **Pick-it** (renamed Pick-3 in 1987), begins on 22 May 1975 (Anchor [verified]: New Jersey). A state game pays less than an illegal game, offers no credit, and cannot be played on the corner. The illegal game survives by paying more, extending credit, and offering convenience. But the state game is a competitor, and it shrinks the illegal market over several years.

For the Eastside, where the numbers banks have employed hundreds and financed churches and businesses for fifty years, this is a threat to the economy.

### 9.9 The Casino Question

**Weather (moderate).** Gambling is spreading. The State's voters approve casino gambling in November 1976, but only for Atlantic City, and the first casino opens there in 1978 [verified]. Saint Jude gets none. Whether the State later widens the law to the Shore is Open. If it does, the Shore becomes the most valuable land in the state and a new battlefield. If it does not, the Shore bleeds customers to Atlantic City, and the families' men go there to work.

### 9.10 Unemployment and Money Troubles

**Weather (strong, 1975 to 1977, and again 1979 to 1983).**

- National unemployment peaks at about 9 percent in 1975 and 10.8 percent in 1982 (Anchor). Saint Jude is worse than the national average in both.
- Inflation runs at double digits in 1974, 1979, 1980 **and 1981** (Anchor). Wages lag.
- Energy prices spike in 1973 and 1979 (Anchor). Gas lines return.
- The city's bond rating is low. A default is possible.

Money problems feed every other problem. A cop who earns too little takes the Pad. A man who loses his job borrows at six-for-five. A factory owner who cannot borrow from a bank borrows from a man who will take half the business.

## 10. Faith

### 10.1 The Archdiocese

**Fixed.** The **Archdiocese of Saint Jude** has about 150 parishes and 80 Catholic schools. Its seat is **St. Jude's Cathedral** in Kerrytown. The Archbishop is a powerful man in the city's life. Catholic Charities, the hospitals, the colleges, and the Knights of Columbus all answer to him.

Since the late 1960s the Mass has been said in English in most parishes, after the Second Vatican Council (Anchor). Many older parishioners regret the Latin, and some priests regret the change of rite. Attendance has begun to fall. The Archdiocese is beginning to close parishes in neighborhoods that have lost their people.

**National parishes.** The Archdiocese has parishes organized by language and origin: Irish (St. Jude's), Italian (St. Rocco's, Santa Maria della Corte), Polish (St. Stanislaus), Portuguese (Our Lady of the Sea), German (Holy Trinity), Black Catholic (St. Martin de Porres), Puerto Rican (San Juan Bautista). Each parish has its own feast, its own society, and its own politics. In 1975 the system is beginning to bend under the weight of falling numbers.

**The Church and the families.** The Church officially condemns crime. In practice, mob men go to Mass, give generously, baptize their children in the parish, and expect the funeral Mass. Pastors differ: some will not accept a man's money, some will. The Archdiocese has been quietly proud of a policy not to ask where the money comes from. The question of Christian burial for a notorious man is one the Archdiocese decides case by case.

### 10.2 Protestants

**Fixed.** Old Protestant churches line Crown Harbor and the Heights: **Christ Church (Episcopal)**, **First Presbyterian**, **Old South Church (Congregational)**. German Lutheran and Reformed congregations settled in the 1840s in the old brewery district north of the Flats and now serve a smaller, mostly Black and Puerto Rican neighborhood: **Trinity Lutheran** and its parish school. Methodists and Baptists exist everywhere.

### 10.3 The Black Church

**Fixed.** Black churches are the center of Black Saint Jude: **Mount Zion Baptist**, **Bethel A.M.E.**, a score of storefront Pentecostal congregations. Pastors are political leaders, bankers, employers, and mediators. A major pastor can deliver 10,000 votes. Some pastors work with the numbers bankers, who give to the church. Some oppose them. The civil rights movement of the 1960s was organized in these churches.

### 10.4 Jews

**Fixed.** The Jewish community has moved from the Racks and the Eastside to Greenwood and the suburbs. Synagogues (Orthodox, Conservative, and Reform), the Jewish Federation, and a community center. The garment trade was once largely Jewish. Jewish lawyers, accountants, and doctors are common in the professional class, and the Jewish community has a long, mixed, and sometimes uneasy relationship with the Italian underworld as customers, partners, and victims.

### 10.5 Others

Greek Orthodox, Russian Orthodox, a small mosque (Black Muslims in the Eastside), and a Buddhist temple in Docks Chinatown.

## 11. Media and Public Culture

### 11.1 The Papers

**Fixed.**

- **The Saint Jude Daily Mirror** ("the *Mirror*"): the morning tabloid, loud, working-class, with crime, sports, gossip, and a good columnist. Circulation about 400,000. It prints on a union press schedule, which means the pressmen's union can slow it down.
- **The Saint Jude Chronicle** ("the *Chronicle*"): the evening broadsheet. Conservative, establishment, respected. Circulation about 250,000. It is in slow decline as evening papers everywhere are.
- **The Courier**: the Black weekly of the Eastside, founded 1912. It matters more than its circulation (about 40,000) suggests.
- **L'Eco della Corte** and **The Kerry Sentinel**: small ethnic weeklies.
- **The Fenwick Daily**: the student paper at the college.

### 11.2 Broadcast

**Fixed.** Three network television affiliates (**Channel 4**, **Channel 7**, **Channel 10**), a public station, and a UHF independent. Local news is an hour at six and eleven. Radio: a news station (WSJD), two Top 40 stations, a soul and gospel station on Dunmore Avenue, a classical station, an Italian-language hour on Sundays, and a station of Irish music on Saturday mornings.

### 11.3 Sports

**Fixed.** The **Saint Jude Admirals** (Triple-A baseball). The **Saint Jude's College Friars** (nationally ranked basketball, popular, and vulnerable). High school sports in the Catholic League, with the big Thanksgiving football game. **Bayview Downs** racetrack, thoroughbred racing in a season from March through November. Boxing at the **Civic Arena** on Friday nights. Sandlot softball and bowling.

**Open.** There is no major-league team in the city. Whether the city wants one, and who would pay for a stadium, is an open question.

### 11.4 Music, Food, Speech

**Fixed.**

- **Music.** The Hill sings Italian song and Sinatra. Dunmore Avenue's clubs play jazz, soul, and rhythm and blues. Kerrytown has ballad pubs. The Teatro della Corte offers opera and concerts. Rock in the college district. Disco arrives in the late 1970s.
- **Food.** Italian sauce (called "gravy" on the Hill), pork stores, pizza by the slice, fish stew on Friday, steamed crabs in summer (the Killbuck's bays), oyster stew, Portuguese sweet bread and linguiça, Polish pierogi, Jewish delicatessens, Black soul food, Greek diners.
- **Speech.** A broad Mid-Atlantic coastal accent. *Down the Point* means the docks. *Up the Hill* means San Lorenzo. A *hero* is a sandwich on a long roll. *Youse* is used by old men. People say "the Jude" only as a joke.

## 12. Women in Saint Jude

**Fixed.** Women are not decoration in this city. They run houses, shops, parishes, wards, hospitals, schools, and sometimes crime.

### 12.1 The Ordinary Facts

- About 45 percent of women of working age work outside the home in 1975 (Anchor: approximate for the period). Many work in the garment shops, as secretaries and clerks, as nurses and nurses' aides, in school and hospital kitchens, in shops, in bars.
- Women are rare in the top ranks of the city's institutions, but not absent. **At most one woman sits on the fifteen-member City Council, and there may be none. A woman on the bench is a rarity: one or two among dozens of judges, and each is watched. No woman has been mayor, police commissioner, County Prosecutor, union president on the docks, or a ward leader of a major ward. Women edit the *Chronicle's* society and features pages, and few reporters on the crime beat are women.** A Sister of Mercy runs Mercy General, **because Catholic hospitals and schools have been run by sisters for a century.**
- Women are admitted to the FBI as agents beginning in 1972 and to the SJPD as patrol officers beginning in 1973 (Anchor for the FBI and for the national trend). **They are well under 2 percent of either force, are kept out of the units that matter (organized crime squads, homicide), and face open hostility from many colleagues.**
- The women's movement has changed college life, newspaper offices, and some parts of the law. It has barely touched the Hill.

### 12.2 Women and Power in the Families

**Fixed.** Women are never inducted. They are never at the table. No price and no event breaks this. It is not an absence of power.

**Tendency.** In practice the wives, mothers, sisters, and widows of mob men are information networks. The Sunday table, the funeral home visit, the church bazaar, the hairdresser's, and the telephone link them across families. Women know who is sleeping with whom, who is dying, who is in debt, who is seeing a priest, and who has stopped coming to Mass. A boss's wife or mother may be the only person who can say a hard thing to him. Widows run businesses their husbands left. A mother can end a feud. A mother can start one.

**Example.** An old don's mother who insists on a war the family cannot afford, a consigliere's wife who runs the family's bakery and its books, a widow who inherits a social club and keeps its secrets.

### 12.3 Women in the Eastside

**Fixed.** In Black Saint Jude, women are among the strongest figures in the churches, the tenants' councils, and the schools, **which is where they were allowed to lead.** **Numbers "bankers" were sometimes women in the early twentieth century (Anchor: Harlem's Stephanie St. Clair is the best-known case). By 1975 the game is run by men, and women work in it as writers, clerks, and runners.** Women lead the Eastside Tenants' Council and run block associations, funeral homes, and beauty shops.

### 12.4 The Rule of Plausibility

**Convention for the Storyteller and the simulation.** A woman holds power in 1975 by one of four routes, and a new woman of consequence should come by one of them:

1. **Family:** wife, mother, sister, or widow of a powerful man, with real influence and no formal title.
2. **The Church:** a sister who runs a school, hospital, or charity; a lay parish leader.
3. **Small business:** a bakery, funeral home, boarding house, bar, beauty shop, or dress shop, often inherited from a husband or father.
4. **Female-dominated institutions:** nursing, teaching, clerical work, social work, the women's pages. Authority there is real and limited.

Formal power in male-dominated fields (police, courts, unions, politics, the mob**, where a woman is never made, 12.2**) is rare, slow, and resisted. A woman who rises there needs a cause the story can state (a sponsor, a scandal, a death, a quota, an exceptional record) and she meets friction at each step. **The numbers rise gradually through the late 1970s and 1980s as they did in the real world; they do not jump.** The simulation should not produce women in positions of power at modern rates, and should not make every woman a victim or a schemer either.

**Open.**
