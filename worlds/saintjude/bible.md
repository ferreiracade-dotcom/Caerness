# SAINT JUDE: THE SETTING BIBLE

## Edition and dating

This is the setting bible for the Saint Jude world package: an Atlantic port city and its four Italian-American crime families, adapted from the Bellacorte Living World Codex. It holds what the city is, how it works and how it talks. It does not hold a script. Who currently holds which post, what is in dispute and what happens next belong to the starting snapshot and to the live campaign, and the live campaign outranks anything written here.

The codex is changeable source material, not canon. Where this bible and the running world disagree about a person, a post, a business or a debt, the running world is right. Where this bible and plausibility disagree, plausibility wins. Rules that look absolute below are tendencies and conventions with consequences unless they are stated as plain physical or legal fact.

### Flexible date rule

The calendar is a continuity tool, not a storytelling constraint. A story chooses its opening season and year (for example "late summer 1980" or "the winter of 1981") instead of defaulting to 1 January. The default frame is the early 1980s, and 1980 is the default opening. The shared starting snapshot is dated about 1975, so a 1980 story begins after roughly five years of the city living on its own. Once play begins, time advances with events and the calendar stays consistent. If a story opens later than 1980, every active character, event, law, technology, price and financial benchmark is re-checked against that year before play.

### Dating rules

1. All prices and balances are in US dollars of the year named. Appendix B is the 1980 reference ledger. Never mix prices of different years without a dated conversion.
2. A technology, law, product, business model, criminal method, cultural reference or market cannot appear before its plausible introduction. Distinguish invention, prototype, commercial release, local availability, affordability and common use. The dated technology and legal sheet is Appendix A.
3. Ventures and pressures that belong to a later decade are possibilities for later years, not facts of an earlier one. They enter only when time, infrastructure, money, skills and local demand support them.
4. History up to about 1975 (the 1946 harbor war, the 1958 Moretti schism, the 1967 Vannucci split, the 1973 dock strike massacre, the 1974 concrete purge) is settled background. Anything the codex placed after 1975 is not settled; the city's own run may or may not produce it.
5. Existing lore is not discarded casually. A material retcon is recorded in the campaign state and is never disguised as something that was always true.
6. The storyteller may create opportunities, apply pressure and resolve what other people do, but does not choose the player character's consequential decisions, loyalties, spoken commitments or compromises.

### Autonomous city

Every significant faction and institution has goals, resources, constraints, relationships, current knowledge and a likely next move, and the city keeps changing when the player is elsewhere: leadership disputes, business openings and failures, police investigations, elections, union fights, territorial pushes, rackets, alliances and gang wars all progress according to capability, incentive, friction and chance.

## Founding and history

### The founding story

**Saint Jude was founded as a Roman Catholic settlement around its first church, St. Jude's, and the city grew outward from that church.** St. Jude is the patron saint of lost causes, and the city took its saint after its founders survived something hopeless. The settlement began as a small colonial-era Catholic refuge, much as Maryland was a refuge for English Catholics, planted beside a Dutch trading post on the river. The Dutch post explains the river's Dutch name, Killbuck.

Which Catholic settlers first arrived is deliberately not pinned down; Italian, Spanish, Portuguese and Irish founders are all possible, or a mix, and a story may settle it. What is settled is the sequence of those who followed. Portuguese fishermen came in the 1800s. The Irish came after the Famine. The Italians came from the 1880s. Each group added its own parishes and its own corners of the city.

St. Jude's stands on the original site near the river mouth, beside Kerrytown and Killbuck Point, and is now the cathedral of the Archdiocese of Saint Jude. The Irish inherited the old founding parish. The Italians built St. Rocco's on the Hill (San Lorenzo) for themselves, and the quiet rivalry between the founding church and the Italian parish is inherited by the families on both sides. The Feast of San Rocco in August is the Italian parish's great festival. Saint Jude himself is the city's patron and, as the docks' own saint, a presence in the harbor.

### Historical caveat

Mass devotion to St. Jude in America is largely a twentieth-century development, so an early dedication of a founding church to him is a deliberate choice for this setting, explained by the founding story above. It is not a claim about real American history. Treat it as the city's own folklore.

### Dated history of the city

These events are settled background up to about 1975.

- **1946, the Four-Corner Harbor War.** A brutal three-month shooting war across the waterfront and San Lorenzo ends in exhaustion and establishes the raw territorial divide that later becomes the Iron Balance.
- **1958, the Moretti schism.** Sal Moretti and his Eastside garment and trucking crews break away from the Sabatini Family in a bloody six-month civil war, with too many buttonmen to be wiped out, and the Moretti Family becomes the fourth sovereign family.
- **1967, the Vannucci split.** Old Giuseppe Vannucci leads the Mercer Avenue and Ironworks crews out of the Castellano Family in a violent break, taking the Neon Strip and the steel foundries.
- **1973, the dock strike massacre.** Castellano crews use cargo hooks and shotguns to crush an independent longshoremen's strike on the South Piers. Kerrytown's Irish count the dead among their own, and a permanent blood grievance between the Castellanos and the Irish follows.
- **1974, the concrete purge.** The Sabatini boss quietly has a relative and three rival contractors put into a bedrock caisson pour to bring the Concrete Club under his sole control.

Events after 1975 are not written here. A 1980 story opens on whatever the five years of the city's own run produced. The codex set a famous assassination of the Vannucci patriarch in 1977; that is no longer a fact of the setting but one of the things that may or may not occur in the run.

## Names and places that changed from the source

The source codex called the city Bellacorte. In Saint Jude, **Saint Jude is the city**, and **Bellacorte is an old Italianate district** inside it: the first commercial quarter built outside the colonial waterfront in the 1860s to 1890s, with palazzo-fronted banks, wholesale halls, cast-iron loft buildings and tightly packed rowhouse streets. Its eastern end runs out into the garment lofts, rail yards and trucking terminals known as East Bellacorte and the Racks.

**The codex's main port name is now Killbuck Point**, the headland where the Killbuck River meets the Atlantic. People call it "down the Point". The source's saint-named meadowlands are now **the Pines**, the pine-barrens country west of the city that was always known by that nickname. **St. Jude's in Kerrytown** keeps its name and is the founding church and the cathedral.

## Where the city sits

**Saint Jude** is a sprawling, grimy, lucrative East Coast port metropolis at the mouth of the **Killbuck River**, where it meets the Atlantic Ocean, with a metropolitan population of about 2.4 million. It is modeled on the geographic and industrial scale of Newark and Jersey City, Boston and Philadelphia, but its prices and details are fictional and it is not an exact copy of any one real city. The default period is the early 1980s, a time of high inflation, high interest rates and industrial restructuring.

The ordinary city is large. Most streets, shops and citizens are not part of a conspiracy at any moment: there are commuters, honest beat cops, parish fish fries, high school basketball games and legitimate roadwork. Rackets and families sit inside that ordinary life, not instead of it.

## The people of the city

The city's population is mixed, and the mix is part of how the underworld is organized. It includes Italian-American families (the Hill, the old Bellacorte district, the Racks and the Strip), Irish (Kerrytown, the docks and the old founding parish), Black neighborhoods on the Eastside and along the Strip, Jewish merchants, accountants, lawyers and some specialist criminals, Russian and Odessa-born immigrants on the Brighton Point peninsula, Puerto Rican communities in the working-class districts, and the old-money Protestant families of Oakridge and the Heights. Portuguese fishing families are a visible older layer on the waterfront.

These groups shape who can be inducted, who can become a wealthy associate, who runs a rival syndicate and who sits on the bench, but none of that is a law of nature. It is custom with consequences, described below.

## 1. Epistemic reality: facts, conventions and street myths

### 1.1 The epistemic truth table

To keep dramatic hyperbole from acting as rigid physical law, setting lore operates across five tiers. A tier says how firmly something holds, and what happens when it is broken.

| Epistemic tier | Definition and behavior in play | Saint Jude examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Established canon (hard fact)** | Objective historical, geographic or institutional reality. Cannot be contradicted without an in-world causal event. | Four Italian-American families operate in the city; the settlement grew around St. Jude's; later laws and technologies are not available before their historical introduction; routine forensic DNA profiling is not available to investigators in 1980. |
| **2. Institutional convention** | Strong, shared, unwritten rules backed by mutual self-interest. Breaking one is physically possible and triggers calculated political or violent retaliation from the people who care. | The Oakridge ("Gold Coast") sanctuary; the ban on killing journalists or uniformed cops; the expectation that a boss sanctions the killing of a made man. |
| **3. Operational tendency** | Standard street practice because it is efficient or safe, with frequent exceptions from human impulse, incompetence or ordinary life. | Hits and disposals in the 3:30 to 5:00 AM shift change; paying the 14th Precinct pad on the first of the month; sensitive business on walk-talks. |
| **4. Street belief or myth** | What wiseguys believe or boast to be true even when legally or forensically wrong. Acting on these beliefs blindly creates unexpected exposure. | "No body, no crime" (believing a dissolved corpse makes a prosecution impossible); believing every elder obeys honor and never touches heroin. |
| **5. Ordinary civic reality** | The baseline life of 2.4 million citizens. | Commuters, hardware stores, honest beat cops, parish fish fries, high school basketball, municipal roadwork. |

### 1.2 The plausibility anchor: the four-family schism and the Iron Balance

In the real American underworld of the period, no East Coast city existed in a vacuum from New York's Five Families, yet several cities kept fierce operational autonomy. Saint Jude is a **sovereign four-family underworld** (**Sabatini, Castellano, Vannucci, Moretti**) that sits alongside non-Italian syndicates.

- **No outside tribute.** The four families do not kick up a percentage of their local rackets to New York or Chicago. When a dispute involves interstate unions (the Teamsters Central States Pension Fund, for instance) or joint maritime smuggling through Killbuck Point with a Brooklyn crew, Saint Jude's bosses deal with New York as respected equals.
- **Why four violent families exist.** The city began with two massive warring clans, the Sabatinis downtown and the Castellanos on the docks. In 1958 the Moretti Family (East Bellacorte and the Racks) broke away from the Sabatinis in a bloody internal war over garment and trucking rackets. In 1967 the Vannucci Family (Mercer Avenue and the Ironworks) split violently from the Castellanos over the steel mills and the nightlife strip.
- **New York stays out.** The Commission declines to intervene, viewing the city as a hornets' nest so long as containers through Killbuck Point keep moving on time. Because the four families are splintered cousins and former traitors, they see each other as usurpers who stole their grandfathers' rackets, not as respectable corporate peers.
- **The Iron Balance.** There is no boss of bosses, no written constitution and no formal tribunal. Boundaries hold by the Iron Balance: mutual deterrence and the management of federal heat. Sit-downs are tense standoffs in greasy diners, unheated garages or funeral parlors, held because an open war costs too much money and draws FBI surveillance vans, not out of respect for any charter.

**Violence is a feature of this city, and sharing is tense.** The families do share some places and industries by tense agreement, which was unusual even in New York, but the lines are defended with more violence than in the real cities Saint Jude resembles.

### 1.3 The technology, legal and forensic baseline (default 1980)

This section is written for the default 1980 opening. Appendix A gives the dated ladder for other years. A technology can exist without being available in the city, be available without being affordable, be affordable without being common, and be common without being reliable.

- **No modern consumer digital trail.** There are no cellular-network location records, no modern citywide street-camera network and no electronic toll records. Computers and closed-circuit cameras exist in limited institutional, industrial, retail and specialist settings. Routine forensic DNA profiling is not available to investigators in 1980.
- **Missing bodies and legal exposure.** Disposing of a corpse in a rendering vat, an arc furnace or a bedrock caisson is a highly effective operational tendency, because it keeps the 14th Precinct homicide squad from opening an immediate murder file (the result is a missing-persons report instead). **It is a street myth that "no body means zero legal risk."** Federal prosecutors and the FBI can and do build RICO predicate acts and obtain Title III wiretap warrants around disappearances using circumstantial evidence: a nervous witness who saw the victim go into a particular business, the victim's watch in a pawn shop, sudden transfers of the victim's loan book, or a boast recorded on a club microphone six months later.
- **What actually catches operators.**
1. Fingerprints and palm prints left on steering wheels, bottles or brass shell casings (countered by thin surgical gloves, clear nail polish or wiping metal with lighter fluid).
2. Ballistics striations, matched with comparison microscopes (countered by revolvers that leave no brass, reamed barrels and gun parts dropped down separate storm drains).
3. ABO blood typing and hair and fiber analysis, which can match a blood group or a carpet roll but cannot identify a single person.
4. Acoustics and paper: Title III hidden microphones ("probes"), pen registers on phones, IRS net-worth audits (proving that a man with a $20,000 salary bought a $250,000 house), 35mm surveillance photographs and informants wearing concealed Nagra body recorders.
- **Communications.** Street soldiers rely on landlines, payphones, trusted messengers and face-to-face meetings. Some businesses use answering services, radios or pagers, but pagers are not universal. Car phones exist as expensive installed equipment for a small affluent clientele. Handheld cellular phones are not a 1980 option; commercial handheld service arrives later in the decade. Nobody should treat modern cell coverage, texts or phone-location records as available.
- **Vehicles.** Workhorse sedans are 1970s Cadillac Sedan de Villes, Lincoln Continentals, Buick Regals, Oldsmobile Cutlass Supremes and heavy Chevrolet Caprices and Ford LTDs, prized for deep trunks and steel frames. Pontiac Trans Ams and Corvettes suit flashier young earners. Vehicles from later model years enter the market only as time advances.
- **Plausible firepower.** Most underworld killings use concealable handguns: the S&W Model 36 Chief's Special snub-nose .38 (often with a filed firing pin and friction tape on the grip), Charter Arms .44 Bulldogs, Colt 1911 .45s, and High Standard .22 target pistols fitted with homemade steel-wool suppressors. Remington 870 shotguns and converted submachine guns of the Ingram MAC-10 type are reserved for public terror messages and heavy robberies, because automatic gunfire brings immediate federal ATF attention.

Influence is rough and shifting: families try to hold their own ground, contest it with each other and with the outside gangs, and share some places tensely.

### 2.2 Transitional corridors and satellite zones

- **The Narrows Bridge and toll plaza.** A soot-stained suspension bridge across the Killbuck River mouth, connecting Killbuck Point to the I-95 freight corridor. Bribed midnight toll collectors log rival trucks and unmarked FBI sedans crossing, tipping off hijack crews waiting at the base of the ramp.
- **Kerrytown ("the Bricks").** Tightly packed triple-decker wooden tenements, smoky corner pubs, parish halls and boxing clubs in the shadow of the South Pier cranes, around the cathedral of St. Jude's. Stronghold of the Charlestown Brigades; Italian buttonmen and uniformed cops rarely enter after dark without backup.
- **The Pines.** Thirty miles west of the city: coastal pine barrens, abandoned cranberry bogs, unpaved sandy fire-roads and dilapidated hunting cabins. Used for testing automatic weapons, hiding fugitives in cooling-off cabins and digging lime-pit graves in soft acid soil. "Taking a ride to the Pines" is street shorthand for a burial.
- **Point Pleasant and the Boardwalk.** A faded 1950s seaside resort town forty minutes south down the coast. In deep winter, when summer tourists leave, cigarette boats and fishing trawlers pull into unpatrolled marina slips at 3:00 AM to offload bales away from the Coast Guard cutters at Killbuck Point.

### 2.3 Iconic hangouts and sanctuaries

- **The Vesuvio Social and Athletic Club (North San Lorenzo).** A peeling brick storefront with an unlit bocce-ball neon sign where retired neighborhood men play scopa. Behind the walk-in pantry is the acoustic-tiled executive backroom of the Sabatini Family, where the Friday envelopes arrive beneath a portrait of the Pope.
- **Pier 4 Greasy Spoon and Fish Market (Killbuck Point).** A battered diner wedged between container cranes, opening at 4:00 AM. A neutral gray zone where Castellano capos, Irish dock bosses and harbor police sit in adjacent vinyl booths. Stolen cargo manifests and customs bribes change hands under the napkin dispenser at booth 3.
- **The Starlight Cabaret and Lounge (Mercer Avenue).** A glitzy supper club with a thirty-foot pink martini glass sign, the crown jewel of the Vannucci cash-washing machine. Upstairs VIP rooms have two-way mirrors used to compromise councilmen, union treasurers and judges; basement locker rooms are used to cut and repackage confiscated narcotics dropped off by crooked vice cops. The sign and the disco-era makeover belong to the late 1970s: in the mid-1970s it is an older cabaret, and what it becomes depends on the city's run.
- **The Pompeii Social Club and Cigar Store (Hunts Row, the Racks).** The brick-and-iron stronghold of the Moretti Family, wedged between garment warehouses and the elevated freight tracks. Hijacked loads of furs, jewelry and electronics are priced and fenced in its windowless basement over espresso and anisette.
- **Gino's Espresso Bar and Villa Roma (central San Lorenzo).** Neutral diplomatic ground where the four families meet when tension boils. Daylight sit-downs happen on Gino's open-air back terrace (where the noise of the elevated train hampers parabolic microphones); formal multi-family standoffs happen in Villa Roma's private subterranean wine cellar.
- **Russo and Sons Funeral Home (San Lorenzo).** An ornamental brick parlor with velvet drapes and a fleet of black Cadillac hearses. Besides lavish mob wakes, its basement preparation room has a private side door used for high-security sit-downs when the streets are crawling with federal vans.

## 3. Underworld culture, hierarchy and practical authority

The clash between **honor** (*Onore*, tradition) and greed or treachery does not divide neatly by family or by generation. Every family reveres the sacred rituals and every family is also rotting from hypocrisy.

### 3.1 Formal hierarchy and practical influence

The formal picture, from the top:

- The **Boss** (*Il Capo*, "the Old Man"), with an **Underboss** (*Sotto-Capo*) and a **Consigliere** (the counselor) beside him.
- Under them the **Caporegimes** (capos, captains, skippers) who lead crews.
- Under the capos the **Soldiers** (*sgarristi*, made men, buttonmen, "friends of ours").
- Outside the family but attached to it, the **Associates** (the unmade: earners, bookies, hijackers, muscle).

#### Formal rank definitions

- **The Boss.** The formal monarch of the family. Insulates himself from wiretaps by rarely giving direct orders to street soldiers and speaks through his underboss, his consigliere or a trusted driver. Receives a tribute cut from every crew.
- **The Underboss.** Second in command and chief operating officer of violence. Manages the capos, settles internal turf disputes and oversees high-level union and construction skims.
- **The Consigliere.** Elder statesman and right-hand adviser. By custom has no street crew of his own, which keeps him from building a rival army and lets him negotiate at inter-family sit-downs, manage political and judicial fixers, and act as a buffer between the soldiers and the boss.
- **The Caporegime.** Commands a neighborhood or racket regime of ten to twenty-five made men plus dozens of associates.
- **The Soldier (made man).** A full inducted member of Cosa Nostra. By institutional convention a made man cannot be killed or struck by another made man without a boss's sanction, though in practice soldiers who cross the wrong line suffer covert "walk-away" disappearances when their killers believe they can hide the deed. Runs his own shylock book, gambling sheet and a W-2 front job, and kicks up a share of his net operating take to his capo every Friday (typically 15 to 20 percent; each family's rate is set by its boss and can change).
- **The Associate.** An unmade operator: from a hungry twenty-year-old neighborhood kid waiting for the books to open, to Jewish arson specialists, corrupt cops and Irish or Russian partners. An associate is placed "on record" with a soldier to have standing; without a soldier vouching for him he has no institutional protection.

#### How practical influence overrides formal rank

Rank does not guarantee obedience. Practical power is a pattern that emerges from who owes whom, and the codex's six levers describe it. They are not scores to track; they are ways to read the state of the family.

1. **The envelope lever (revenue control).** A capo who brings in a large sum every Friday can quietly ignore a boss's minor edicts because the boss cannot afford to lose that income. Conversely, a broke consigliere loses practical respect even while holding a top title.
2. **The muscle lever (trigger loyalty).** Who do the buttonmen actually love or fear? If a boss orders a capo's execution, the hit succeeds only if the shooters are more loyal to, or more afraid of, the boss than attached to their capo.
3. **The access gate (the old man's ear).** Bosses isolate themselves in Oakridge or in backrooms to avoid bugs. Whoever drives the boss's car or sits in his kitchen controls what the boss hears, and can frame a rival by feeding the boss distorted rumors.
4. **Slow-walking and quiet obstruction.** Subordinates rarely defy a superior to his face. An unhappy capo slow-walks an order: he claims he could not find the target, blames police patrols on the Narrows Bridge, or holds back key information until the boss's plan fails by itself.
5. **Personal versus institutional loyalty.** Some soldiers are loyal to the family as a symbol; others are loyal only to the specific man who paid their mother's hospital bills.
6. **The non-Italian ceiling.** By custom only men of Italian descent through the father (an Italian father and surname) are inducted as made men. Non-Italian operators can become wealthy career associates or lead rival syndicates but are not made and do not vote at a family table. This is a custom of these four families, not a law of nature. A boss who made an exception would be breaking a convention with consequences: the other families, his own men, and the sponsors who vouched for the old rule would all have views, and the exception would be remembered.

### 3.2 Taboos with consequences, never impossible

**A taboo here is something the people who care will react to, not something that cannot happen.** Any of the following can be done. What follows depends on who learns of it, how sure they are, how much they care, and what they can do about it. The engine and the storyteller treat each taboo as an act with attached consequences, never as a wall.

For each taboo: who cares, and the plausible range of reactions.

#### Killing a cop

- **Who cares:** the whole precinct and department, the Feds, the press, the Governor, and every family whose business a manhunt would disrupt.
- **Reactions:** a killing brings the precinct and the FBI at once. Detectives on the pad will raid every social club in the city if a badge is killed. The families may turn the killer over, pressure him, or disown him to end the heat. Some crews would hide him, at a price.

#### Killing a reporter

- **Who cares:** the paper and its owners, the other papers and broadcasters, politicians, the Governor, federal authorities, and the families, who fear what a martyred reporter does to public tolerance.
- **Reactions:** national attention, a crackdown, and pressure from other bosses on whoever allowed it. The usual substitutes (smashing cameras, slashing tires, having pressmen "lose" a Sunday edition off the delivery trucks) exist precisely because a killing is so expensive.

#### Killing or striking a made man without sanction

- **Who cares:** the dead or injured man's capo, crew and sponsors; his boss; the other families.
- **Reactions:** a sit-down, a demand for the killer, a retaliatory body, or a war. A boss may quietly accept it, or even have wanted it. A made man's death that is hidden well enough may never be traced; if it is traced, the consequences attach to the act.

#### Holding out

- **Who cares:** the capo or boss being shorted, and anyone who learns of it.
- **Reactions:** from a tune-up and a debt, to demotion, to death for a large or repeated theft. The reaction scales with how much was hidden, how sure they are, and how much the man is worth to the crew.

#### Informing

- **Who cares:** everyone who might be named. Law enforcement protects the informant; the families hunt him.
- **Reactions:** suspicion alone can end a man's standing. A man who walks out of a grand jury room without being jailed for contempt is suspected by his own crew. A confirmed informant is hunted, and so are his relatives if the family is vicious enough; WITSEC can protect him for a price. Families sometimes kill the wrong man on bad information.

#### Dealing narcotics where the family forbids it

- **Who cares:** a boss and capos who forbid it for reasons of honor, heat or personal belief, and the young zealots who believe in that ban.
- **Reactions:** some bosses really forbid narcotics; many officially forbid them while quietly taking a cut through front men. A man caught may be ignored, taxed, demoted or killed, depending on whom he is dealing for and who is looking.

#### A made man's wife

- **Who cares:** the husband, his brothers-in-law and sponsors, and the family as a whole.
- **Reactions:** an affair with, or an insult to, a made man's wife can bring an unsanctioned killing, a beating, a forced exile or a sit-down. Inter-family marriages carry the problem into other families' houses.

#### Violence in the Oakridge sanctuary

- **Who cares:** all four families, the Oakridge residents, the police of the suburbs, and judges and politicians who live there.
- **Reactions:** gunfire or sirens in the Gold Coast risk severe retaliation from every family, and wealthy, well-connected people become hostile witnesses. Still, the sanctuary is a convention: it has been broken, and it can be broken again.

#### Disrespecting a boss in public

- **Who cares:** the boss, his men, and everyone watching.
- **Reactions:** from an apology and a payment, to a beating, to a sanctioned killing, depending on the boss's temperament, his current weakness or strength, and who witnessed it. A boss who swallows an insult has also decided something about himself.

**Permission and drift.** A boss can quietly permit what his family officially forbids. A taboo can fade when the people who enforced it are dead or discredited. Two crews can disagree about whether a given act was a violation at all. Whatever happens, the consequence flows through the knowledge and rumor systems: who learns what, and when.

### 3.3 Sacred traditions and domestic rules

- ***La Punciuta*, the making ceremony.** Books open around the Feast of San Rocco in August to replace dead or imprisoned soldiers. In a locked cellar before the boss, underboss and consigliere, a dagger and a revolver rest on white linen. The boss pricks the inductee's trigger finger with a silver needle and smears the blood on a prayer card of St. Jude, the patron saint of the city and its docks. As the burning card passes from palm to palm, the inductee swears in Sicilian dialect: *"May my flesh burn like this saint if I ever betray my brothers, my family, or the silence of this Thing of Ours."* Induction normally requires an Italian father, an open slot when the books open, and someone to vouch for the man; all three are conventions that can change, and a family may keep the books closed for years under federal heat.
- **The *compare* (sponsor) bond.** An established soldier raises a young recruit, sits him at his Sunday table and stakes his own life on the kid's reliability. Betraying a *compare* is considered worse than patricide, which makes it agonizing when a boss orders a man to lure his own mentor into a room lined with painters' drop-cloths.
- **The weekly rhythm (a general tendency, not a law).**
- *Monday, the count.* Bookies and numbers banks tally the weekend's sports action. Winners are paid by noon; losers have until Tuesday at 5:00 PM before the vig compounds.
- *Wednesday and Thursday, the work.* Sit-downs, union site inspections, hijackings and collections.
- *Friday afternoon, the envelope.* Associates kick up to soldiers, soldiers to capos, capos deliver to the boss. Shorting an envelope brings a tune-up; hiding a score is a capital offense if discovered.
- *Friday night, comare night.* Married wiseguys take mistresses (*comares*) to clubs and the Civic Arena fights.
- *Saturday night, wives' night.* Reserved for legal wives at the same restaurants; waiters pretend they have never seen the husbands before.
- *Sunday, Mass and gravy.* Morning Mass at St. Rocco's, then a four-hour family dinner.
- **The widow's purse and the slow rot.** By convention, when a made man goes to prison in silence (*stands tall*), his capo delivers a weekly cash envelope to his wife. In practice envelopes arrive faithfully in the first year (on the order of $1,500 a week), but by the third year greedy capos absorb the prisoner's shylock book, envelopes shrink to $200, and his wife works a bakery register. That slow rot is the single most common psychological pressure that makes a hardened veteran in a federal penitentiary consider flipping.

### 3.4 The four human molds

Age never dictates loyalty or morality in Saint Jude. Four recurring types cross every family and every generation.

1. **The rotten patriarchs (cynical elders).** Men in their sixties and seventies who preach about St. Jude at ceremonies and quietly finance heroin pipelines through front men. Terrified of dying on a steel cot in federal prison, an old capo facing a twenty-year RICO bid will often flip, or murder his own loyalists, faster than a young kid.
2. **The young zealots (true believers).** Young soldiers and capos who swallowed the myth of honor whole. They refuse to touch narcotics, dress in conservative wool suits, pay widows down to the last dollar, and grow disillusioned when they see that their gray-haired bosses are hypocrites.
3. **The old butchers (aging sociopaths).** Buttonmen in their late fifties and sixties who hate diplomacy and business fronts and survive on 1950s street brutality and impulsive diner shootings.
4. **The pragmatic sharks (any age).** Operators who treat the underworld as a balance of leverage, logistics and quiet disappearances, keeping bodies out of the newspapers so the money never stops.

### 3.5 The families as organizations

Each family is its people: its made men, its associates on record and off record, who answers to whom, and the money that flows up. Real strength comes from relationships and money. Rank is the formal picture and practical power can drift away from it.

**Money comes up from below.** Soldiers and capos open and run their own rackets and kick up a share of their net take. The boss runs the family's big interests and does not plan every book or hijack.

**Turf has three layers, with rough and shifting lines.**
- Street spots: blocks, bars, numbers routes and protected shops, held by a soldier or capo while neighbors encroach.
- Industries and unions: the port, concrete, trucking, garment, waste and sanitation, held through union locals and cartels and sometimes split between families by a tense agreement.
- People: who is on record with whom.

In real cities, street rackets were neighborhood-based while control of industries ran citywide through unions and cartels. The families here try to hold their own ground, contest it with each other and with the outside gangs, and share some places tensely.

**Agreements.** Deals between families and with outsiders have recorded terms: who gets what share of which business, for how long. Agreements are tense and kept up by mutual fear of ruin and of federal heat, not by mutual trust. Breaking one is an offense that others can learn of and react to.

**War.** Responses come from the person: temper, means, stakes and what he believes happened. There is no fixed ladder of escalation. A violent capo may jump straight to a hit; a cautious one may swallow an insult. Fear of mutual ruin and of federal heat pushes towards settling, not always successfully. Deniable work goes through off-record associates, outside gangs (the bikers, an Irish crew, the Kings) and freelancers (an arsonist, a forger), until the rumor mill pins it on someone; how certain each side is decides the response. The bloodiest wars are often internal (succession, coups and purges), but wars between families happen too.

### 3.6 The four families

These are family profiles, not rosters. Who leads each one, who is rising and who is in trouble is decided by the snapshot and the running world.

- **The Sabatini Family** (Crown Harbor and North San Lorenzo). The oldest and richest of the four, the "downtown" family, with the deepest ties to City Hall, the Department of Buildings and the judiciary. Its engine is construction: the Concrete Club bid-rigging cartel, ready-mix, and a Cadillac dealership. Its strength is cash; its vulnerabilities are an aging leadership and a split between young traditionalists and corrupt elders. It wants to keep financial supremacy without being dragged into a street war.
- **The Castellano Family** (the North Piers of Killbuck Point and South San Lorenzo). The dock family: the port, the waterfront union locals, the checkers and customs bypass, freight and the wholesale fish market. Hooks and pier rats to the downtown Sabatinis. Its vulnerabilities are federal pressure on the waterfront, internal succession ambition, and a long-running border friction with the Irish on the South Piers.
- **The Vannucci Family** (the Strip and the Ironworks). Born of the 1967 split. The smallest made roster of the four, squeezed between the Sabatini politicians and the Moretti expansionists. It runs the Neon Strip's nightclubs, poker, prostitution and strip joints, the steel foundries and rendering plants, and a joint sanitation cartel. It washes cash through nightlife and cares about vengeance and honor more than its means can afford.
- **The Moretti Family** (East Bellacorte and the Racks along the I-95 corridor). Born of the 1958 split. Garment, trucking, wholesale fencing, chop shops and rail yards. Aggressive and expansionist, poorer in political cover than the Sabatinis, with a restless leadership that provokes street skirmishes and a border with the Eastside projects and the Kings.

### 3.7 The outside organizations

- **The Charlestown Brigades** (Irish; South Piers and Kerrytown). Keep a rough half share of the port with the Castellanos, move guns and explosives (some of it for causes overseas) out of the South Piers, and hold fierce neighborhood loyalty in the Bricks. Divided between those who want steady port profit and those who want blood for the 1973 massacre.
- **The Vorovskoy Syndicate** (Russian and Odessa mob; Brighton Point). Fuel-tax daisy-chain fraud, gem and gold smuggling, medical-clinic fraud, contract killers. Treated as untrustworthy outsiders by all four families and dependent on lawyers and shell companies for paperwork. Pays a street tax to the families and tries to play them against each other.
- **The Mercer Street Kings** (Black; Eastside and the Strip). Wholesale cocaine and Eastside distribution, with supply lines up the I-95 corridor and by boat through Point Pleasant. Disciplined street soldiers and enormous volumes of small bills that need laundering, which makes them dependent on the Italian fronts. Greedy Italian capos eye their supply.
- **The Iron Horsemen MC** (outlaw bikers; Killbuck Flats). A straw-purchase gun pipeline up and down I-95, rural methamphetamine, contract arson and warehouse robbery. A fortified compound in the Flats with a machine shop. Seen as expendable low-class muscle by the made men and constantly targeted by ATF gun-tracing stings.

## 4. Law enforcement, wiretaps, courts and prison

Law enforcement operates across two tiers: the **municipal and state level** (permeable, reactive and transactional) and the **federal level** (proactive, patient and insulated from street bribery). These are tendencies. Individual officers and agents can be honest, ambitious, incompetent or compromised, and a bribe or a friendly judge changes the odds without guaranteeing the outcome.

### 4.1 Two badges: the 14th Precinct and the Federal Strike Force

| Feature | Saint Jude Police Department (SJPD), the 14th Precinct | The Feds (FBI, DEA, the U.S. Attorney's Strike Force) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Headquarters** | A soot-stained brick stationhouse at Mercer and 9th. | The 12th floor of the Crown Harbor Federal Building. |
| **Jurisdiction** | Street beatings, bar shootings, local bookmaking, numbers, arson, and dead bodies found in trunks. | RICO (racketeering), interstate hijacking, union extortion, wire fraud and wholesale narcotics. |
| **Corruption level** | A systemic tendency known as "the Pad". Vice and homicide detectives in the precinct collect monthly envelopes on the first of the month, although clean patrol captains and Internal Affairs exist and have to be worked around. | Near zero. Agents are rotated in from out of state, with no childhood ties to San Lorenzo or Kerrytown. An attempted bribe of a federal agent adds a federal felony count. |
| **Operational style** | Reactive. Respond after shootings stop, chalk bodies, take statements from witnesses who "saw nothing", and file folders unless public or media pressure forces a crackdown. | Proactive. Watch a social club through a telephoto lens for one to three years without making an arrest while building a multi-count RICO conspiracy case. |
| **Institutional convention** | Never shoot a uniformed cop. Even detectives on the Pad will burn down every social club in the city if a badge is killed. | Never swing at an agent serving a subpoena. Smile, offer an espresso, tell him to talk to the family's lawyer, and say nothing. |

#### How the Pad actually works, and its limits

Paying off a lieutenant's network at the 14th Precinct is not a magic wand that erases public homicides. It is an **early-warning and procedural-sabotage service**.

- **The 48-hour tip-off.** Before the State Police or a clean captain forces a raid on a floating crap game or a wire room, the lieutenant calls the payphone outside a family's club, lets it ring twice, hangs up, and calls back. The crew leaves two low-level kids and $500 on the table so the raiding cops get a newspaper bust, while the $100,000 bankroll is already gone.
- **Botching the chain of custody.** When a made man is pulled over with two .38s under the seat, a detective on the Pad writes the wrong license plate on the warrant affidavit or leaves the evidence log unsigned. The family's defense lawyer files a motion to suppress, the state judge tosses the gun charge, and the soldier walks.
- **Where the Pad fails.** If a shooting happens in broad daylight in front of civilian witnesses, hits a bystander, or lands on the front page of the *Daily Mirror*, the lieutenant cannot bury the file. He has to make an arrest to protect his own badge.

### 4.2 The electronic war: Title III wiretaps and counter-surveillance

- **How the FBI listens.** To plant a hidden microphone inside a private business or home, the special agent in charge must obtain a Title III federal court order, showing probable cause to a federal judge that normal investigative techniques have failed.
- **The black-bag insertion.** At 3:30 AM, FBI technical agents pick the lock of a social club, restaurant or construction trailer and wire tiny microphones ("probes") into the building's electrical wiring inside chandeliers, espresso machines or baseboard radiators.
- **The listening post.** The microphone transmits a short-range radio signal to a hollowed-out utility van parked two blocks away, or to a rented apartment across the street. Agents log visitors with 35mm cameras and operate Ampex reel-to-reel tape recorders. Under Title III minimization rules, if two men are talking about baseball or family dinner, agents must switch off the recorder and spot-check every couple of minutes.
- **Pen registers and trap-and-trace.** Attached to corner payphones at the telephone exchange, these print paper tapes of every seven-digit number dialed from that booth.
- **Counter-measures: the walk-talk.** Sensitive business (sanctioning a hit, splitting a hijack score, naming a partner) is conducted on noisy sidewalks behind cupped hands, so that lip-readers with binoculars cannot follow.
- **Counter-measures: acoustic gestures.** Stroking the chin silently refers to the boss; tugging the earlobe or touching the nose warns that someone in the room is suspected of wearing a wire.
- **Counter-measures: sweeps.** Retired telephone linemen on mob payrolls sweep social clubs with detectors on a regular schedule. When a live bug is found, savvy capos leave it in place and have soldiers sit beside it feeding false arguments and fake truck schedules onto the federal tapes.

### 4.3 Flipping, informants and the grand jury squeeze

1. **Confidential informants** ("stoolies"). Bookmakers, bartenders or low-level associates feeding tips to the FBI or the precinct for cash, or to have a minor gambling or drug charge quietly dropped. They are hidden behind code numbers and never testify in court.
2. **Cooperating witnesses** ("flipping"). When an operator is pinched facing a long sentence on narcotics or RICO predicates, the Feds play him tapes of his own boss complaining about him, show him photographs of his mistress, and offer a cooperation agreement: wear a concealed body recorder on his friends, testify in open court, and enter WITSEC with a new identity.
3. **The grand jury contempt trap.** When prosecutors lack enough evidence to indict a soldier, they serve an immunized subpoena to a secret federal grand jury and grant use immunity, stripping his Fifth Amendment right to silence. If he still refuses to answer, the federal judge holds him in civil contempt and locks him in the downtown federal holding center for up to 18 months without trial. **Paranoia trigger:** if a subpoenaed man spends three hours in the grand jury room and walks out without being jailed for contempt, his own crew immediately suspects he sang.

### 4.4 Bail, courtrooms and "reaching the box"

- **State court bail.** A 24-hour bail bond office across from the Municipal Courthouse posts bail within two hours for a 10 percent non-refundable cash premium plus the deed to a relative's San Lorenzo rowhouse as collateral.
- **Federal court.** Federal bail decisions follow the law in force at the time. Do not apply the preventive-detention provisions of the 1984 Comprehensive Crime Control Act to a defendant whose case predates them. Prosecutors may challenge the source of proposed bail collateral and courts may scrutinize it, but later statutory powers must not be imported into earlier years.
- **Jury tampering ("reaching the box").** Federal judges are hard to buy, so the family lawyers and the families target the twelve civilian jurors. Only one holdout out of twelve is needed to force a hung jury and a mistrial. Leaked jury pool lists are cross-referenced against union halls, parish rosters and bookmakers until a compromised relative is found and offered a $25,000 envelope plus a mortgage payoff.

### 4.5 Doing a bid: state and federal prison ("the College")

1. **The downtown federal holding center ("the Triangle").** A grim fourteen-story brutalist building connected to the Federal Courthouse by an underground tunnel. It houses active soldiers and protective-custody informants on separate tiers. Bribed guards smuggle prosciutto, cigars and messages in hollowed-out pens, and occasionally unlock a protective-custody door for five minutes.
2. **Ironhaven State Penitentiary ("the Castle").** A 1908 Gothic granite fortress two hours upstate, for state murder, armed robbery and assault convicts. Racially divided and violent, yet D-Block is feudal royalty for connected wiseguys: slipping $300 a week to rural guards earning $14,000 a year buys silk robes, hot plates for cooking steak and garlic in cells, prison sports pools and street convicts as bodyguards.
3. **Federal time.** White-collar earners and frail elders fight for assignment to minimum-security camps like Danbury; hardened soldiers and capos go to walled penitentiaries like USP Lewisburg, where all four families share the "Italian Table" in the mess hall and watch for signs of the slow rot.

## 5. The economy of face, nightlife and social reactivity

Power here is half physical and half theater. With no public internet, restaurants, nightclubs, boxing arenas and street corners are the live stage where a man's standing is broadcast and tested.

### 5.1 The four currencies of street standing

These are qualitative interpretive lenses for judging how people react to a character and a crew. They are not numerical stats.

| Currency | What it measures | How it is broadcast | The double-edged sword |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Weight (gravitas, rank)** | Institutional backing and political juice: who vouches for you, whose ring you kissed, and what happens if someone touches you. | Who stands up when you enter a social club; whether a capo walks with you on the sidewalk; whether the precinct looks away when you double-park at a hydrant. | High weight makes street punks bow and makes you a prime target on the federal strike force's corkboard. |
| **2. Iron (menace, body count)** | Raw, proven capacity for violence: are you an earner who pays for muscle or a man who actually pulls the trigger? | The quiet hush when your car pulls up; scars, cold eyes, and rumors of who did not come home after taking a ride with you. | High iron means debtors pay on Monday without excuses, but legitimate businessmen and politicians are terrified to be photographed with you. |
| **3. Flash (bankroll, liquidity)** | Visible spendable wealth and generosity. | The cut of your wool coat, the year of your Cadillac, peeling off crisp hundreds for valets, buying rounds, a high-maintenance comare. | High flash draws hangers-on, corrupt cops looking for bigger bribes, the jealousy of broke old-timers, and IRS net-worth audits. |
| **4. Word (honor, reliability)** | Whether a handshake from you is a gilded bond or a trap: do you pay your markers, protect your crew and keep your mouth shut? | Old-school elders trusting you with sensitive sit-downs; outside syndicates willing to extend large amounts of contraband on consignment. | High word earns die-hard loyalty from your men, while ruthless pragmatists try to exploit your predictability. |

### 5.2 The nightlife stage: how a room reacts

Walking through the door of a major nightclub or restaurant at 11:00 PM triggers a visible ritual of status.

- **The curb and the velvet rope.** A nobody parks three blocks away in the rain, gets patted down at the rope and pays the cover. For a respected earner the bouncer unhooks the rope before his shoes hit the pavement, parks his Lincoln or Corvette under the neon marquee in the "boss spot", skips the frisk, and whispers who is already inside ("careful tonight, there are four guys from Kerrytown in the back corner").
- **The maitre d's dance and the floating table.** Low-level guys are seated near the swinging kitchen doors or the drafty front window, exposed to sidewalk shooters. When a heavyweight arrives on a Friday night, waiters carry a round mahogany table out of a storage closet and set it in the center of the floor or against the "power wall": solid brick behind the spine, with sightlines on every entrance.
- **Sending bottles and the tribute walk.** Sending a bottle of champagne or good whiskey across the room tests standing: raising a glass accepts peace, while telling the waiter "take it back, tell him I bought my own" publicly humiliates the sender. Lower-ranking men walk over during the meal, wait silently with hands clasped until the heavyweight pauses, kiss cheeks if both are made, and never pull up a chair unless invited.

### 5.3 The five social mirrors and the 48-hour rumor mill

1. **Mirror 1: neighborhood civilians.** Small-time punks wait in line. Men with weight and honor see lines part at the butcher shop and owners refuse payment (so you slip a folded $50 into the apron anyway, so you never owe a civilian a favor). Men who rule purely by fear (*cafoni*, *gavones*) get shaking hands and averted eyes and no warning calls when the FBI raids the block.
2. **Mirror 2: your own crew.** Your reputation radiates onto "your people". If you pull off a heist or survive an ambush, your twenty-one-year-old driver drinks free on Mercer Avenue because touching him means answering to you. If you back down at a sit-down or let a debtor short you, your bookies start skimming by Tuesday and your bodyguard starts looking for a stronger capo.
3. **Mirror 3: peer wiseguys (tall poppy syndrome).** Kicking up consistent envelopes earns praise at Villa Roma, but getting too rich or flashing a nicer car than your capo turns warmth to ice, and may bring suicidal assignments designed to get you killed before you outgrow your collar.
4. **Mirror 4: rival syndicates.** The Irish test personal guts and straight dealing; the Russians test cold discipline and punctuality; the Kings test modern cash-flow vision and corporate respect.
5. **Mirror 5: upperworld and badges.** Beat cops go from slapping your hood with nightsticks to calling you "Mister", and a precinct lieutenant meets you behind the racetrack. Politicians take your cash in coatrooms, while old-money wives at the Oakridge country club still whisper behind tennis rackets when your wife walks past the pool.

**The 48-hour rumor mill.** *Hours 1 to 6:* tow-truck scanner drivers, ER nurses and 4:00 AM dockworkers see the physical aftermath. *Hours 6 to 24:* barbers, bail bondsmen and wire-room clerks piece together who did it, and overdue debtors show up breathless with full envelopes. *Hours 24 to 48:* the tabloid's crime columnist prints a blind item in the *Daily Mirror*, and the best nightclub rearranges your table on Friday night.

## 6. Media, church, labor, medicine and upperworld networks

### 6.1 The media and the Catholic Church

- **Print and broadcast.** The 25-cent tabloid (the *Saint Jude Daily Mirror*, with a star crime columnist whom rival capos use to leak embarrassing tips) competes with the broadsheet (the *Saint Jude Chronicle*) and with Channel 4 Action News, whose shoulder-mounted camera crews wait outside St. Rocco's. By institutional convention, killing a journalist is forbidden because it draws in the Governor and the White House; instead crews smash cameras, slash tires, or have union pressmen "lose" 50,000 copies of the Sunday edition off the delivery trucks.
- **The Catholic Church paradox.** The families are top private donors to the Archdiocese of Saint Jude. The pastor of St. Rocco's on the Hill hears buttonmen's confessions, blesses Cadillacs and refuses to help the FBI, while the rector of St. Jude's cathedral in Kerrytown may publicly refuse blood-money envelopes and preach against dockside heroin. Being denied a Mass of Christian Burial by the Archdiocese is the ultimate post-mortem disgrace for a boss's family. How individual priests behave is a matter for the people involved, not for this bible.

### 6.2 Domestic dynasties, arenas, the seasonal pulse and going on the lam

- **Dynastic marriages and the "prince" problem.** Inter-family marriages mean that domestic abuse, or flaunting comares in front of a wife's made brothers, can trigger unsanctioned honor killings. Spoiled sons of made men snort cocaine at the clubs and pick fights with dangerous men who are not allowed to hit them back, until a shooting drags two families into war.
- **Glitz and fix arenas.** Friday night fights at the Saint Jude Civic Arena (fighters going into the tank in the sixth round to clear a trainer's shylock debt; ringside doctors stopping fights early on minor cuts for 8-to-1 odds). The Bayview Downs racetrack backstretch (veterinarians administering sodium bicarbonate "milkshakes" or tranquilizers). Concert promoters paying cash security fees, and indebted singers performing free at Oakridge weddings.
- **The seasonal pulse.** *August, the Feast of San Rocco:* a five-day heatwave festival in San Lorenzo, a statue pinned with 500 bills, cellar inductions, and scores settled under the noise of street bands. *November and December, holiday hijack season:* highways choked with furs, Scotch and electronics, and the big "Christmas envelopes" due on December 20th. *January and February, dead winter:* freezing gales freeze the ground solid in the Pines (forcing furnace and vat disposals), and bosses winter in Miami or Palm Beach, leaving underbosses in charge during prime mutiny season.
- **Going on the lam.** A document forger in the Racks pulls "dead baby passes" from church baptismal archives to obtain genuine birth certificates, Social Security numbers and passports. Fugitives hide in shuttered Point Pleasant bungalows or in Hallandale, Florida condos funded by I-95 shoebox cash couriers, while rivals at home quietly carve up their undefended territory.

### 6.3 Hidden labor, midnight medicine, information and the 2:00 AM law

- **Labor unions.** A Teamsters local (feudal commercial trash stops; independent haulers get dumpsters torched or filled with wet cement), a hotel and restaurant workers' local (planting hotel maids to search out-of-town prosecutors' luggage and bartenders to eavesdrop on executives after midnight), and a municipal sanitation local (fabricating "emergency water main repairs" to close streets during drops or excavations). The longshoremen's local on the docks and the building trades are the other great levers. The number of each local is a detail of the snapshot.
- **The midnight doctor circuit.** State law requires emergency rooms to report gunshot wounds within two hours, so crews use a sterile clinic above a San Lorenzo pharmacy or addicted "midnight doctors" ($5,000 cash per kitchen-table extraction). For severe internal trauma, bribed shift supervisors at Mercy General Hospital admit patients through the 3:00 AM loading dock under fake civilian names, with an armed soldier guarding the hallway.
- **The street information network.** Tow-truck drivers monitor high-band police scanners around the clock and call mob contacts before police dispatch when crashes or bodies are found, so that crews can recover dropped guns. Neighborhood tailors clean wool suits stained with blood or gun oil in silence. Morticians pass whispered messages between rival capos standing by open caskets.
- **The 2:00 AM law and the 4:00 AM shift change.** Legal bars lock their doors at 2:00 AM, shifting action to steel-door after-hours clubs. Between 3:30 AM and 5:00 AM the precinct's night shift rotates off before the morning patrolmen hit the streets, which makes it the prime window for hits, disposals and heavy cargo drops.

### 6.4 The upperworld machine: politics, real estate, banking and port tycoons

- **Municipal politics.** The Department of Buildings and Inspections (inspectors slap three stop-work red tags on independent developers who refuse mob concrete until bank interest bankrupts them); City Council ward heelers (diverting paving funds and reassigning aggressive precinct commanders to suburban traffic duty); and the County District Attorney's office (permeable state prosecutors funded by political action committees who "lose" ballistics files or reduce murder charges to manslaughter).
- **Real estate, offshore banking and tycoons.** Mob shell companies use straw buyers to purchase condemned tenements for pennies and flip them to HUD urban-renewal developers at a 400 percent markup when rezoning tips leak six months early. Trust lawyers set up Curacao and Cayman discretionary trusts, and corrupt vice presidents at the local banks issue inflated construction loans on mob fronts. At Killbuck Point, shipping executives facing wildcat strikes that cost millions a day invite Castellano or Moretti capos to private lunches, exchanging cash envelopes so that labor grievances vanish and ships unload on schedule.

## 7. Racket economics and competency reference

This section keeps the codex's accounting vocabulary and rates as reference for judging deals. It is a reference ledger, not a rule that fixes any outcome. Appendix B takes precedence wherever a figure here conflicts with it, and any worked example must balance. Figures marked "Assumption:" were garbled in the source and have been recomputed or reconstructed here; each is listed under Adaptation notes.

### 7.1 Accounting rules

**Gross handle, net operating take and distributable profit.**
- **Gross handle or gross receipts** is the total money wagered into a sports or numbers book, or the total cash rung into a nightclub or front register. Tribute is calculated on net operating take, not on gross handle.
- **Net operating take (the tribute base)** is gross receipts minus direct operating payouts: winning bettors, the finger (a tipster's fee, about 10 percent of the expected score), a drop garage, the pad, and legitimate front overhead such as rent, liquor suppliers and union wages. Family kick-ups and tributes are percentages of net operating take.
- **Skim versus washed profit.** In a front business, the skim is untaxed cash pulled off the top of the register before accounting. It is immediate dirty cash but raises tax and liquor-board audit risk. Washed cash is dirty street money rung into a pizzeria or bakery register as false sales; it loses a share to taxes, and what remains is clean and bankable. Assumption: about 30 percent is a rough planning figure for that loss, not a legal rate, and Section 7.4 item 1 explains why no universal figure applies.

**Loansharking rates and rules.** Weekly rates are never mixed with annual rates.

| Tier of loan | Rate | Typical size | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wholesale family rate | 1 to 1.5 percent a week | Up to about $100,000 | Lent by a boss or capo to a made man as capital to put on the street. Assumption: the codex quotes 1 percent in one place and 1.5 percent in another; the range covers both. |
| Commercial business rate | 2 to 3 percent a week | $20,000 to $250,000 or more | Lent to garment manufacturers, restaurateurs and developers. Assumption: the figure lost from the source is reconstructed from the playbook range of $20,000 to $100,000 for ordinary borrowers, with larger sums for developers. |
| Street "points" rate | 5 percent a week | $1,000 to $10,000 | Standard street loan to gamblers and small hustlers: $50 a week in vig per $1,000 borrowed. |
| "Six-for-five" knockdown | 20 percent a week | $100 to $2,000 | Short-term micro-loans to desperate patrons, dockworkers and corner hustlers: borrow $5 on Monday, owe $6 on Friday. |

**The principal rule (a tendency).** On standard shylock loans, borrowers are usually not allowed partial principal payments that reduce their weekly vig. A debtor owing $10,000 at 3 points pays $300 every Friday until he can pay the full $10,000 in one lump sum, or until the crew converts the unpaid debt into a 51 percent equity takeover (a bust-out).

**Sports book math (the 11-to-10 rule).** Bettors risk $110 to win $100.
- Worked example: a book takes balanced action of $100,000 measured by the amount to be won, $50,000 on each side. Losers pay in $55,000 (the $50,000 stakes plus the 10 percent vig), the book pays winners $50,000, and it keeps $5,000, which is 5 percent of the $100,000 handle.
- Assumption: the source's figures for this example were garbled; the numbers above are the only consistent reading of the surviving text (11-to-10 odds, $55,000 collected, 5 percent of a $100,000 handle). The $5,000 is the book's margin before its own costs; the finger, the pad and layoff fees come out of it before any tribute base is computed.
- On unbalanced action, the bookie either gambles by holding the risk or pays a 2 to 5 percent fee to lay off the excess with a master layoff bank about 30 minutes before kickoff.

**Numbers and policy bank math (the handle method).** The day's winning number is a three-digit number from 000 to 999, derived from the parimutuel handle of the first seven races at the Bayview Downs racetrack.
- True odds are 999 to 1 against, or one in a thousand. Assumption: the street payout is 600 to 1 (the source figure was lost); this means winning hits pay out about 60 percent of the handle over time, consistent with the source's "55 to 60 percent". Heavily played "cut numbers" pay less. Assumption: 500 to 1 for a cut number, since the source's example number and payout were lost.
- Out of the gross handle: street runners keep 20 to 25 percent, the soldier or controller takes 5 to 10 percent, winning hits consume about 55 to 60 percent over time, and the capo's policy bank clears about 10 to 15 percent as net operating take. For example 22.5 plus 7.5 plus 57.5 leaves 12.5.

### 7.2 Competency tiers and the plain-English rule

**Plain-English playability.** A player never needs to memorize mob slang, pager codes or accounting math to play. The player may state actions in plain everyday English ("I want to lend $5,000 to some guys at the bar" or "I want to hijack a liquor truck"). The storyteller translates that intent into historically plausible procedures for the campaign year, having crew members or contacts guide the setup in character and glossing slang through context.

| Competency tier | Rank or archetype | Typical capital and reach | Operational scope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

## 8. Operational playbooks: how the rackets work

### 8.1 Classic racket playbooks by competency

**Working a shylock book (loansharking).**
- *Tier 1, collector and knockdown guy.* Put $5,000 of hijack cash, or $10,000 borrowed from your soldier at the wholesale rate, on the street in chunks of $500 to $2,000 to bar patrons and dockworkers on six-for-five or 5 points. Drive bar to bar every Friday afternoon collecting envelopes. If a man hides, track him down, deliver a non-lethal tune-up (two fingers in a car door, since dead men do not pay vig) and add a $200 late penalty to next Friday's payment.
- *Tier 2 and 3, commercial banker and bust-out artist.* Lend $20,000 to $100,000 at 2 to 3 points a week to garment sweatshop owners, restaurateurs and subcontractors turned down by banks. When an owner misses three Friday payments, step in with an accountant to take a silent 51 percent share of the deed, put an associate at the front register, max out the store's 90-day supplier credit lines on prime beef, liquor or electronics, fence the inventory out the back at 50 cents on the dollar, and hire an arson specialist to burn the empty building for the insurance payout.

**Pulling a cargo hijack (trucking and pier theft).**
- *Step 1, buying the finger.* Pay 10 percent of the expected score to a corrupt dispatcher, shipping clerk or other insider for a verified truck number, cargo value and route, so you do not hijack 40,000 pounds of worthless drywall.
- *Step 2, secure the drop garage and the master fence first.* Before rolling, rent an Ironworks warehouse bay for $2,500 (to get the rig off the street before police helicopters spot the roof numbers at sunrise) and secure a commitment from a master fence to buy the load at 35 cents on the dollar.
- *Step 3, the heist.* Either a friendly give-up (pay the driver $5,000 to leave the keys in the ignition at a Route 9 diner at 4:00 AM and tape him in the men's room so that he keeps his union pension) or a cowboy hijack (box the rig in at the Narrows Bridge ramp at gunpoint).
- *The payout math (illustrative, not a guaranteed result).* Assume a load with a legitimate resale value of $200,000. A fence paying 35 percent offers $70,000. If the agreed finder fee is 10 percent of that offer ($7,000), and the stated vehicle and warehouse and driver costs are $2,500 and $5,000, the remaining pool is $55,500 before any unlisted losses or payments. If 80 percent is distributed to a three-person crew ($44,400, or $14,800 each) and 20 percent is retained for the organizer and contingencies ($11,100), the arithmetic balances. A fence reselling at 85 percent of estimated retail would receive $170,000 and have a $100,000 gross spread before resale costs, taxes, losses and overhead. These assumptions must be re-estimated for each deal.

**Running a sports book and numbers bank.**
- *Tier 1, sheet writer and runner.* Take bets in bars or bodegas on flash paper (which burns instantly with a cigarette touch if vice walks in); call action into a basement wire room using a call-forwarding "cheesebox"; enforce the Tuesday 5:00 PM settle-up on bounced checks.
- *Tier 2 and 3, backer and layoff manager.* Put up a $50,000 bankroll for a civilian bookmaker, pay the precinct pad $1,000 a month for raid warnings, and split net operating take 50/50. Manage the Sunday 12:30 PM layoff dilemma: decide whether to hold heavy one-way neighborhood action on the home team or pay the vig to lay off $100,000 with a master bank.

**Moving weight (cocaine and heroin insulation rules).**
- *Street level, associate or outsider.* Physically touches the package, drives trap-cars, cuts club powder with inositol and lidocaine, and faces long mandatory federal minimums, plus execution by his own capo if caught dealing without kicking up a silent cut.
- *Financier level, made man or capo.* Never sees or touches the powder. Hands a gym bag of "loan" cash ($150,000) to an unmade front man in a steam room for a 40 percent return on delivery. Cannot call a formal sit-down if a drug shipment is stolen.

### 8.2 Working the five classic front businesses (wash versus skim)

1. **Neighborhood cash front** (pizzeria, espresso bar, butcher shop; Tier 1 to 2; illustrative capital $8,000 to $40,000). Treat reported sales, actual customer sales, inventory purchases, payroll, rent, utilities, taxes and cash deposits as separate ledger entries. A quoted amount of cash passed through a register is not profit and does not mean that the same amount becomes clean, spendable income. There is no universal 30 percent tax rule; tax exposure depends on the business, the reported income, deductions and legal circumstances. Use the Appendix B business ledger and reconcile all cash.
2. **Nightlife front** (supper club, disco, strip joint; costs and skim depend on scale). Record gross sales, legitimate operating costs, inventory, payroll, licence costs, taxes, cash removed by owners and unexplained shortages separately. A weekly skim is money diverted from the business, not a cost-free additional profit stream; it reduces reported cash and may create inventory, accounting, tax or partner-dispute exposure.
3. **Blue-collar route front** (jukeboxes, pinball and arcade machines, linen supply; Tier 2 to 3). Wash dirty street cash by stuffing it into coin and dollar bags attributed to jukeboxes and amusement machines, with no paper receipts; expand territory by sending associates to throw a rival's jukeboxes onto the sidewalk. Video-game cabinets belong to the late 1970s onward, and specific titles such as Pac-Man belong to late 1980 and later. 4. **Logistics front** (trucking, warehousing, towing; Tier 2 to 3). Weld a false 4-foot steel bulkhead inside a 40-foot refrigerated trailer for smuggling space. ### 9.3 Open ventures and the five-question formula

**Open ventures that can be launched whenever the date allows.**
- *College basketball point-shaving.* Bribing starters at the local state university with a couple of thousand dollars a game, gold chains and car leases to win by 6 points instead of the 10-point spread.
- *Indie record labels and radio payola.* Taking over a small record label, bribing East Coast disc jockeys to spin a single, pressing bootleg cassettes, and forcing every bar jukebox to buy 500 copies.
- *The "velvet rope" escort and blackmail ring.* A high-end call-girl paging service in luxury apartments, compromising visiting executives, judges and state senators.
- *Asbestos abatement and demolition scams.* Charging the city millions for certified school hazmat removal during an asbestos enforcement push, paying non-union kids a few dollars an hour in paper dust masks to rip it out, and dumping the bags in the Flats at midnight. Date-gated: asbestos enforcement is a later-decade development, not a defined 1980 panic.
- *Armored car and airport vault heists.* Turning an inside guard at an armored-car company or the airport cargo vault for a multi-million-dollar cash grab.
- *Casino bust-out junkets.* Chartering buses or flights to Atlantic City on mob credit, skimming junket commissions and seizing the companies of businessmen who lose. Date-gated: Atlantic City's first legal casino opened in 1978, so this is not available in a 1975 city.
- *Olive oil and produce adulteration.* Mixing cheap soybean oil into drums labeled as imported Sicilian extra-virgin olive oil.

**The five-question universal formula (how any new venture is evaluated).**
1. **Whose turf is the roof under?** It requires family membership in that district or a 10 to 20 percent street rent paid to the local capo.
2. **Which union touches the supply chain?** The dock longshoremen, the Teamsters, the hotel and restaurant workers or the building trades.
3. **What is the current-year paper trail?** Dirty cash needs a wash front and has to avoid the $10,000 bank currency-transaction report; clean-paper businesses provide a skim, a W-2 shield or a bust-out.
4. **Who needs an envelope on the first of the month?** The precinct pad for street and vice rackets, the Department of Buildings or state commissioners for commercial ones.
5. **Whose rice bowl are you breaking?** Every new dollar pulls customers from an existing capo or syndicate and generates competitive friction within weeks.

## 10. Examples of the kind of pressure Claude might invent

**These are examples, not a list, and not facts of the setting.** The source codex opened with three fixed flashpoints. They are not part of the bible, are not stored anywhere, and are not scheduled. Pressures are invented by the storyteller, from the live state of the city, and checked by the engine against what is possible in the period and what the world already holds. An invented pressure must name its people, places, money and dates, and the world must be able to support it.

The following examples only show the kind of thing that could arise. None of them is required, expected or happening.

- A boss faces a federal racketeering trial. His subordinates calculate how to survive it, who might testify, and who would inherit; an ambitious underboss or capo quietly sounds out other families about the succession.
- A large drug shipment disappears from a pier before the checkers can stamp it. The men who secretly financed it do not know whether a low-level hijack crew took it, a federal agency seized it to see who panics, or one of the partners robbed the others.
- A junior employee of a family's lawyer holds the books of more than one illicit enterprise and is taken into protective custody before a grand jury. Every faction hunts for the hotel room.
- A promising young earner with a legitimate business is recruited by a capo who needs his warehouse, and has to decide what he owes and to whom.
- A reporter's questions about a missing contractor start to matter to a precinct lieutenant, who needs the story to end.

## 11. Street dictionary, pager codes and wiretap euphemisms

The slang is period street talk and some of it is deliberately rude or offensive; it is here so that characters can speak in voice. A player never needs to learn it, and the storyteller glosses it through context.

### 11.1 Status, Hierarchy & People

* **A Friend of Ours (*Amico Nostro*):** An inducted Made Man. A formal, binding credential certifying both the speaker and the man introduced have taken the blood oath. Misrepresenting an unmade man as *Amico Nostro* is a capital offense.
* **A Friend of Mine:** Anyone who is **not** an inducted member of Cosa Nostra—an associate, an Irish dock boss, a corrupt cop, or a civilian partner. Signals to other Made Men: *Watch your mouth and remember he does not have the protection of the button.*
* **Earned His Button / Straightened Out:** Inducted into a family via *La Punciuta*.
* **Opening the Books / The Books Are Closed:** Allowing new inductions vs. freezing inductions due to federal heat or internal paranoia.
* **On Record:** Formal registration of an associate under a specific Made Man. Nobody from another crew or family can tax, recruit, or touch you without going to a sit-down with your Soldier first.
* **A Knockaround Guy:** A career street operator who doesn't hold a 9-to-5 job, knows how to keep his mouth shut, and makes his living entirely through hustles, collections, and scores.
* **A Pezzonovante (Literally ".90 Caliber"):** A high-ranking boss, politician, or heavyweight figure—often used with quiet resentment by street soldiers referring to ivory-tower bosses in Oakridge.
* **Compare (*Com-pah-reh*):** Godfather to your child or your sponsor in the family; colloquially used between deeply loyal blood-brothers in a crew.
* **Comare (*Goomah*):** A wiseguy’s steady mistress, kept in a separate condo on the Neon Strip or taken out on Friday nights.
* **A Wonderbread / A Medigan (*Mericano*):** A mainstream white American civilian outside neighborhood culture (like an out-of-town developer or an FBI agent).
* **A Gavone (*Cafone*):** A loud, uncultured, flashy slob who brings heat on himself by throwing hundred-dollar bills around nightclubs or talking too loud in restaurants.
* **A Clock-Puncher / A Citizen:** A straight civilian who works a legitimate job for a weekly paycheck.

### 11.2 Money, Rackets & The Math

* **A Point:** One percent interest per week on a shylock loan, or one percent ownership in a legitimate front business or nightclub.
* **The Vig / The Juice:** Weekly interest payment on a loanshark debt, or the 10 percent commission charged by a sports bookie on losing bets.
* **Six-for-Five:** Harsh street-level knockdown loan rate: borrow five dollars on Monday, owe six dollars on Friday (20 percent weekly interest).
* **A Nickel / A Dime / A Yard / A Large:**
* *A Nickel:* $500 (or $5,000 in high-roller games; also means a 5-year prison sentence).
* *A Dime:* $1,000 (or $10,000; also means a 10-year prison sentence).
* *A Yard:* $100 (or $100,000 in commercial real estate/construction).
* *A Large:* $1,000 (*"He's into the book for twenty large"*).


* **Putting Money on the Street:** Investing lump-sum capital (from a hijack or drug score) into a shylock book so it generates permanent weekly vig.
* **A Bust-Out:** Deliberately bankrupting a legitimate business after the owner falls behind on a loanshark debt—maxing out wholesale credit lines, fencing inventory out the back door, and burning the building for the insurance check.
* **Swag:** Stolen wholesale cargo from a hijacked truck or shipping container before it is fenced.
* **A Give-Up:** Staged hijacking or burglary where the truck driver, night watchman, or store owner is in on the score for a 10 percent cut.
* **The Edge / The Nut:** *The Nut* is the fixed weekly overhead a crew has to pay out (Friday kick-up to the Capo plus the 14th Precinct bribe). *The Edge* is the unfair advantage a wiseguy demands in every business deal.
* **Holding Out:** Hiding a score or reporting a smaller profit to your Capo so you can pocket the difference instead of kicking up the full percentage. Punishable by death.
* **Washing the Paper / The Spin Cycle:** Laundering dirty street cash (especially $5 and $10 drug bills from the Eastside) through high-volume cash fronts like *The Starlight Cabaret*, vending routes, or parish casino nights.

### 11.3 Violence, Work & Disposals

* **A Piece of Work / Doing a Job:** A contract murder.
* **Getting Whacked / Getting Clipped / Gone:** Executed. When a man disappears into rendering vats or slag furnaces, street guys speak of him exclusively in the past tense the very next morning: *"Frankie? Frankie’s gone. Don't look for him."*
* **A Tune-Up / Giving Him a Message:** Non-lethal beating delivered to a late debtor or disrespectful civilian (broken fingers in a car door or pistol-whipping) designed to keep the victim out of the morgue so he can still pay his vig next Friday.
* **Going to the Mattresses:** Renting anonymous safe-house apartments across town where buttonmen sleep on bare mattresses with shotguns by the door during an all-out shooting war.
* **A Double-Drop ("A Cold Car"):** Stolen four-door work car fitted with license plates stolen off an identical make-and-model commuter car so police dispatchers can't flag the plates during a hit.
* **Iron / A Piece / A Roscoe:** A firearm.
* *A Dirty Piece:* A gun that has been used in a shooting ("has a body on it").
* *A Clean Piece:* An untraceable firearm with acid-etched serial numbers fresh off the Iron Horsemen’s southern pipeline or a Moretti family armory.


* **Taking a Ride to the Pines:** Driving a condemned man thirty miles west into the **Pines** to be buried in the cranberry bogs.
* **Pouring Him into the Skyline:** Sabatini crew slang for dropping a body into a 60-foot bedrock caisson shaft in Crown Harbor before morning concrete trucks arrive.
* **Going in the Vat / Turning into Soap:** Vannucci crew slang for disposing of a corpse in the industrial bone-grinders and tallow rendering tanks at *San Lorenzo Provisions*.
* **Making Him a Bumper:** Moretti crew slang for locking a body inside a car trunk and crushing it into a steel cube in the hydraulic baler at *Eastside Auto Crush*.
* **Jewish Lightning / Italian Remodeling:** Hiring an arson specialist to burn down a failing restaurant or busted-out warehouse for the commercial fire insurance payout.

### 11.4 Law Enforcement, Courts & Prison Slang

* **The Pad:** Monthly bribery payroll managed by 14th Precinct Vice and Homicide squads.
* **The G / The Feebs / The Choirboys:** The FBI Organized Crime Strike Force on the 12th Floor of the Federal Building.
* **Wearing a Nagra / Wired Up:** Carrying a concealed **Nagravision SN** miniature reel-to-reel tape recorder strapped to the chest or groin for the U.S. Attorney.
* **A Stoolie / A Rat / Going Bad:** Turning state's evidence or becoming a Confidential Informant.
* **Going into the Program:** Entering the Federal Witness Security Program (WITSEC).
* **Standing Tall / Doing It on One Foot:** Taking a long prison sentence in total silence without implicating your crew or Boss.
* **The Contempt Trap:** Being granted Use Immunity before a Federal Grand Jury and choosing to serve 18 months in the **downtown federal holding center** for civil contempt rather than answer questions.
* **Reaching the Box:** Bribing or intimidating one of the twelve jurors in a criminal trial to guarantee a hung jury.
* **Going Away to College:** Going to federal or state prison (*The Triangle* = the downtown federal holding center; *The Castle* = Ironhaven State Penitentiary).

## Appendix A: Dated technology, legal and forensic control sheet (1980 default)
### A1. What the opening year means
The default economic reference frame is the early 1980s, a period of high inflation, high interest rates, industrial restructuring, and economic uncertainty. The Storyteller selects the campaign’s opening season and year to fit the story, then applies the corresponding historical conditions. Saint Jude is an East Coast port metropolis inspired by the scale and industrial mix of Newark/Jersey City, Boston, and Philadelphia, but its local prices are fictionalized assumptions anchored to national and regional benchmarks. The city is not a disguised exact copy of any one real city.
The 1980 economy is not a modern economy with old telephones. Information travels through paper, people, landlines, radio, physical records, and institutions. Delay and uncertainty matter. A rumour may take hours or days to travel; a bank reconciliation may take days; a ledger can be stolen or altered; a witness can be pressured; a telephone record may exist without revealing the words spoken. The absence of modern tools does not make police or criminals incompetent.
### A2. Technology adoption ladder
| Period | Plausible technology and social reach | Simulation rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1980–1981 | Landline telephones, payphones, answering services, radio dispatch, pagers in some professional/business circles, typewriters, carbon copies, paper ledgers, cash registers, 35mm photography, reel-to-reel and cassette recording, office photocopiers, early personal computers and terminals in specialist workplaces. | Paper and human networks dominate. Computers are possible but are not ubiquitous. No ordinary handheld cellular phones. |
| 1982–1983 | Increasing personal-computer visibility; more office automation; early commercial cellular networks and portable cellular handsets become commercially possible around 1983, but are rare and expensive. | Early adopters are wealthy firms, specialists, or technically sophisticated institutions. Cellular service depends on coverage and carrier access. |
| 1984–1985 | Handheld cellular phones are commercially available, but expensive, bulky, and uncommon; business computing spreads unevenly; video cassette ownership and rental become more visible. | Treat new tools as advantages owned by particular actors, not default city infrastructure. |
| 1986–1989 | Wider adoption of personal computers, fax machines, office databases, video rental/duplication, and cellular services; new recording and communications equipment becomes more accessible over time. | Adoption varies by wealth, sector, neighbourhood, skills, and access. | 1990 onward | Later technologies and systems may enter only when historically and locally plausible; electronic tolling, widespread digital networks, mobile location data, and modern surveillance must not be backdated into the early 1980s. | Date every major introduction; do not silently modernize the setting. |
**Critical distinctions:** a technology can exist without being available in Saint Jude; available without being affordable; affordable without being common; and common without being universally reliable. An NPC may own specialist equipment only if their resources, contacts, and competence justify it.
### A3. Opening communications baseline
- **Routine:** rotary and push-button landlines, payphones, written notes, in-person meetings, switchboards, secretaries, answering services, police and commercial two-way radio, mail, and physical message carriers.
- **Possible but not universal:** numeric pagers, beepers, portable tape recorders, commercial security alarms, closed-circuit cameras at specific sites, and computers used for accounting or institutional records.
- **Expensive specialist equipment:** installed car phones, advanced radio interception equipment, professional surveillance cameras, sophisticated alarm systems, and early computing systems.
- **Not available as routine 1980 infrastructure:** handheld commercial cellular phones, consumer text messaging, smartphone functions, cloud storage, GPS tracking of ordinary vehicles, widespread street-camera networks, internet search, social media, and modern digital location histories.
- **Communications consequences:** messages can be missed; people can be unavailable; payphones may be occupied or out of order; switchboard operators and secretaries can become witnesses; paper address books and call logs can be lost; and a trusted messenger may misunderstand or betray a sender.
### A4. Legal and forensic guardrails
Use laws and capabilities actually in force during the selected campaign year. In particular, do not apply the federal preventive-detention provisions introduced by the 1984 Comprehensive Crime Control Act to a defendant whose case predates their effective date. RICO and court-authorized Title III electronic surveillance already exist during the early 1980s, but their use remains constrained by law, evidence, staffing, budgets, and institutional priorities.
At the opening date, investigators may use witness statements, informants, surveillance, photographs, fingerprints, palm prints, conventional ballistics, handwriting and document examination, blood-group testing, hair and fibre comparison, telephone records, financial records, search warrants, grand juries, and authorized wiretaps. The quality and availability of each technique depend on the agency and case. DNA profiling is not a routine forensic tool available to Saint Jude investigators in 1980. Closed-circuit television does exist in limited settings; the correct rule is no modern, ubiquitous citywide CCTV network, not that cameras do not exist at all.
Do not treat legal procedure as magic. Corrupting a local detective may compromise a particular lead or evidence chain, but it cannot automatically erase every witness, federal file, newspaper story, or parallel investigation. A successful legal challenge needs a plausible procedural or evidentiary basis. Federal agents are not omniscient or incorruptible by metaphysical law; they are harder to influence through local street-level relationships and must still operate within institutional constraints.
### A5. Date audit notes
The source codex contains references that were written for a 1985 start and were corrected to 1980. Their presence in a later-decade appendix does not make them part of the opening state for the selected campaign year. Apply these controls:
- The handheld Motorola DynaTAC 8000X is not available as an ordinary commercial option in 1980; commercial handheld cellular service arrives later.
- Lincoln Town Cars are not an appropriate default 1980 model-year vehicle. Use contemporary Lincoln Continentals and other vehicles actually on the market; later models can appear as the calendar advances.
- The 1984 federal preventive-detention provisions do not apply at the selected campaign start.
- References to VHS duplication farms, credit-card processing tricks, later-era electronics, and other technology-dependent ventures are opportunities gated by market availability, cost, expertise, and local adoption, not guaranteed 1980 businesses.
- No specific credit-card authorization threshold is universal across issuers. Treat manual authorization and fraud controls as institution-specific, not as a magic fixed dollar amount.
- Any later-decade law, court practice, product, slang, business model, or cultural reference must be dated before it enters the active campaign.

### B7. Business accounting rules
Every operating business keeps a ledger with at least: opening cash, sales/revenue, cost of goods, gross profit, wages, rent, utilities, insurance, maintenance, licences and taxes, debt service, owner distributions, extraordinary losses, closing cash, inventory, receivables, payables, and asset value. A business can be profitable on paper but run out of cash because customers have not paid or stock must be purchased before sales arrive.
Use these definitions consistently:
- **Revenue:** money earned from sales or services before expenses.
- **Gross profit:** revenue minus direct cost of goods/services sold.
- **Operating profit:** gross profit minus ordinary operating expenses.
- **Net profit:** profit after applicable interest, taxes, and extraordinary costs.
- **Cash on hand:** liquid money actually available now; not the same as profit or asset value.
- **Working capital:** cash and near-cash resources needed to keep the business operating through normal payment cycles.
- **Owner distribution:** cash removed from the business after obligations are considered; not a business expense in the same sense as wages or rent.
- **Loan:** creates a liability for the borrower and an asset/receivable for the lender. Loan principal is not profit or revenue. Interest is income to the lender and an expense to the borrower.
- **Kick-up / tribute / protection payment:** record separately from legitimate operating costs so the business’s actual profitability and criminal obligation remain visible.
For an ordinary cash business, a rough starting assumption is that direct costs, payroll, rent, utilities, taxes, shrinkage, and other expenses consume a substantial share of revenue. Do not assign a fixed profit margin to every sector. Use the business type, sales volume, location, competition, owner competence, and local economy to set margins. If the player invests in a business, show the major assumptions rather than inventing a guaranteed return.
### B8. Credit, debt, and interest
| Credit type | Working convention | Simulation guidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary bank / secured business loan | Market rates vary materially in the high-interest 1980 environment; use a scenario-specific annual rate, generally far below weekly street-loan rates. | Creditworthiness, collateral, term, lender, and prevailing rates matter. Record principal, annual rate, payment schedule, fees, and security. |
| Supplier credit | Often 30 days, sometimes shorter or longer by trade and relationship. | A missed invoice can stop stock deliveries before the business becomes insolvent. |
| Personal loan from a known contact | Negotiated rate and schedule. | Record whether interest accrues, when payment is due, and what happens on default. |
| Illegal street loan | Negotiated or coercive terms; can be quoted weekly. | State the actual principal, payment amount, schedule, fees, and consequences. Never mix annual and weekly rates or calculate them as if equivalent. |
| Trade credit / invoice factoring | Discount or fee based on risk and speed. | A business may exchange future receipts for immediate cash at a cost. |
**Interest calculation rule:** state whether interest is simple or compounded and how often it accrues. One point per week means 1% of the outstanding principal per week only if the contract defines it that way; it is not equivalent to 1% per year. If a loan uses a fixed weekly payment or “six-for-five” structure, calculate from the actual amount advanced and due date rather than using a generic annual percentage formula.
### B9. Underworld prices and earnings: model with ranges, not magic numbers
Criminal markets are volatile and unusually dependent on trust, territory, police attention, product quality, supply, violence, and the identity of the parties. For that reason, the engine should not declare a universal “price of a racket.” It should estimate a deal from the following components:
1. **Capital at risk:** money tied up in inventory, loans, bribes, equipment, premises, or a bankroll.
2. **Gross receipts:** actual collections or sales before costs.
3. **Direct costs:** product purchase, labour, transport, rent, equipment, spoilage, losses, or payments to suppliers.
4. **Protection and political costs:** territorial tribute, corrupt payments, union fees, legal fees, and intermediaries, each recorded separately.
5. **Loss risk:** theft, arrest, seizure, bad debt, violence, and shutdowns. Expected losses are not always smooth monthly expenses; a single event may wipe out a year’s profit.
6. **Net operating take:** receipts minus direct and operating costs, before distributions and any retained liabilities.
7. **Distribution waterfall:** shares to operators, local authority, investors, lenders, and the parent organisation according to established canon and agreements.
8. **Cash actually available:** what remains after due payments and the working capital required to continue.
Any playbook with numbers must balance. A worked example must show the gross score, all deductions, who receives each payment, and the net amount remaining. If the example does not add up, it cannot be used as a rule. A racket’s success should also affect rivals and the city: new earnings attract competitors, violence attracts scrutiny, and expansion can trigger rent-seeking or retaliation.
### B10. Economic change over time
The calendar does not freeze prices. Maintain a yearly economic snapshot containing general inflation, unemployment and hiring conditions, interest-rate environment, fuel and transport costs, property trends, major industrial closures or expansions, and local crime/policing pressure. The engine may use national historical trends as anchors, but should not mechanically apply the same inflation rate to every item. Rents, wages, fuel, used cars, construction, and illicit goods can move differently.
When the year changes:
- Update routine prices and wage offers in the ledger, using dated historical benchmarks where available and clearly marked local estimates otherwise.
- Keep existing contracts at their agreed terms unless renegotiated or legally variable.
- Preserve the historical purchase price of an asset, but update its estimated current market value and carrying costs separately.
- Reconcile cash balances; inflation does not automatically create or destroy nominal dollars in a ledger.
- Let recessions, interest rates, union disputes, port volume, construction cycles, policy changes, and local wars produce unequal effects across districts and industries.
- Record why an important price changed. Do not use unexplained arbitrary swings merely to punish or reward the player.

### Fixes to garbled figures (design §14)

The source's dollar signs had swallowed text between them (an inline-math side effect), so figures were lost. Each fix below is marked "Assumption:" in the bible text.

27. **Codex 8.1, sports book.** The garbled "Bettors risk $110 to win 50k on each side ... pays 5,000 Net Operating Take (5% of total $100k handle)". Recovered as: 11-to-10 action, $50,000 to be won on each side of a $100,000 handle, losers pay $55,000, winners are paid $50,000, the book keeps $5,000, which is 5 percent of the handle. Noted that the $5,000 is the margin before the book's own costs, not after the finger and the pad, which the codex's own definition of net operating take would subtract.
28. **Codex 8.1, numbers math.** Lost: the number range, true odds, street payout and cut-number example. Reconstructed: 000 to 999, true odds 999 to 1, street payout 600 to 1 (consistent with the stated 55 to 60 percent consumed by hits), cut numbers at 500 to 1. The percentage split (runners 20 to 25, controller 5 to 10, hits 55 to 60, bank 10 to 15) was checked and noted to add up for the middle of the ranges.
29. **Codex 8.1, loanshark rates.** Lost sizes: wholesale lent to a made man up to $100,000; commercial $20,000 to $250,000+ (from the playbook's $20,000 to $100,000, with larger sums for developers); street points $1,000 to $10,000; six-for-five $100 to $2,000. Tier 2: $25,000 to $250,000 with loans of $5,000 to $50,000 (the $25,000 floor and loan sizes survive; the upper bound is set at the Tier 3 floor). The Tier 2 loan size ($5,000 to $50,000) is lower than the playbook's commercial loan range ($20,000 to $100,000); the playbook range is kept for Tier 2 and 3 together, since Tier 3 includes the upper end.
